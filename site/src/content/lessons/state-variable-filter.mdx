<Part id="why" title="A bright sound has to be made dark, and one memory is too gentle to do it.">

Play one note on almost any synthesiser, with nothing after the oscillator, and what comes out is a buzz. The loop this page opens on is that sound: a saw wave at <Src path="site/labs/bench.cpp" at="const float saw_step = 110.0f / lab::kSampleRate;">110 Hz</Src>, a low A.

A saw wave is not one frequency. It holds its note, then a tone at twice the note, at three times, at four times, and so on to the top of hearing. Those are its harmonics, and each is a little weaker than the one before. A spectrum shows them. It sorts a sound by frequency, low at the left and high at the right, and draws how much there is of each.

  set="buzz"
  view="spectrum"
  min={-110}
  max={-10}
  top={12000}
  caption="The saw as a spectrum. Every spike is a harmonic. The first, at the left, is the note. They get closer together to the right only because the axis counts in octaves, and they get weaker as they go."
/>

The first spike is the note you played. All the others are its colour, and the highest of them are the buzz. <Hear set="buzz" label="Hear the saw as it is" />

So the sound has a note that is wanted and an edge that is not. A fader is no help, because a fader turns the note and the edge down together. The question this lesson answers is how to take the top off a sound and leave the bottom of it alone, and then how to move the place where that happens while the sound plays.

### The filter you already have is too gentle

The tool is called a low-pass filter: it lets the low frequencies pass and holds the high ones back. The frequency where the holding back sets in is its cutoff. <Lesson id="one-pole" cap /> built the smallest one there is, a filter with a single memory, which is called a one-pole. That lesson says what a pole is, and measures what a cutoff marks: the frequency at which half the power still passes. Here it is on the saw, with its cutoff two octaves above the note.

<Still
  taps="in onePole"
  set="onepole"
  view="spectrum"
  min={-110}
  max={-10}
  top={12000}
  caption="The saw going in and coming out of the one-pole. The output's spikes are lower towards the right, and every one of them is still there."
/>

It has dulled the sound. It has not closed it. <Claim id="one-pole-short">With its cutoff at 440 Hz the one-pole takes 16.2 dB off what the saw holds between 2 and 5 kHz</Claim>, and in the picture the output's spikes still stand well clear of the bottom all the way to the right. <Hear set="onepole" label="Hear it through one pole" />

The reason is the one-pole's slope, which stage 3 of that lesson explains. Past its cutoff it lets through half as much for every doubling of frequency, which is 6 dB an octave, and it has no other setting. Measured on the kit's own, <Claim id="one-pole-slope">with the cutoff at 250 Hz it falls 5.8 dB between 1 and 2 kHz</Claim>, two octaves over the cutoff, where the slope is not yet quite the whole 6. The saw's harmonics already fall away at about that rate by themselves, so the filter does little more than tilt a sound that was tilted to begin with.

A one-pole lacks a second thing, and it is the one musicians miss more. It cannot favour a frequency. One memory can only lean towards its input, so its curve runs level and then downhill and has no way to rise. A filter that could lift the frequencies right at its cutoff would mark the place where it closes, and that mark is most of what people mean when they say a filter has a sound.

### Two memories that feed each other

Hang a weight on a spring and pull it down. Two quantities describe it at any instant: where the weight is, and how fast it is moving. Each drives the other. The speed changes the position, and the position, through the spring, changes the speed. One quantity alone can only settle. Two that feed each other can swing.

Before computers were digital, engineers worked out such motions on analogue computers. The working part of those machines was the integrator, a circuit that keeps a running total of what it is fed. Speed is the running total of acceleration, and position is the running total of speed, so two integrators in a row, with their outputs fed back to the start, are a spring and a weight made of electricity. The quantities such a loop keeps were called its state variables.

In the late sixties the same loop was turned into a filter, and it kept the name: the state-variable filter. Put a sound in where the force on the weight would go. What comes out of the second integrator, the position, is a low-pass. It is twice as steep as a one-pole, because the sound has been through two running totals and not one. Here it is on the saw at the same cutoff.

### And it can lift what it does not cut

The spring has something a single memory has not: a brake. With a strong brake the weight creeps back to rest. With a weak one it swings past rest and back, many times, always at the same rate. In the filter that rate is the cutoff, and the brake is a control. It is called Resonance, and a higher setting is a weaker brake.

<Part id="picture" title="Three curves that meet at the cutoff.">

One picture holds what this filter does, and it has three lines because the loop has three outputs. Read each line like this. Pick a frequency on the bottom edge, go straight up to the line, then across to the left edge. That is how many decibels a steady tone at that frequency comes out above or below the level it went in at. A line at 0 dB is a wire.

The lab measures the lines. It sends a single sample through the kit's filter, keeps what rings out of each output, and works out how much of each frequency there is in it.

The Low-pass line is level at the left and falls away at the right: low tones pass and high ones are held back. The High-pass line is its mirror image. The Band-pass line passes the frequencies near the cutoff and holds back both ends.

Here is a sentence to check against the picture. With Resonance where it starts, the Low-pass and High-pass lines cross on the dashed line, and <Claim id="minus-3">they cross at −3.01 dB, the level at which half the power gets through, while the Band-pass line passes that frequency at 0.00 dB</Claim>. Now raise Resonance to 4 and look again: the crossing has gone up to the height part 1 measured, and the Band-pass line still touches 0 dB.

Two controls set everything in the picture.

- **Cutoff** is where. It slides all three lines along the frequency axis, and the place where they meet goes with it.
- **Resonance** is how sharply. It lifts the Low-pass and High-pass lines around the cutoff and leaves them as they were far from it, and it narrows the Band-pass line around the cutoff.

The slopes are fixed, and they are what the second integrator bought. <Claim id="high-slope">Well below the cutoff the high-pass rises 12.0 dB an octave, measured from 125 to 250 Hz with the cutoff at 1 kHz</Claim>. <Claim id="low-slope">With the cutoff at 250 Hz the low-pass falls 12.1 dB between 1 and 2 kHz</Claim>. <Claim id="band-slope">The band-pass loses 6.04 dB over the octave from 1 to 2 kHz above that cutoff, and 6.0 dB over the octave from 31.25 to 62.5 Hz below it</Claim>. The band-pass has half the slope because the sound it carries has been through one integrator and not two.

</Part>

The same filter turned over does the opposite jobs. A high-pass set low takes away what lies under an instrument's lowest note, and a band-pass keeps only the middle of a sound, as a small loudspeaker does. Type chooses between them, and part 5 shows that the choice costs nothing: all of them are already there.

<Part id="build" title="Start with one running total. Add a second. Then let go of the brake.">

This part puts the filter together a piece at a time. Each step adds one piece, and after each the lab sends a single sample through what has been built so far and measures its curve again. "Hear it so far" plays the loop through it.

The listings are the lab's own source. This code was written for the lesson, to be read: it is not the engine's. It runs beside the engine's filter all the same, and the last step says how far apart the two land.

<Build>
  <Step title="One integrator in a loop" controls="cutoff">
    An integrator is a running total. On each sample it adds a small share of what it is fed to what it already holds. Feed it a steady value and the total climbs without end, which is no use by itself. So the first piece feeds the integrator the input less its own output. Now the total climbs only while it is behind the input, and stops when it has caught up.

    A total that chases its input follows slow changes and falls behind fast ones. That is a low-pass, and it is the one-pole of part 1 made from an integrator. What the total has not caught up with, the input less the output, is the fast part of the sound: a high-pass. The build keeps both, and plays the low one.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/state-variable-filter.cpp"
      from="// One integrator in a loop. An integrator is a running total"
      to="s1 = flush_denormal(2.0f * low - s1); }"
      name="one"
    />

    The share is `g`. A small `g` is a slow total and a low cutoff. It is made from Cutoff by a tangent, and part 5 says why a tangent. The cutoff is first kept between <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" at="const float f = clamp(hz, 5.0f, sample_rate * 0.49f);">5 Hz and 0.49 of the sample rate</Src>, as the kit keeps it.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/state-variable-filter.cpp"
      from="// The cutoff as an integrator's gain"
      to="g = kit::tan_prewarp(kit::kPi * kit::clamp(hz, 5.0f, lab::kSampleRate * 0.49f) / lab::kSampleRate); }"
      name="tune"
    />

    <Claim id="build-one">With the cutoff at 250 Hz, a tone at 2 kHz comes out of this step 5.9 dB under a tone at 1 kHz</Claim>. One integrator gives 6 dB an octave, and no more.

  <Step title="A second integrator" controls="cutoff">
    The second piece is another running total, fed by the first. And the loop is closed round both: the input now has both totals taken away from it before it reaches the first integrator.

    There are three sounds in the loop now. What is left of the input is `high`. The first total is `band`. The second total is `low`. Each of the three needs the other two on the same sample, so the first line of `two` is the three of them solved for `band`, and the next two lines follow from it.

    Two totals that feed each other can swing, as the weight on the spring did, and something has to stop them. That is `k`, the share of `band` that is taken away from the input. It is the brake. For now it is fixed at <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" at="float k = 1.4142f;">1.4142</Src>, where the kit's filter starts it.

    <Listing

      name="two"
    />

<Part id="engine" title="The kit's filter and the device around it, opened up.">

Everything from here on is the engine's own code. The page compiles the engine's C++ and runs the loop through it, and each figure is read out of the running code at the point the text is talking about. Under each stage is a mark that says where it comes from.

Two things are being read. The first is the kit's state-variable filter, a class many of the engine's devices use. The lab ticks it once for each sample and reads its three outputs. The second is the Auto Filter, the device on the plate in part 9. It does not use the kit's class. It carries a filter stage of its own with the same arithmetic, runs it at twice the sample rate, and moves its cutoff with an envelope and an oscillator. The cutoff the kit's filter stands at on each sample is the device's, so both follow the same controls.

The first picture is the loop itself.

<StageBlock tap="band" n={2} title="The first integrator, and how fast it runs">
  An integrator passes slow sounds at a large size and fast ones at a small size, and the size halves for every doubling of frequency. At exactly one frequency its gain is 1, and that frequency is the filter's cutoff. So the cutoff is nothing but how fast the integrators run.

  A program cannot keep a true running total of a sound. It has samples, and it can only add them up. The kit uses the trapezoid rule: from one sample to the next the total grows by <M t="2g" /> times the mean of this input and the last. It keeps one memory to do it, which this page writes <M t="s_1" /> and the kit's code calls `ic1`.

  <Controls ids="cutoff" />

    answers each sudden step of the wave with one short pulse. Try Cutoff lower, and notice each
    pulse last longer.
  </Wave>

  />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    The first line is the output: what the memory holds, plus <M t="g" /> times what came in on this sample. The second line makes the memory ready for the next sample. Twice the output less the old memory is the output plus <M t="g" /> times this input once more, so each input is counted twice, <M t="g" /> times it now and <M t="g" /> times it on the next sample. From one output to the next the total has grown by <M t="g" /> times the sum of two inputs, which is <M t="2g" /> times their mean, and that mean is why the rule is named after a trapezoid.

    For slow sounds <M t="g" /> would be <M t={String.raw`\pi f_c / f_s`} />, with <M t="f_c" /> the frequency the cutoff stands at and <M t="f_s" /> the sample rate. <StageBlock tap="ring" n={4} title="Resonance: how hard the brake is">

<StageBlock tap="svfLevel" n={11} title="Why this form: its memories mean the same at any cutoff">
  A filter that only ever stood still could be written several ways. This one is written to be swept, and what makes it good at that is what its two memories hold. They are the integrators' totals, which are near enough the band and low outputs themselves. Those mean the same thing at any cutoff. Change <M t="g" /> between two samples and both memories are still right.

  The other common way to write the same curve is called the direct form. The kit keeps one for equalisers that stay put, and <Lesson id="equalising" /> is about it. Its two memories are mixtures of past inputs and outputs, weighted by the filter's numbers, and they mean something only beside the numbers that made them. The lab runs the kit's direct form as a low-pass beside the kit's state-variable filter, on the same sound and the same moving cutoff.

Each card sets the page to its settings and plays them. None of them is a rule. They are places to start from, and the controls stay yours to move once a card is sounding.

    A sound that holds still is given a slow movement of its own.   </Way>
  <Way title="Let the playing open it" tag="Envelope amount above zero" set="wah">
    <Still

    A band-pass with a high Resonance picks one region out of a sound, and the envelope moves that region with the player's hand. <Claim id="way-wah">On the picked guitar the loudest note lifts the band 0.7 octaves</Claim>. Mix below 1 keeps some of the plain sound under it. Shorten Envelope release for a quicker answer, and see part 7 for what happens when it is too short.

<Part id="wrong" title="Six ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Resonance is gain" set="howl">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="in out"
      set="howl"
      view="spectrum"
      min={-110}
      max={0}
      top={12000}
      caption="The saw going in and coming out. The output's fourth spike, where the cutoff is, stands far above the input's and above every other spike in the picture."
    />

    The number on Resonance is how many times larger a tone on the cutoff comes out. <Claim id="gain-q">At a Q of 25 a tone on the cutoff comes out 27.96 dB up</Claim>, through the kit's filter and through the device alike. On a sound with a harmonic on the cutoff the whole level rises: <Claim id="fault-howl">with the cutoff on the saw's harmonic at 440 Hz and Resonance at 10, the output is 7.2 dB louder than the saw that went in</Claim>. The kit's filter has no guard against this. Nor has this page's player much room for it: <Src path="site/src/lab/player.ts" at="export const volumeGain = (volume: number) => 0.5 * volume * volume">with its volume all the way up it plays at half gain</Src>, so a sound past twice full scale clips in your ear and not in the pictures. No card on this page comes out over full scale, and the controls can go well past twice that: <Claim id="loud-range">with Resonance at 25 and Cutoff on the saw's own note, 110 Hz, the device's loudest sample is 4.0 times full scale</Claim>.

    Turn Resonance down, or turn the sound down before the filter. The Auto Filter has a third answer, which is Drive. It bends both memories so that the loop cannot store more than a fixed amount, however long it is pushed. <Claim id="drive-caps">A full-scale tone on the cutoff at a Q of 25 comes out at 0.24 of full scale with 6 dB of Drive, where without Drive it comes out at 25 times full scale</Claim>. <Claim id="fault-tamed">The saw, at this card's settings with 6 dB of Drive added, comes out 0.3 dB quieter than it went in</Claim>. <Hear set="tamed" label="Hear it with Drive" />

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="An envelope that chatters" set="chatter">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="sweep"
      set="chatter"
      from={0.7}
      span={0.4}
      caption="Four tenths of a second of where the cutoff stands, on a bass note. It does not rise once and fall once. It shakes up and down with every cycle of the note."
    />

    A bass note is a slow wave. With Envelope attack and Envelope release both short, the envelope follows the wave itself, up and down with every cycle, and not the loudness of the note. The cutoff then shakes at the speed of the note. Listen for a roughness on each note that is not in the bass going in.

    <Claim id="fault-chatter">On the bass, with Envelope release at 10 ms, the cutoff travels 83 octaves up and down over the four seconds. With Envelope release at 200 ms and nothing else changed, it travels 19 octaves</Claim>. Lengthen Envelope release until the cutoff moves once for each note.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A cutoff that jumps" set="jump">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="picture"
      set="jump"
      view="spectrogram"
      top={8000}
      caption="The output over the four seconds: time across, frequency up. The top edge of the sound is a row of flat steps with upright sides, and each side is a jump."
    />

    The loop itself does not misbehave when its cutoff jumps, as stage 11 measured. But the sound on either side of a jump is two different sounds, and the join between them can be heard. The device slides each of its own controls over <Src path="cpp/kit/device.h" at="static constexpr float kSmoothingSeconds = 0.005f;">5 ms</Src>, so that a hand on Cutoff moves it in a short ramp and not in a jump. Its oscillator is not smoothed: a square is meant to snap. If the snap is not wanted, choose Triangle or Sine, or lower Resonance so that there is less of an edge to move.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The top octave bends">
    The tangent of stage 2 puts the cutoff in the right place and nothing else. Every other frequency is still squeezed towards half the sample rate, so a low-pass falls faster than it should as it gets there. <Claim id="warp-slope">With the cutoff at 1 kHz the kit's low-pass falls 13.3 dB between 4 and 8 kHz</Claim>, more than a decibel over its slope lower down.

    For a filter inside a detector that does not matter. For a filter that is the sound, do as the device does and run it at twice the rate, where the squeeze is out of hearing. Stage 12 measures the difference.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="It is late">
    The device's output is behind its input by the delay stage 12 measured, because of the converters round its loop. Inside the device that is allowed for: Mix blends the filtered sound with an input that has been held back by the same amount. Outside it, a copy of the sound that goes round the device arrives early. The device reports its delay, so a host that knows to ask can hold the other path back.

    The stages read from the kit's filter in part 5 are not delayed at all. Drawn against them, the device's output sits a little to the right.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A ring that never quite ends">
    A ring dies away by a fixed ratio for ever, into numbers so small that some processors handle them very slowly. Nothing can be heard of this, and a device left ringing into silence would still cost more and more to run. Both memories of the kit's filter and of the device's stage are passed through a guard that calls <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h" at="constexpr float kDenormalThreshold = 1.0e-15f;">anything under a very small threshold</Src> nothing at all, so a filter left alone reaches exact silence.

  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A low-pass filter only takes sound away.">
    With Resonance above its starting value it also adds. The prediction of part 1 measures a tone on the cutoff coming out ten times as large, and the first fault of part 7 measures a whole sound coming out louder than it went in.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The cutoff is the frequency where the sound stops.">
    Nothing stops at the cutoff. Part 2 measures the low-pass there at half power, and <Claim id="octave-on">with the cutoff at 1 kHz the kit's low-pass is 12.4 dB down an octave further on, at 2 kHz</Claim>. The cutoff is where the curve turns the corner, and Slope and Resonance say how sharp the corner is.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Low-pass, band-pass and high-pass are three different filters.">
    In this filter they are three places on one loop, worked out together on every sample whether or not they are used. Stage 1 of part 5 adds them up again and gets the input back. Type only chooses which of them to listen to.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Two gentle filters in a row make the same thing.">
    Two one-poles one after the other do fall 12 dB an octave in the end, and that is all they share with the loop. <Claim id="two-poles">Two of the kit's one-poles in a row, each with its cutoff at 1 kHz, pass that frequency at −6.0 dB</Claim>, twice the loss of the loop at its starting Resonance, and Resonance does not change it, because neither of them has a brake to let go of. The corner of their curve is soft, and it stays soft.

    <Still
      taps="twoPoleResponse:alt lowResponse:out"
      values={{ resonance: 4 }}
      view="spectrum"
      min={-48}
      max={24}
      caption="Two one-poles in a row and the loop's low-pass, on one cutoff, with Resonance at 4. The loop rises to a point at the cutoff. The two one-poles sag there, and Resonance does not reach them."
    />

  </Myth>
  <Myth says="24 dB is twice the filter, so it has twice the peak.">
    The device gives each of its two stages the square root of the Resonance asked for, so that their peaks multiply back to the one asked for. Stage 13 measures the same gain at the cutoff on either Slope. What doubles is the steepness past the corner.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Resonance adds a tone of its own to the sound.">
    This filter adds nothing that was not put in. It rings only when something strikes it, and the ring dies away: stage 5 measures that no setting tried makes it answer a single sample with anything larger than that sample. What is heard as the filter's tone is the part of the sound that lies near the cutoff, turned up.
  </Myth>
</Myths>
