<Part id="why" title="A held chord stands still. Something has to move it.">

A pad holds one chord for two seconds and then another for two more. While a chord is held, none of its notes moves. A spectrogram draws time across and frequency up, and marks each frequency more strongly the more of it there is. On a spectrogram each of these chords is a stack of level lines.

  taps="pictureIn"
  view="spectrogram"
  set="held"
  top={6000}
  min={-79}
  max={-19}
  caption="Four seconds of the pad: two held chords, two seconds each. Each line is one partial of a chord. Notice that from either end of the picture to the change of chord in the middle, every line runs level."
/>

Listen to it first. <Hear set="held" label="Hear the pad as played" />

A sound like that is easy to make. Let it play round a few times, and listen for how soon your attention leaves it. How do you make a held sound move, without touching its notes and without riding its level?

### A hand on a reel of tape

The first answer was found with tape. Two machines play the same recording into one mixer. Someone leans on the rim of one reel, and that machine falls a little behind the other. The two copies no longer line up. Some frequencies now arrive upside down against their twins and cancel, and as the hand lets go, the cancelled places sweep through the sound. That is flanging, and <Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" /> builds it out of a delay.

Two tape machines are a lot to carry. A phaser gets a sweep of the same family from something far smaller: a few tiny filters that change when each frequency arrives, and nothing else about it. It has no tape in it and no delay line. In the engine each of those filters is one line of arithmetic.

### The obvious answer fails

Start with the filters by themselves. Put the pad through a chain of four of them and call what comes out the copy. Every frequency in the copy has been held back, the low ones for longer than the high ones, and an oscillator is moving those amounts up and down all the time. If moving filters were enough, the copy would be the effect.

<Still
  taps="picture"
  view="spectrogram"
  set="copy"
  top={6000}
  min={-79}
  max={-19}
  caption="The copy alone, while its filters sweep. Notice that it is the picture above: the same lines at the same strength, with no trace of the sweep."
/>

It is not. Measured in slices a sixth of a second long, <Claim id="why-copy">over the whole loop the copy's level is less than 0.001 dB from the pad's, and in none of the four octaves from 200 Hz to 3,200 Hz is it more than 0.2 dB away at any moment</Claim>. The filters changed when things arrive. They did not change how much of anything there is. The usual account of hearing is that the ear weighs how much of each frequency there is, and is far less sensitive to when each one arrives. Whether you can tell the two apart at all is for your ears to say. <Hear set="copy" label="Hear the copy alone" />

  choices={['The pad, as it was', 'The pad, a little louder', 'The pad with some frequencies missing']}

The pad with some frequencies missing. Think of one partial of the chord. In the copy it arrives late. If it is late by exactly half of its own cycle, the copy of that partial is the original upside down, and half of one plus half of the other is nothing. If it is late by a whole cycle, the two are in step again and add up to what was there before.

With the filters held still, the pad comes out <Claim id="why-stopped">4.16 dB quieter</Claim> as a whole, and the loss is not spread evenly. It sits in two narrow places.

### Then move the gaps

Two notches that stand still are a tone colour, like one fixed setting of an equaliser. Now let the oscillator move the filters. The lateness of every frequency changes, the two places where the copy is half a cycle late slide along with it, and the notches travel through the chord.

<Still
  taps="picture"
  view="spectrogram"
  set="swept"
  top={6000}
  min={-79}
  max={-19}
  caption="The pad and its copy mixed, while the filters sweep. Notice the two gaps that cross the lines of both chords, rising and falling once in the loop, and that each line comes back once a gap has passed."
/>

The filters have added no note and nothing has been turned up or down by hand, and yet parts of the sound now come and go. Measured in the same slices, the octave of the pad from 800 Hz to 1,600 Hz comes out <Claim id="why-swept">between 0.6 dB and 11.2 dB quieter</Claim> than it went in, depending on the moment. <Hear set="swept" label="Hear it swept" />

That is a phaser: a copy that is late by a different amount at every frequency, a sum, and something slow to move it. The copy alone is not the effect and neither is the sum alone.

</Part>

<Part id="picture" title="One tone, its late copy, and what the two add up to.">

Take the simplest sound there is, one steady tone. Its copy comes out of the filters at the same frequency and the same height, a little late. Mix the two, half of each, and what you get depends on one thing only: how late.

Lateness is counted here as an angle. One whole cycle of the tone is a whole turn, 360°, so a copy that is half a cycle late is half a turn behind, 180°.

Check this against the picture. Leave both controls where the page starts them, with Tone at <Src path="site/labs/phasing.cpp" at='lab::Control tone{"tone", "Tone"'>200 Hz</Src>. <Claim id="tone-start">The copy is 112° behind the tone, a little under a third of a cycle, and the mixed line stands 0.56 times as tall as the tone</Claim>.

Those two numbers belong together. Write <M t={String.raw`\theta`} /> for where the tone is in its cycle and <M t={String.raw`\psi`} /> for how far behind the copy is. Then half the tone plus half the copy is

<M
  block
  t={String.raw`\tfrac12 \sig{in}{\sin\theta} + \tfrac12 \sig{alt}{\sin(\theta - \psi)} = \sig{out}{\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\psi}{2}\right) \sin\!\left(\theta - \tfrac{\psi}{2}\right)}`}
/>

Read it against the picture. The left side is the two lines that were added. The right side is the line they make: one wave of the same frequency, half as late as the copy, and <M t={String.raw`\cos(\psi/2)`} /> times as tall. When the copy is not late at all, the cosine of nothing is 1 and the mix is the tone. At 112° it is the cosine of 56°, which is 0.56, the height that was measured. At 180° it is the cosine of 90°, which is 0.

So raise Tone until the mixed line lies flat, a little above 330 Hz. <Claim id="tone-cancel">At the frequency the lab works out for that, 331.6 Hz, the copy is 180° behind and the mixed line is less than 0.001 times as tall as the tone</Claim>. Keep going to 800 Hz, where Centre is. <Claim id="tone-whole">There the copy is 360° behind, which puts it back on top of the tone, and the mixed line is as tall as the tone was</Claim>.

Now move Centre instead and leave Tone alone. The same thing happens from the other side: the lateness of this one tone changes, and its mix grows and shrinks. That is all a phaser's oscillator does: it moves this one setting up and down.

A played sound is many tones at once. Each has its own lateness, so at any moment some are cancelled, some are whole and most are in between. What this picture leaves out is why four small filters should hold each frequency back by a different amount, and that is where part 5 begins.

</Part>

    The piece everything else is made of is a filter that lets every frequency through at full strength and holds each one back by a different amount. It is called an all-pass, after the first of those two things.

<StageBlock tap="phase" n={1} title="One all-pass stage: every level kept, every frequency made late">
  The chain is one small filter used many times, and the engine's is the first piece of the build, line for line. It holds three numbers: `a`, the input of one sample ago and the output of one sample ago.

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[n]} = \at{stage-step}{a}\,\sig{in}{x[n]} + \sig{in}{x[n-1]} - \at{stage-step}{a}\,\sig{out}{y[n-1]}`}
  />

  Read it term by term. <M t="x[n]" /> is the sample going in now, <M t="x[n-1]" /> the one before it, and <M t="y[n-1]" /> what the filter put out last time. The same number <M t="a" /> weighs the new input and, with its sign turned, the old output. The old input goes through whole.

  That matched pair of weights is what keeps every level where it was. <Lesson id="one-pole" cap /> has a filter that remembers its own last output, and <Lesson id="equalising" /> one that remembers what went in as well. Here the two memories are set against each other so exactly that whatever one lifts at a frequency the other takes away. Only the lateness is left. In the picture of stage 2 of <Lesson id="equalising" /> this filter has one pole and one zero, at mirrored places inside the circle and outside it, and the two distances keep one ratio all the way round.

  This is the smallest all-pass there is, and other lessons meet it in other clothes. <Lesson id="spring-reverb" cap /> keeps the same filter with one memory in place of two. Give it a delay of hundreds of samples where this one has a single sample, and it is the all-pass of <Lesson id="plate-reverb" />, which answers one sample with a train of echoes.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/devices/phaser/phaser_stage.h"
    from="struct PhaserStage {"
    to="return y; } };"
    name="stage"
    at={{ step: 'const float y = flush_denormal(a * x + x1 - a * y1);' }}
  />

  The one number <M t="a" /> comes from the corner frequency, in two steps:

  <M block t={String.raw`t = \tan\!\left(\frac{\pi f_c}{f_s}\right)`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`a = \at{coefficient-quotient}{\frac{t - 1}{t + 1}}`} />

  <M t="f_c" /> is the corner and <M t="f_s" /> the sample rate. A low corner makes <M t="t" /> small and <M t="a" /> close to −1. A corner at a quarter of the sample rate makes <M t="t" /> equal to 1 and <M t="a" /> equal to 0, and the first equation is then a plain delay of one sample. So <M t="a" /> is below zero for every corner under a quarter of the sample rate, and that is all of Centre's range. <Claim id="stage-sign">One sample of 1 put into the one stage of the build's first step comes out first as <M t="a" /> itself: −0.90 with Centre at 800 Hz, −0.987 at 100 Hz and −0.49 at 5 kHz</Claim>. The sign matters, and stage 2 says what for.

  The engine does not call the tangent function. It works <M t="t" /> out as one short polynomial over another, `n` over `d`, and folds the division into the last line, because every stage on both sides is given a new <M t="a" /> <Src path="cpp/devices/phaser/phaser.h" at="static constexpr int kControlInterval = 4;">once every four samples</Src>. It also keeps the corner <Src path="cpp/devices/phaser/phaser_stage.h" at="const float x = kit::kPi * kit::clamp(hz, 10.0f, sample_rate * 0.45f) / sample_rate;">between 10 Hz and 0.45 of the sample rate</Src>.

  The line as a formula:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{\varphi(f)} = -2\arctan\!\left(\frac{\tan(\pi f / f_s)}{\tan(\pi f_c / f_s)}\right)`}

  Read it against the picture. <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} /> is the turn the stage gives a tone at the frequency <M t="f" />, and the minus sign says the tone comes out late. At 0 Hz the top tangent is 0 and so is the turn. At the corner the two tangents are equal, their ratio is 1, the arctangent of 1 is an eighth of a turn, and twice that is <Claim id="quarter-turn">a quarter of a turn, −90°</Claim>. <StageBlock tap="wet" n={2} title="A chain of stages: the turns add up">
  Filters in a row do two things to a tone. Their gains multiply, and one times one is still one. Their turns add. So <M t="N" /> stages on one corner turn a tone <M t="N" /> times as far as one stage does, and the lag of part 2 is

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{ctl}{\psi(f)} = -N\,\varphi(f)`} />

  which runs from nothing at the bottom of the spectrum to <M t="N" /> half turns at the top. The device offers <Src path="cpp/devices/phaser/device.json" at={'"choices": ["4", "6", "8", "10", "12"],'}>4, 6, 8, 10 or 12 stages</Src>.

  With four stages a tone at the corner has been turned four quarters: <Claim id="whole-turn">−360°, a whole turn, and what is left when the tone is taken away from its copy is more than 130 dB under the tone</Claim>.

  The chain keeps levels as each stage does. At every number of stages and every Spread, <Claim id="flat-level">no frequency comes out more than 0.001 dB from the level it went in at</Claim>. On the noise loop the copy's spectrum matches the input's <Claim id="same-spectrum">bin for bin within 0.2 dB, and as a whole within 0.001 dB</Claim>.

  What the chain changes is when each frequency arrives. A turn is a delay: a tone turned by some part of a cycle comes out that part of its own cycle late. Through four stages at 800 Hz, with the oscillator still, a 50 Hz tone comes out <Claim id="low-delay">1.59 ms late</Claim>. Counted that way <Claim id="high-delay">the delay grows less at every step up the spectrum, and at 10 kHz it is 0.19 ms</Claim>. So this chain holds the low frequencies back longest, though it turns them least.

  That direction goes with the sign of <M t="a" />. Below zero, as in every stage here, an all-pass of this form holds the lows longest. At zero it holds every frequency for one sample. Above zero it holds the highs longest, and <Lesson id="spring-reverb" /> puts a hundred stages of that sign in a row and measures the chirp they make. Compare <Listen tap="in">the input</Listen> with <Listen tap="wet">the all-passed copy</Listen>, and judge whether you can tell them apart.

  The stages need not share a corner. Spread tunes them apart. Stage <M t="i" /> of the chain sits at

  <M block t={String.raw`c_i = f_c \cdot 2^{\,\at{tune-lowest}{S}\,q_i}`} />

  where <M t="S" /> is Spread as a fraction and <M t="q_i" /> runs in even steps from −1 at the first stage to +1 at the last. At 100 % the chain covers an octave either side of the corner. The lag still runs from nothing to <M t="N" /> half turns, but less steeply through the middle, and the crossings move apart. It is a gentle control. At the device's own <Src path="cpp/devices/phaser/device.json" at={'"default": 30,'}>30 %</Src> the two crossings of four stages move to <Claim id="spread-30">329 Hz and 1,939 Hz</Claim>, and at 100 % to <Claim id="spread-100">303 Hz and 2,106 Hz</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="plain" n={3} title="Mixed with the input: the notches">
  The output is the input, crossed over to the copy by Mix, which the equation calls <M t="m" />:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{\text{out}} = \text{in} + (\sig{alt}{\text{wet}} - \text{in})\,\at{mix-left}{m}`}
  />

  At <M t="m = 0" /> that is the input alone and at 1 the copy alone. At one half it is half of each, the sum of part 2, where a tone and a copy <M t={String.raw`\psi`} /> behind it add up to <M t={String.raw`\cos(\psi/2)`} /> of the tone. That is nothing where <M t={String.raw`\psi`} /> is a half turn, and again at three half turns, at five, and at every odd number. So the notches are where

  <M block t={String.raw`N\,\varphi(f_k) = -(2k - 1)\cdot 180^\circ`} />

  and solving that for the frequency gives

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`f_k = \frac{f_s}{\pi}\arctan\!\left(\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi f_c}{f_s}\right)\tan\!\left(\frac{(2k-1)\,\pi}{2N}\right)\right)`}
  />

  <M t="k" /> counts the notches from the bottom. Each stage stops short of a half turn, so <M t="N" /> of them stop short of <M t="N" /> half turns, and the odd numbers below <M t="N" /> are half of <M t="N" />.   The second check is this stage's argument as a measurement. At every frequency where the chain was found to cross an odd number of half turns, the mix at one half reads <Claim id="null-depth">under −100 dB</Claim>.

  With four stages at 800 Hz the device puts its notches at <Claim id="first-notch">331.6 Hz</Claim> and <Claim id="second-notch">1,923 Hz, and at every number of stages the measured frequencies are less than 0.01 Hz from the formula</Claim>. A steady tone at the first one comes out <Claim id="notch-null">more than 130 dB down</Claim>, and a tone a hundredth higher in frequency <Claim id="notch-side">only 37 dB down</Claim>.

  Away from one half the two parts are not the same size and cannot cancel completely. What is left at a notch is <M t={String.raw`|1 - 2m|`} /> of the input: at a Mix of a quarter or of three quarters the notches are <Claim id="mix-quarter">6 dB deep</Claim>. That is why the cross-over here is a plain straight one. Only equal amounts cancel.

  **Why it is not a comb.** A delay turns phase too. Holding a sound back by a fixed time turns every frequency in proportion to the frequency, a straight line that never levels off, so it crosses an odd number of half turns at even steps for as long as there are frequencies. That is the comb a short delay makes, the subject of <Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" />. The all-pass chain's line bends, and it runs out of turns.

<StageBlock tap="response" n={6} title="Feedback: the chain hears itself">
  The last control sends the chain's output back to its own input, one sample late and scaled by Feedback, which the equation calls <M t="g" />:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\at{chain-loop}{x[n]} = \text{in}[n] + \at{limit-limit}{L}\bigl(g \cdot \sig{alt}{\text{wet}[n-1]}\bigr)`}
  />

  <M t="x" /> is what goes into the first stage. <M t="g" /> runs <Src path="cpp/devices/phaser/device.json" at={'"min": -95,'}>from −0.95 to 0.95</Src>. <M t="L" /> is a limiter that does nothing below full scale, and it can be left out for now.

  Follow a tone round the loop. Each lap it is turned by the chain and scaled by <M t="g" />. Where the chain turns it a whole number of turns, which is at the bottom of the spectrum and between each pair of notches, every lap lands in step with the last and they pile up:

  <M block t={String.raw`1 + g + g^2 + g^3 + \dots = \frac{1}{1 - g}`} />

  Where the chain turns it an odd number of half turns, which is at the notches, each lap lands upside down on the last:

  <M block t={String.raw`1 - g + g^2 - g^3 + \dots = \frac{1}{1 + g}`} />

  Those are the two cases of step 5 of the build, as sums. The output at half Mix is half the input plus half that copy, so the peaks of the mix rise and sharpen.

The first two numbers are the two sums above with <M t="g = 0.4" />. At the peak the mix is half of one plus five thirds, which is four thirds of the input. At a notch it is half of one less five sevenths, a seventh of the input. The notch fills because the two halves are no longer the same size there.

  The notch moves because of the one sample of delay in the loop. A delay adds a turn of its own that grows with frequency, so it shifts the upper notch more than the lower. The same sample is why there is a peak above the top notch, where chain and delay together come to a whole turn again. At 95 %, the end of the range, the peaks reach <Claim id="feedback-95">20.4 dB at 780 Hz and 20.3 dB at 6.754 kHz</Claim>.

  The sign matters. With <M t="g" /> below zero every lap is turned upside down once more, so the pile-up moves to the frequencies of the odd half turns.   Feedback stops short of 100 %, so each lap is smaller than the last and the loop always dies away.   </Way>
  <Way title="Step, not glide" tag="Shape on Random" set="steps">
    A sweep does not have to be smooth. <Claim id="way-steps">With Shape on Random and Rate at 2 Hz the corner holds eight different places in the four seconds</Claim>, and jumps from each to the next. Rate is in hertz and nothing ties the oscillator to a tempo: for a change on each beat, work out the beats in a second and set Rate to that. Square does the same between two places only.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A cloud of notches" tag="Twelve stages, Spread full" set="dense">
    More stages do not make a stronger version of the same thing. They make more notches, closer together. <Claim id="way-dense">With the oscillator held still, twelve stages at a Spread of 100 % put six notches between 96 Hz and 6.28 kHz</Claim>. After the card, listen for whether any single notch still stands out, and for the difference from the two plain notches of part 1.
  </Way>
</Ways>

</Part>

<Part id="wrong" title="Five ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="All copy, no effect" set="copy">
    It is natural to turn Mix all the way up for the strongest effect. That leaves the copy alone, and part 1 heard the copy alone. At a Mix of 1 the device has <Claim id="mix-one">no notches at all: the measured curve is level within 0.001 dB</Claim>. Bring Mix back to the middle. The notches are deepest at one half and grow shallow to either side of it.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A loop that rings" set="rings">
    Near the end of its range, Feedback makes each peak ring. Counting until the output has fallen 60 dB under what went in, and with the oscillator held still, <Claim id="fault-rings">one click rings on for 72 ms at a Feedback of 95 %, where at 0 % it is over in 2.3 ms. On the drums the loudest sample comes out 2.4 dB higher than the loudest that went in</Claim>. Every hit sets the peaks ringing at their own pitch, which changes as the sweep moves. Turn Feedback down until the hits sound like hits again, and leave room above the loudest of them. This page's player has room for twice full scale and no more: <Src path="site/src/lab/player.ts" at="export const volumeGain = (volume: number) => 0.5 * volume * volume">with its volume all the way up it plays at half gain</Src>, so a sound past that clips in your ear and not in the pictures. No card here comes out over full scale, and the controls can reach the player's limit: <Claim id="loud-range">with Feedback at 95 %, Mix at 1, Depth at 0 % and Centre at 110 Hz, the loudest sample of the mix loop comes out at 2.0 times full scale</Claim>.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Swept off the top" set="top">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="picture"
      view="spectrogram"
      set="top"
      top={24000}
      min={-72}
      max={-42}
      caption="Noise with Centre at its highest and Depth full. Notice the gaps climb towards the top of the picture, stop there, and stand still until the corner comes back down."
    />

    Centre at its highest with Depth full asks for a corner of <Claim id="corner-over">28 kHz at the top of the sweep, above half the sample rate</Claim>, where no frequency can be. The stages stop at 0.45 of the sample rate, as stage 1 says, while the corner's own reading goes on up. <Claim id="parked">The reading is over that limit, 21.6 kHz, for 0.72 s of every cycle</Claim>, and for that long the notches are parked at the top of the spectrum and nothing moves. <Claim id="parked-tone">A tone at 18.42 kHz, where the lower notch is parked, comes out more than 80 dB down all that time, but for its first and last hundredth of a second</Claim>. Lower Centre or Depth until the top of the sweep stays in the band you can hear.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A shallower sweep in mono">
    With Stereo away from 0 the two sides have their notches at different frequencies at any one moment. Add the sides together, as a phone's speaker does, and each side fills in the other's notch. <Claim id="fault-mono">With Stereo at 90°, a 331.5 Hz tone dips 43 dB on each side as the notch passes, and only 8.3 dB in the sum of the two sides</Claim>. On the pad at the same setting, <Claim id="fault-mono-pad">the octave from 800 Hz to 1,600 Hz comes out between 0.6 dB and 11.2 dB quieter on the left side, depending on the moment, and between 2.4 dB and 9.8 dB quieter in the sum</Claim>. If the effect has to survive being played in mono, check it there, and bring Stereo down.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Steps in the sweep">
    A filter whose number changes in jumps can put a click into the sound at every jump. The device is built to keep away from that. It tunes the stages <Src path="cpp/devices/phaser/phaser.h" at="static constexpr int kControlInterval = 4;">once every four samples</Src>, lags the oscillator by the 3 ms of stage 5, and fades a change of Stages. The price is that a Square or Random sweep has rounded corners. There is nothing to set: it is here so that the rounding is not mistaken for a fault.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Five things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A phaser shifts the phase, and that is what you hear.">
    The shift by itself changes no level, and on a held sound part 1 could not find it with a meter. What is heard is the cancelling that happens when the shifted copy meets the original. Stage 2 has the copy alone to listen to, and stage 3 the mix.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="An all-pass filter does nothing to a sound.">
    It keeps every level and changes when each frequency arrives. A held sound hides that. A click does not: counting until it has fallen 60 dB, <Claim id="myth-click">one sample put through the four stages the page starts with comes out spread over 2.5 ms, its tallest sample 0.66 times as tall as what went in, with all of its energy still there, within 0.001 dB</Claim>. Stage 2 measures the same lateness on a low tone.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="More stages make the notches deeper.">
    More stages make more notches, one for every two, and at a Mix of one half, with no Feedback, each of them is a whole cancellation. Stage 3 counts them, and its check stays under the same bound at every setting of Stages. How deep they are is set by Mix, and by Feedback, which fills them in.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The notches sit at Centre.">
    With four stages a tone at Centre goes through untouched: in the picture of part 2 its mix is as tall as the tone was. The notches of four stages lie more than an octave below Centre and more than an octave above it, as stage 3 measures. Whether anything lands on Centre depends on the count: <Claim id="myth-centre">with six stages the middle one of three notches stands at 800 Hz</Claim>, and with eight a tone there goes through untouched again.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Feedback makes the notches deeper.">
    It makes the peaks between them taller and narrower, and it makes the notches shallower. Stage 6 measures both at the device's own starting Feedback. Listen for the peaks, not the gaps, when Feedback is up.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
