<Part id="why" title="Two sounds can be equally tall and nowhere near equally loud.">

The picture at the top of this page holds two sounds. One is a drum loop. The other is a steady tone, which the page makes so that its tallest sample is exactly as tall as the tallest sample of the drums. A meter that shows the top of the wave cannot tell them apart. Measured over the whole loop, <Claim id="equal-peaks">the tallest samples of the two are less than 0.01 dB apart, and the average size of the tone is 15.7 dB over that of the drums</Claim>. The unit is the decibel, and part 2 says exactly what it is. Until then, read a bigger number as a bigger difference.

Turn your own volume down first, because the tone is loud. Then listen to <Listen tap="tone">the tone</Listen> and to <Listen tap="out">the drums</Listen>.

The reason is in the picture. A drum hit is a spike. It is at its height for an instant, and it has all but gone before the next one comes. The tone stands at its full height from the first sample to the last. An ear does not judge a sound by its single tallest moment. It answers to how much sound arrives over a stretch of time.

So here is the question this lesson answers. Which number, read off a sound, says how loud it is? Answering it needs the unit all such numbers are written in, so the lesson also says what a decibel is, once and plainly. Two later lessons lean on that: <Lesson id="compression" />, and <Lesson id="limiting" />.

### What the fix measures

A loudness meter is built to answer to what an ear answers to. It averages the sound over time. It counts the frequencies an ear hears well for more than the ones it hears badly. It leaves out the stretches where nothing is playing. Parts 4 and 5 build and open every one of those.

With Set by on Loudness, the page sets each loop's fader from that reading. <Claim id="by-loudness">Both loops then read −20.0 LUFS, and the tallest sample of the drums stands 6.8 dB over the tallest sample of the pad</Claim>. LUFS is the loudness meter's scale, and part 2 comes back to its name. Compare the two again: <Hear set="evenDrums" label="hear the drums, set by loudness" /> and <Hear set="evenPad" label="hear the pad, set by loudness" />.

Whether they now sound equally loud is for your ears to say. A meter's job is to get close enough that a small move of a fader finishes the work.

### The height still matters

None of this makes the peak a useless number. Sound in a computer has a top. A sample cannot be larger than full scale, and a wave that tries to go over it is cut off flat, which is distortion. The peak is the reading that says whether a sound fits under that top.

So a sound has two numbers that matter and they answer different questions. The loudness says how it will be heard. The peak says whether it fits. Matched for loudness, the drums of this page need 6.8 dB more room over their heads than the pad does. Mixing is, in good part, the work of keeping both numbers where they should be, and the two lessons named above are about the tools for it.

### Four readings and a fader

The page has one loop playing through one fader, and four readings of what comes out.

- **Peak** is the tallest sample of the last moments.
- **The peak between the samples** is the tallest point of the smooth wave that the samples stand for, which can lie between two of them.
- **Level** is the average size of the wave over a stretch of time.
- **Loudness** is a level taken after the sound has been weighted the way an ear weights it, with the silences left out.

The Fader is a gain, counted in decibels. Set by lets one of the readings set it. Beside the loop runs the tone of the opening picture, always exactly as tall as what comes out, so that every picture has two sounds with equal peaks to compare.

</Part>

### What a decibel is

A decibel is a way of writing how many times bigger one thing is than another. It is not an amount of sound. It is a ratio, and it is written so that multiplying becomes adding.

Start from the Fader. Raise it by 6 dB and the sound is twice as tall. Raise it by 6 dB again and it is twice as tall again, which is four times. Measured on the drums, <Claim id="plus-six">a Fader at 6 dB makes the output 2.00 times as tall, and at 12 dB 3.98 times</Claim>. Going down, each 20 dB is a tenth: <Claim id="minus-twenty">at −20 dB the output is 0.100 times as tall, and at −40 dB 0.0100 times</Claim>. Equal steps of the Fader are equal multiplications.

Here is the rule that the curve draws, with <M t="a" /> and <M t="b" /> any two heights and <M t="d" /> the decibels between them.

<M block t={String.raw`d = 20 \log_{10} \frac{a}{b}`} />

Read it from the inside out. <M t="a/b" /> is how many times as tall <M t="a" /> is as <M t="b" />. The logarithm counts tens: it is 1 when that ratio is ten, 2 when it is a hundred and 3 when it is a thousand. It is 0 when the two are equal, and it goes below zero when <M t="a" /> is the smaller. The 20 in front sets the size of the unit, so that ten times as tall is 20 dB. Twice as tall comes out as 6.02 dB, which everyone calls 6.

The Fader uses the same rule the other way round. Its setting <M t="F" /> is a number of decibels, and the sound is multiplied by the gain <M t="g" /> that those decibels stand for.

<M block t={String.raw`g = \at{kit-gain}{10^{F/20}}`} />

Every 20 dB in <M t="F" /> adds one to the power of ten, so it multiplies <M t="g" /> by ten. The engine's kit has both directions as one line each, and the curve above is drawn with the first of them. It multiplies by 0.05 where the formula divides by 20, which is the same thing.

### Decibels add where gains multiply

This is why sound is counted this way. Two gains one after the other multiply: half of a half is a quarter. In decibels the same two gains add: −6 and −6 is −12. A chain of ten devices is ten multiplications, and in decibels it is a sum that can be done in the head. When a compressor turns a sound down by 9 dB and a gain after it turns it up by 9 dB, the two cancel exactly, and nobody has to work out a product.

There is a second reason, and it is one to hear. Press Play, move the Fader from −24 dB to −18 dB, and then from −6 dB to 0 dB. Each move doubles the height of the sound. As heights, the second move is eight times as large as the first. Judge for yourself whether it sounds eight times as large, or whether the two steps seem much alike. A scale in decibels gives equal room to equal ratios, and that is the scale a meter is marked in.

### Nought decibels is not silence

A gain of 0 dB is a ratio of one. It multiplies by 1 and changes nothing. That is why a fader's resting place is marked 0, and why the Fader of this page starts there.

Silence is a gain of zero, and no number of decibels reaches it. Each 20 dB down is another tenth, and a tenth of something is never nothing. A real fader has to cheat at the bottom of its travel. The engine's channel fader goes down to <Src path="src/core/devices/native/Utility.ts" at="min: -60,">−60 dB</Src>, which is a thousandth, and at that last position it is shut: it multiplies by zero. The Fader of this page does the same. Try it at its lowest, and notice the table say so.

### A decibel always compares with something

By itself, "12 dB" says how far apart two things are. That is all a fader, a gain or a difference between two readings needs.

When a number in decibels describes one sound, something fixed has to stand in for the other. For samples in a computer that fixed thing is full scale, the largest number a sample can be. A height written as decibels against full scale is 0 for a sample at the very top and negative for everything else. Its short name is dBFS. On this page, every peak and every level is written that way, and that is why they are all below zero.

The loudness meter's readings are in LUFS, which stands for loudness units against full scale. It is the same decibel and the same reference. The different name is a warning that the sound was weighted before it was measured, so the reading is not the level and should not be compared with one. A difference between two loudness readings is written in LU, loudness units, and one LU is one dB: move a fader by 1 dB and the loudness moves by 1 LU.

### Twenty for heights, ten for powers

One more thing explains a number that would otherwise look like a mistake. Some formulas on this page have 10 in front of the logarithm where the one above has 20.

The power of a sound goes with the square of its height: twice as tall is four times the power. A level meter works with squares, as part 4 shows, so what it holds is a power. The logarithm of a square is twice the logarithm, and the 10 makes up for it.

<M block t={String.raw`10 \log_{10} \frac{a^2}{b^2} = 20 \log_{10} \frac{a}{b}`} />

Both sides are the same number of decibels. Use 20 for anything that multiplies a sample, such as a height or a gain. Use 10 for anything that is already a square, such as a power or an average of squares. Then a decibel means one thing whichever was measured.

</Part>

  <Step title="Mean square over a window" controls="window">
    One tall sample says nothing about its neighbours. The next piece asks how big the wave is on average. A plain average will not do, because a wave swings above and below zero by equal amounts and its plain average is nothing. So each sample is squared first, which makes it positive, the squares are averaged, and the square root of the average brings the answer back to the size of a sample. That is the root of the mean of the squares, called RMS for short.

    <M
      block
      t={String.raw`x_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \sig{in}{x[n-k]}^{2}}`}
    />

    <M t="N" /> is the number of samples in the window, and the sum runs back over the last <M t="N" /> of them from sample <M t="n" />. Window sets how long that is. The listing does not add the window up afresh at every sample. It keeps the sum, adds the newest square and takes away the one that has just left.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/level-and-loudness.cpp"
      from="void add(float left, float right) {"
      to="mean = sum > 0.0 ? sum / length : 0.0; }"
      mark="sum += square - squares[at];"
      name="window"
    />

  <Step title="Frequency weighting" controls="window fader">
    An ear is not equally sensitive at every frequency. It hears the middle of the range well and the very low notes poorly. This piece puts two filters in front of the squares: one that lifts the upper frequencies a little and one that turns the lowest down. The sound itself is not filtered. <StageBlock tap="level" n={5} title="Level: the squares, smoothed twice">
  This is the detector of the Ambient Compressor, the reading that device's threshold is compared with. It begins as step 2 of the build did, by squaring. For each sample it takes the mean of the two channels' squares.

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`s[n] = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\sig{out}{y_l[n]}^{2} + \sig{out}{y_r[n]}^{2}\bigr)`}
  />

  <M t="y_l" /> and <M t="y_r" /> are the left and the right sample, and <M t="s[n]" /> is their mean square. What follows is where it parts from the build. The device does not average over a window. It runs <M t="s" /> through a smoothing filter, which on each sample moves a fixed share of the way from where it was to the new value.

  <M block t={String.raw`u[n] = s[n] + \at{pole-step}{c\,\bigl(u[n-1] - s[n]\bigr)}`} />

  <M t="u[n]" /> is the smoothed value and <M t="c" /> the share of the old distance that is left, a number just under 1. A filter of this kind never forgets a sample outright. Each one fades, and the device sets the fading to <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h" at="static constexpr float kRmsSeconds = 0.03f;">30 ms</Src>. It is the one-pole filter of <Lesson id="one-pole" />, and the kit has it in one line.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/kit/filters.h"
    from="// One-pole lowpass, y += (1 - a)(x - y)"
    to="state = flush_denormal(x + (state - x) * a); return state; }"
    name="pole"
    at={{ step: 'state = flush_denormal(x + (state - x) * a);' }}

  The device uses two of them, one after the other. The second does to <M t="u" /> what the first did to <M t="s" />, and its output is called <M t="v" /> here. One filter alone lets the wave's own swing through as a ripple. With two, <Claim id="two-poles">the reading on a 100 Hz tone moves by less than 0.01 dB</Claim>.

  That fixed distance is worth knowing by heart. The mean of a sine's squares is half the square of its height, and half a power is 3.01 dB down. Music reads a good deal further under its peak than that, and how much further is the crest of part 3.

  Three things about this detector are particular to the device. It averages the two channels where a loudness meter adds them. It has a high-pass filter in front, which the compressor calls Ignore below and which this page sets as low as it goes.   The offset, <Src path="src/core/analysis/loudness.ts" at="export const LOUDNESS_OFFSET_LU = -0.691">−0.691</Src>, undoes the lift the shelf gives a tone at 1 kHz, so that the scale has a fixed point that is easy to remember. <Claim id="reference">A 1 kHz sine 0.1 tall on both channels reads −20.0 LUFS. Its tallest sample is at −20.00 dB against full scale, and its level is −23.01 dB</Claim>. The level is 3.01 dB under the peak, as stage 5 said of any sine. The loudness is back at the peak because this meter adds the two channels where the level averaged them, and two equal channels added are twice the power of their average, which is 3.01 dB more.

  The listing is what the library does when a tenth of a second is complete. It does not keep a list of blocks. It sorts each block into a bin by its loudness, <Src path="src/core/analysis/loudness.ts" at="const HISTOGRAM_LU_PER_BIN = 0.01">a hundredth of a unit</Src> wide, so that an hour of music costs it no more memory than a second.

  The reading is called the loudness so far because the gate is not fixed. Each new block moves the average, the average moves the gate, and a block that passed a moment ago can be left out now. <Part id="ways" title="Five things people do with these readings.">

Each of these is a habit of working. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the scale of part 2 and the lanes of part 5 make of it.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Compare at equal loudness" tag="Set by on Loudness" set="evenMix">
    Before deciding which of two versions of a sound is the better one, bring both to the same loudness. A louder version is easily taken for a better one, and a change that only made things louder can pass for an improvement. After the card, pick another loop from the row of sounds: Set by brings each of them to the same reading, so what is left to hear is the sound and not its size.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Read the gap between peak and level" tag="Set by on Level" set="gap">
    The distance from the level up to the tallest sample says how much of a sound is hits. <Claim id="gap">Set by Level, the drums come out with their tallest sample at −4.3 dB and their level at −23.0 dB</Claim>, which leaves little room over the hits for turning the loop up. A compressor or a limiter makes that gap smaller, and reading it before and after says how much either has done. After the card, pick the pad and compare the two rows of the scale in part 2.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Pick the window for the question" tag="Window at 3 s" set="slow">
    A reading is an answer to a question about some stretch of time. For "how loud is this phrase", average over seconds. For "how hard is this hit", average over a few hundredths of a second. After the card, go to stage 6 and bring Window down from 3 s, and stop where the line begins to follow the hits and no longer the phrase.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Leave room over the samples" tag="Set by on Peak, on noise" set="hiss">
    A sound whose samples reach the top has no room left for the wave between them. <Claim id="hiss">With noise set by its peak, the samples reach −6.02 dB and the wave between them reaches −1.1 dB</Claim>, nearly 5 dB higher. When a sound is brought close to full scale, read the peak between the samples and leave a decibel over it. After the card, raise Fader by 2 dB and read the two peaks in the lanes of part 5.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Check a meter with a tone" tag="Tone at 1 kHz" set="evenDrums">
    A meter can be tested with a sound whose readings are known beforehand. For a sine at 1 kHz on both channels, the level is 3.01 dB under the tallest sample and the loudness in LUFS equals the tallest sample in decibels against full scale, as stages 5 and 8 measured. After the card, read the tone's row of the scale in part 2 against that, then move Tone and watch the loudness leave the peak while the level hardly moves.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Five ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Matched by peak" set="tallPad">
    Two sounds are brought to the same height and one of them is far louder. It is the failed match of part 1, where the pad came out well over the drums on the loudness meter with not a hundredth of a decibel between their peaks. The peak says whether a sound fits and nothing else. Match by loudness, and then check the peak.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="An average that counts the rests" set="restsLevel">
    A sound with a rest in it reads lower on a plain average than it does while it plays, and a gain set from that reading is too high. <Claim id="rests">The pad with half of it silenced is turned up 2.9 dB further when it is set by its level, and 0.3 dB further when it is set by its loudness</Claim>. Listen to the card: the pad is the same pad as before, and it comes back louder. For anything with rests in it, use a reading that has a gate.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A long tail read as quiet" set="ringing">
    The same fault without any silence. Chords that ring away spend most of their time far under their own strum, and a plain average is pulled down by all of that time. <Claim id="ringing">Set by Level, the chords come out at −17.8 LUFS, with their tallest sample at −1.2 dB</Claim>. That is more than 2 LU over the loudness the cards of part 3 share, and a little over a decibel from full scale. The gate of stage 9 is the cure here too, since the blocks of the tail fall under it.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Over full scale between the samples">
    Every sample is under full scale and the output still distorts, because the wave the converter makes from the samples goes over. A peak reading taken from samples cannot see it. Stage 4 measured the distance: almost nothing on the drums, several decibels on noise. Read the peak between the samples whenever a sound is close to the top, and where it has to be held there, use the limiter of <Lesson id="limiting" />.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A window shorter than the wave">
    A level taken over less than one cycle of the lowest note goes up and down with the wave, and anything steered by it shakes at that rate. Stage 6 measured it on a 100 Hz tone: a window of one cycle held still, and a window a tenth as long swung by many decibels. Make the window at least as long as a cycle of the lowest note that matters, or smooth the reading twice as the compressor of stage 5 does.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="Nought decibels is silence.">
    A gain of 0 dB multiplies by one and changes nothing. On a meter, 0 dB against full scale is the loudest a sample can be. Silence has no number of decibels at all, and the Fader of part 2 has to be shut at the bottom of its travel to reach it.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A decibel is a unit of loudness.">
    A decibel is a ratio. It says how many times bigger one thing is than another, and it needs to be told what the other thing is. The same unit counts the gain of a fader, a height against full scale and a reading of the loudness meter, and part 2 sets the three beside each other.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Twice the decibels is twice the sound.">
    Decibels add where sizes multiply. Going from 6 dB to 12 dB on the Fader does not double anything a second time over: each 6 dB is one doubling, so the curve of part 2 reads about 2 at the first and about 4 at the second. And −12 dB is not twice as quiet as −6 dB by any measure. It is a quarter of the height where −6 dB is a half.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The meter with the tall bars shows how loud it is.">
    A peak meter shows how close the tallest sample is to the top. Part 1 set two loops to the same peak and the loudness meter read them far apart. The opening picture is the short form of it: a tone and a drum loop that are equally tall.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="If no sample is over full scale, nothing is over.">
    The wave between the samples can stand higher than any of them. The tone of stage 4, at its highest setting, is 3 dB taller than every one of its own samples, and the lane of that stage shows how far noise goes.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Equal levels are equally loud.">
    Level counts every frequency alike and takes its average over everything, rests included. Loudness does neither. In part 3 a bass and a pad at one level read more than 2 LU apart, and in part 7 a pad with a rest in it is turned up too far by its level and nearly rightly by its loudness.
  </Myth>
</Myths>
