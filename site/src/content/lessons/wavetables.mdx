<Part id="why" title="One stored cycle plays one note. Read faster, it folds.">

A synthesiser needs waves of many colours: a hollow reed, a bright saw, a vowel. Each of them can be written as a sum of sine waves, one at the note's own frequency and others at twice, three times, four times that frequency and so on up. Those are its harmonics. Adding up hundreds of sines for every sample of every key is too much work to do while a note plays.

So the engine's Wavetable does the sum once, when it starts, for one cycle of each wave, and keeps the answers: <Src path="cpp/devices/wavetable/wavetable.h" at="static constexpr int kSize = 2048;">2,048 numbers for a cycle</Src>. This lesson calls each number an entry, and the list a table. After that, a note costs a look in a list.

Nothing goes into this lesson's lab. It presses one key <Src path="site/labs/wavetables.cpp" at="constexpr int kKeyDown = 480;">10 ms into the loop</Src>, on the Wavetable and on the engine's Sampler at once, and every sound and picture on this page is read out of those two devices while that key is down. The first four parts are about the Wavetable. The Sampler arrives in part 5.

The simplest way to play a stored cycle is to send out one entry for each sample, and to start again after the last. That gives a note, but only one. The lab runs at <Src path="site/labs/lab.h" at="constexpr float kSampleRate = 48000.0f;">48,000 samples a second</Src>, and a cycle that long comes round at <Claim id="one-pitch">23.4 Hz, with Pitch at 220 Hz and at 880 Hz alike</Claim>. Pitch has nothing to turn.

To play any other note, the read has to move through the list at another speed. For a higher note it skips entries. For a lower one it reads between them. That is the whole idea of a wavetable, and part 4 builds it. The trouble lies in what the cycle holds.

The Wavetable's fullest copy of a cycle holds <Src path="cpp/devices/wavetable/wavetable.h" at="static constexpr int kHarmonics = 512;">512 harmonics</Src>. Read at the speed of a note, harmonic number 2 comes out at twice the note's frequency, number 3 at three times, and number 512 at 512 times. Low on the keyboard that does no harm.

Higher up, the top harmonics would stand above half the sample rate, and a sampled sound cannot hold them. <Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" cap /> shows what becomes of a frequency up there: it comes out under half the rate instead. It has folded back. A harmonic that has folded is no longer a multiple of the note. It lands between the note's own harmonics, at a pitch that has nothing to do with the tune.

Both ways of reading can be heard. <Hear set="full" label="Hear the full cycle at a high key" /> plays the Wavetable's own stored saw, read at the speed of a high key with nothing to protect it. The lab does that reading itself, because the device never does. <Hear set="kept" label="Hear the Wavetable on the same key" /> plays the device's own oscillator. Listen for tones under and between the note in the first, and for whether they are there in the second.

Measured on those two sounds, <Claim id="full-folds">at 1,760 Hz what is not on a harmonic of the note stands at −13.4 dB against the whole sound in the full cycle, and at −107.8 dB in the Wavetable's oscillator</Claim>. Every figure in this lesson for what is off the note, which is what has folded back and whatever else a read adds, counts the same band: <Claim id="band">everything more than 8.8 Hz from every multiple of the note's pitch, in 65,536 samples of the held note, which is 1.37 s of it, starting 0.5 s after the key goes down</Claim>. The figure is the level of that band against the level of everything in the stretch. For the Sampler, whose note does not hold still that long, part 5 uses a shorter stretch and says so.

So the question of this lesson: how does the device play one stored saw at every key and leave so little between the harmonics?

Written down, the cycle is a sum:

<M
  block
  t={String.raw`c(\theta) = \sum_{n=1}^{H} a_n \sin\bigl(2\pi\,(n\,\theta + \varphi_n)\bigr)`}
/>

Read it against the two pictures. <M t={String.raw`\theta`} /> is the place in the cycle, from 0 at its start to 1 at its end: the first picture's axis. <M t="n" /> counts the harmonics up to <M t="H" />, which is 512 for the cycle drawn. <M t="a_n" /> is the height of bar number <M t="n" /> in the second picture, and <M t={String.raw`\varphi_n`} /> says where in its own cycle that harmonic starts. Position and Table change the heights. Pitch does not: it only decides where the bars stand, because a cycle read <M t="f" /> times a second puts harmonic <M t="n" /> at <M t={String.raw`n f`} />. A bar fits while

<M block t={String.raw`n\,f < \tfrac{1}{2} f_s`} />

with <M t="f_s" /> the sample rate, and folds when it does not.

The sentence to check against the picture: <Claim id="first-fold">with Pitch at 46.8 Hz or under it, all 512 bars stand under half the rate, and at 47 Hz, 510 do</Claim>. The two that do not have come back from the right-hand edge. At the Pitch the page is on now:

A stored cycle is cheap to read, and it can hold any wave that repeats. What it cannot hold is a sound that does not repeat: a breath, a struck drum, a word. So the two devices of this lesson have different jobs. Each card below shows a sound, says why this is the tool for it, lists a setting and plays it. A card's button moves the whole page to its setting, so every figure further down shows it too, and the play bar offers the way back to where you were.

<Part id="build" title="Six pieces: from a list of numbers to the Wavetable's voice.">

This part starts with the stored cycle and nothing else, and adds six pieces to it one at a time. After each piece the picture is drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

The build reads the device's own stored cycles. It also takes from the device, as the key plays, what is not this lesson's subject: where in its cycle each oscillator starts, the envelope that shapes the note, and the numbers the device's controls have glided to. The reading is done again in the build's own lines. With the sixth piece in, the build is held against the device's output on every render.

<Part id="ways" title="Five ways to use stored sound.">

Each card sets the whole page and plays. The first four play the Wavetable, the last the Sampler. After a card, the controls are yours: move one and the sound carries on from the card's setting.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Stay in one place" tag="Motion at 0" set="still">
    Every place in a row is a wave of its own, and with Motion at 0 the read stays on it. The Spectral table has four clusters of harmonics that wander and change places along its row, so each place is a different steady chord of partials. After the card, move Position a little at a time and listen for one colour turning into the next without a step. Stage 1 of part 5 shows the two stored cycles the read is between.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Two oscillators a few cents apart" tag="Detune, with Spread at 0" set="beat">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="out"
      set="beat"
      caption="The output over the loop with the two oscillators detuned and both in the middle. Its outline swells and thins as they drift in and out of step."
    />

    Two copies of one wave, tuned a little apart, slide past each other. Where they line up they add, and where they are opposite they cancel, and each harmonic does this at its own rate, the high ones fastest. Spread is at 0 on this card, so both oscillators stand in the middle and add and cancel in full. After the card, raise Spread. One oscillator moves towards each side, the swell in each side gets shallower, and at 1 it is gone: each side then has one oscillator alone, and the drift is between the two sides.

  </Way>
  <Way title="A colour that crosses from side to side" tag="Motion, with Spread at 1" set="wide">
    Detune is at 0 on this card, so the two oscillators of the key play the same pitch. They still differ, because the second one reads the Motion LFO a quarter of a cycle after the first, as stage 5 of part 5 has it. Each is at a different place in the row at any moment, and Spread puts one on each side. Listen for a brightness that moves across without the note moving. After the card, bring Spread down to 0 and listen for the motion folding into the middle.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Make harmonics, then take them away" tag="Cutoff low, Resonance up" set="closed">
    The Hollow table is a square wave whose pulse narrows along the row: a wave with a great many harmonics. On this card the filter is closed over most of them, and Resonance lifts the ones near Cutoff. The table gives the filter something to work on, and Motion keeps changing what that is. After the card, sweep Cutoff upwards and listen for the harmonics the table made coming back one after another.
  </Way>
  <Way title="There and back along a recording" tag="Loop on Ping-pong" set="pingpong">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="samplerPicture"
      view="spectrogram"
      set="pingpong"
      caption="The Sampler with Loop on Ping-pong. The upper harmonics grow as the head reads forwards, up to the turn a little under two seconds in, and shrink again as it reads back the way it came, until the key is let go."
    />

    With Loop on Ping-pong the read head turns round at each end of its stretch and comes back. There is no jump, so there is nothing for a crossfade to hide, and a sound that changes all the way along, as this recording does, loops without a seam. Tune is down a fourth on this card, which also makes each pass longer. After the card, try Loop on Forward and listen for the colour starting again at each pass.

<Part id="wrong" title="Four ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="The whole cycle, read too high" set="full">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="naive osc"
      view="spectrum"
      set="full"
      min={-110}
      max={-10}
      caption="The full cycle read at a high pitch, and the Wavetable on the same key. The first line has peaks all the way between the harmonics. The second has the harmonics and little else."
    />

    This is the fault of part 1, and the reason for half of this lesson. A cycle with more harmonics than the pitch has room for puts the rest back down among the others, at frequencies that have nothing to do with the note. The card plays it. Listen for the tones between the harmonics. After the card, move Pitch a little and listen for some of them going down as the note goes up: a harmonic that has folded once moves the opposite way to the pitch.

    What to do about it is step 4 of the build: keep copies with fewer harmonics and read the one that fits. Making the read between entries finer does not help, as step 3 measured.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A recording played far from its own pitch" set="shrunk">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="head"
      set="shrunk"
      min={0}
      max={2}
      caption="The read head with the recording played an octave up. It crosses its stretch in half the time, so the stretch comes round twice as often."
    />

    A Sampler changes pitch by changing speed, and speed changes everything else with it. <Claim id="sample-shrinks">At Tune 0 the head reaches the end of its stretch 1.40 s after the key goes down. 12 semitones up, it gets there after 0.70 s</Claim>. The slow change of colour in this recording goes by twice as fast. So would the attack of a real instrument, its vibrato, and the fixed resonances of its body, which move up with the note when they should stay where they are.

    The usual cure is more recordings: one for every few keys, each played only near its own pitch. This device holds one sound, so the cure here is to stay within a few semitones of it, or to use the Wavetable where the motion must not depend on the key.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Just over the Sampler's threshold" set="justOver">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="sampledFull sampled"
      view="spectrum"
      set="justOver"
      min={-110}
      max={-10}
      caption="The key two semitones up, read from the full-rate recording and from the half-rate copy the Sampler chose. The lines part at the far right, where the recording's highest harmonics are: the half-rate copy ends below them. What it adds between the harmonics is too fine to see at this size, and the number in the text measures it."
    />

    The Sampler changes to its half-rate copy as soon as a key's step passes the first threshold of stage 10. Two semitones up is just past it, and on this bright recording the change costs more than it saves. <Claim id="guard-cost">With Tune at 2 semitones, −40.8 dB of what the Sampler makes is off the note, where the full-rate recording read at the same speed leaves −92.5 dB. At Tune 0, where both read the full-rate recording, the figure is −104.1 dB</Claim>, counted as in stage 10.

    The second of those figures is one key, and it flatters the full-rate read. The count takes for the note whatever lies inside its band around a harmonic, and what a read between frames adds to a sound lands at frequencies that move with the speed: inside the band at one Tune, clear of it at the next. <Claim id="guard-range">Half a semitone on, at Tune 2.5, the Sampler leaves −40.7 dB and the full-rate read −51.7 dB. At every whole Tune from 2 to 7 semitones the Sampler leaves between −38.6 dB and −40.8 dB, and the full-rate read leaves less each time. It comes nearest at 7 semitones, with −44.2 dB, and at 8 semitones the order is the other way</Claim>. So across that stretch the half-rate copy is the worse of the two, and the gap on the card's own key is not the measure of how much worse.

    Two things in part 5 point at why. The half-rate copy holds sound right up to half its own rate, which is where the curve of stage 9 is at its lowest. And the filter that makes the copy <Src path="cpp/devices/sampler/sampler.h" at="// ones other than the centre are zero: flat to 0.22 of the sample rate," to="// 50 dB down from 0.28. halfband_[m] is tap 2m + 1.">is not yet fully closed</Src> at the frequency where folding starts. The octave of stage 10 goes some way to telling them apart. <Claim id="guard-eleven">With Tune at 12 semitones the head moves by exactly one frame of the half-rate copy on every sample, so nothing is read between frames there, and the Sampler's figure is the copy as its filter made it. A semitone under, at Tune 11, the head stands between frames again: the Sampler leaves −39 dB and the full-rate read −34 dB</Claim>. That points at the read between frames as the larger of the two causes, and it makes the octave the guard's best case.

    What to do: keep a bright sound within a semitone of its own pitch, where the Sampler still reads the full-rate recording, or record it again at the pitch it is wanted at.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Taking the entry below">
    Reading a table at the entry the phase has just passed, and not between two, is the cheapest read there is. It is also the one mistake in this lesson that a bigger table does cure, since the staircase gets finer with every entry added. Step 3 of the build has the measure of it on a 2,048-entry saw, and stage 4 of part 5 on the kit's sine table. The straight line costs one more fetch and a little arithmetic, and the Wavetable and the kit's sine table both pay it.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Five things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A wavetable is a very short sample.">
    Both are stored numbers read by a pointer that moves, and that is where the likeness ends. A Sampler's pitch is its speed, so a higher key gets through the recording sooner: the second card of part 7 shows the stretch halved by an octave. A Wavetable's pitch is how fast it goes round one cycle, and its place in the row is a separate number that the key does not touch. Stage 5 of part 5 has the measurement: the same motion, point for point, two octaves apart.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A bigger table would stop the folding.">
    The rule of part 2 has the harmonic's number, the pitch and the sample rate in it. The number of entries is not there. A cycle with 512 harmonics folds at the same pitches whether it is stored in 2,048 entries or in a million. A bigger table makes the read between entries more exact, which is the fourth card of part 7, and that is a different fault.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A better read between entries would stop the folding.">
    Step 3 of the build tried it. Going from the entry below to a straight line brought the read much nearer to the stored cycle, and the part of the sound off the note hardly moved. The folded tones are not a mistake in reading the cycle. They are the cycle, read correctly, at a pitch it has too many harmonics for.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A copy with fewer harmonics makes the high notes dull.">
    The harmonics a copy leaves out are the ones that would have been over half the sample rate at that key. They could not have sounded as harmonics. All they could do is fold. What is kept reaches high at every pitch: stage 2 of part 5 measures the top harmonic of the copy read across the whole of the Pitch control, and it stays in the top octave of what the sample rate can carry.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A straight line is too crude for sound.">
    It depends on how many entries the fastest wave in the table gets. In the Wavetable that is four at the least, in the fullest copy, which only the lowest keys read, and it doubles with every copy above. Step 3 of the build measured the fullest copy of the saw, where the fastest wave gets the fewest entries. Higher up, the straight line has little left to get wrong: <Claim id="line-fine">with Pitch at 880 Hz, the copy of the saw the key reads, read between its entries, lies −92 dB from that copy itself, where the fullest copy lay −46.4 dB from itself</Claim>. A recording can hold waves with barely two frames each, and there the Sampler takes four neighbours, as stage 9 shows. The read is chosen for what is stored.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
