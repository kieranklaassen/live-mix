<Part id="why" title="An oscillator gives one colour at one level. A note is neither.">

Press a key on a bass synthesiser and the note snaps open, then closes and darkens as it dies. <Hear set="voice" label="Hear the note" /> Listen for the bright edge at the very start, and for how round the note has become a moment before it is gone. The lower picture above is that note's size over time.

The upper picture is where the sound begins, and it does not die away. It is an oscillator: a few lines of code that draw the same wave shape again and again, once for every cycle of the note. Nothing goes into this lesson's lab. It presses one key on the engine's Ladder Bass, <Src path="site/labs/oscillators-and-subtractive-voice.cpp" at="constexpr int kKeyDown = 1440;">30 ms into the loop</Src>, holds it for the time that Key held for sets, and reads the sound at every block on the way to the output.

Here is an oscillator with nothing after it. It is the one in the engine's shared code, the kit, which <Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" /> builds and measures. It plays a saw wave, a ramp that climbs and drops, and the lab turns it on when the key goes down and off when the key comes up. <Hear set="raw" label="Hear the oscillator alone" /> Listen for what does not happen. It does not swell, fade, brighten or dull. It starts, stays and stops.

A steady note is a sum of sine waves: one at the note's own frequency, and others at twice, three times, four times that frequency and on up. Those are its harmonics, and <Lesson id="fourier-transform" /> is about taking a sound apart into them. The first harmonic is the pitch. How strong the others are beside it is the colour of the sound. In the two pictures below, time runs across and frequency runs up, so each level line is one harmonic.

The bass's note moves in both. On its output, at the lesson's starting settings, <Claim id="voice-moves">the tenth harmonic stands 19 dB under the first 10 ms after the key goes down and 36 dB under it at 300 ms, and the first harmonic itself has fallen 28.8 dB in between, each read over the four cycles of the note that follow the moment named</Claim>. The note begins nearly as bright as the bare saw. It ends darker and far quieter.

How is a note that opens bright, closes dark and dies away made out of a wave that does none of those things?

### The obvious answers fail

The harmonics could be made one at a time: a sine wave for each, with a level and a fade of its own. That is additive synthesis, and it can make any steady sound there is. It is also a great deal to set, because a low saw has harmonics all the way to the top of hearing and each one would need its own numbers.

A cheaper idea keeps the oscillator and turns its level down over the note. <Lesson id="plucked-string" cap /> tries exactly that against a string and measures what goes wrong. A fade multiplies the whole wave by one number, so every harmonic falls together, and the note ends as bright as it began, only smaller.

### Start with too much, and take away

The technique of this lesson turns the problem round. The wanted harmonics are not built up. The sound starts as a wave that has all of them, and the ones that are not wanted are removed. The remover is a low-pass filter, which lets through what lies below a frequency called its cutoff and takes away more and more of what lies above it. <Lesson id="one-pole" cap /> and <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" /> each build one. Move the cutoff and the colour moves. This is subtractive synthesis.

A note then needs little. One or more oscillators supply the harmonics. A filter decides how many of them get through. And one slowly changing number, which the engine's bass calls the contour, tells the filter when to open and close and tells the output how loud to be. That chain, played by one key, is a voice.

<Part id="build" title="Start with a bare wave. Add one piece. Listen. Add the next.">

This part makes the voice. It starts with a single oscillator and adds five pieces to it, one at a time. After each piece the pictures are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

Each piece is the device's own arithmetic, written out again a function at a time, and with all six in place the build is held to the device on every render. Part 5 then reads the same pieces in the device's source.

Stretched to run from −1 to 1 it is a saw wave. Held at 1 for the first half of the cycle and at −1 for the second, it is a square wave. Wave shares the level between the two readings.

Stage 1 of part 5 measures how much folding is left in this voice.

<Part id="engine" title="Inside the Ladder Bass, block by block.">

The Ladder Bass is one voice: whatever keys are held, it plays the newest. When a key goes down while another is still held, the device slides the pitch from the old key to the new one in the time its Glide control sets. The lab never holds two keys at once, so it has no Glide, and this page measures nothing about it. The device's whole signal path is one loop in <Src path="cpp/devices/ladder-bass/ladder_bass.h" at="void process(int frames) {">`process`</Src>, and the picture below is that loop. The sound runs along the main row. The contour is not sound. It is a number, worked out beside the sound, that is used in two places.

### The same chain, a voice for every key

The bass plays one note at a time. A chord needs the whole chain once for every key, and the engine's pad instrument, Dusk, has <Src path="cpp/devices/dusk/dusk.h" at="static constexpr int kMaxVoices = 10;">ten of them</Src>. Each is the same three things in the same order, with different choices made at each. The lab holds three keys on it for as long as it holds the bass's key.

<StageBlock tap="padEnv" n={7} title="An envelope with a slow rise and a level it holds">

<Part id="wrong" title="Clicks, a note that never opens, and three ways to lose the bottom.">

The chain is simple, and most of what goes wrong in it is one piece doing exactly what it says while another is set against it.

<Part id="myths" title="Five things people say about subtractive synthesis.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="Opening the filter adds brightness.">
    A low-pass filter adds nothing. The harmonics were made by the oscillator, and opening the filter stops taking them away. In the row of part 2, the bars before the filter do not move when Cutoff does. This is why the choice of wave comes first: a filter wide open over a sine wave has nothing to let through.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The cutoff is where the sound stops.">
    The cutoff is where the slope begins. Stage 5 of part 5 measured the filter at its cutoff and above it: a harmonic at the cutoff is turned down, not removed, and those above it are turned down further the higher they stand. The curve there has no wall in it.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Two oscillators are twice as loud as one.">
    Only while they are in step, and two oscillators that are tuned apart are in step for a moment at a time. The second step of the build shows the level swelling and emptying as they pass each other. What a second oscillator adds is movement.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="More resonance makes a bigger sound.">
    It makes a peak, and it takes level from everything under the peak to do it. Stage 5 measured the loss, and the fourth fault of part 7 is what it does to a low note. This filter gives nothing back for it.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A voice needs one envelope for the filter and another for the level.">
    Many synthesisers have two. Neither instrument on this page does. The bass's one contour and the pad's one envelope each move the cutoff and the level together, as the last step of the build and the two signal-flow pictures of part 5 show. One shape is enough for a note that is brightest when it is loudest.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
