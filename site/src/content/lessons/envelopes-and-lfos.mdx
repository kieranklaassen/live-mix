<Part id="why" title="A tone has no beginning and no end. A note has both.">

Hold down a key of a pipe organ and the pipe speaks at once, at one loudness, until the key is let go. Strike a piano key and the note is loudest at the start, then dies away under your finger. Draw a bow across a string and the note swells. All three can play the same pitch, and nobody mistakes one for another. A large part of the difference is how loud the note is at each moment of its life: its outline.

An oscillator has no outline. It makes a tone, and the tone never stops. The one in this lesson is the kit's saw wave, which the lab starts at <Src path="site/labs/envelopes-and-lfos.cpp" at='lab::Control pitch{"pitch"'>220 Hz</Src>. In the picture at the top of the page it is the band that runs the whole width of the loop. Listen to it for a few seconds: nothing in it says where a note begins. <Hear set="tone" label="Hear the tone alone" />

So here is the question this lesson answers. How does a tone that never stops become a note, which starts when a key goes down, ends after the key is let go, and has an outline of its own in between?

Nothing goes into this lesson's lab. It presses one key <Src path="site/labs/envelopes-and-lfos.cpp" at="constexpr int kKeyDown = 480;">10 ms into the loop</Src>, holds it for the time that Key held for sets, and lets it go. Every sound and picture on this page is that one note.

### The obvious answer fails

Let the key switch the tone on and off. While it is held the tone passes, and when it is let go the tone is cut. Written as a number, the key is called a gate. The gate is 1 while the key is held and 0 while it is not, and the switched tone is the tone times the gate.

The key does not wait for the wave to pass through zero. Measured on that render, <Claim id="switch-jump">the switched tone goes from nothing to 0.23 between one sample and the next, more than half of the tone's peak of 0.4, and it falls from 0.24 to nothing as fast when the key is let go</Claim>. A jump is a sound of its own. Listen for a tick at each end of the note, which is not part of the tone. <Hear set="switched" label="Hear the tone switched" />

There is a second failure, and it is the larger one. Even when the key falls kindly, on a zero of the wave, a switched tone has one outline only: all of it, then none of it. It can be the organ pipe. It cannot be the piano or the bowed string.

### A number that takes its time

What is wanted is a number to multiply the tone by, as the gate was, but one that takes its time. It rises when the key goes down, and it falls when the key is let go. Drawn against time, that number is the outline of the note. The thing that makes it is called an envelope generator, or an envelope for short.

### And a number that never stops

An envelope moves because somebody played a note, once for each note. It has a relative that moves all the time and for nobody. A low-frequency oscillator, an LFO, is an oscillator turning too slowly to be heard as a tone. Its wave is not listened to. It is used as a number, and sent to move something that is: a pitch, to make vibrato; a level, to make tremolo; the cutoff of a filter, to make a sweep.

The two are the same kind of thing. Each is a signal nobody hears, which moves something everybody hears. The envelope asks when the key went down. The LFO does not ask anything.

This lesson has one envelope and three LFOs. The envelope and the first LFO are the kit's own classes, and that LFO bends the tone's pitch. The other two live inside devices: the Auto Filter carries an LFO for its cutoff, and the Tremolo carries one for its level. The note passes through both.

<Part id="picture" title="Three times and one level.">

An envelope of this kind is set with four numbers, and it is usually called by their initials: attack, decay, sustain, release, or ADSR. Three of the four are times. One is a level.

- **Attack** is how long the level takes to reach the top after the key goes down.
- **Decay** is how fast it then falls towards the sustain level.
- **Sustain** is the level it holds for as long as the key is held.
- **Release** is how fast it dies away once the key is let go.

<Controls ids="attack sustain release" />

<Trace taps="gate envelope" min={0} max={1.05}>
  The key and the envelope's level across the loop. Press play, and a line runs along the picture
  with the sound: where it crosses the level is the number the tone is being multiplied by at that
  moment. Try Sustain from one end to the other, and notice the flat part move up and down without
  getting longer or shorter.
</Trace>

Check this against the picture. Leave the three controls where the lesson starts them. The key goes down near the left edge, and the level reaches the top <Claim id="attack-time">0.200 s</Claim> later, which is what Attack is set to. From the top it falls, and it settles at <Claim id="sustain-level">0.5</Claim>, which is what Sustain is set to. It stays there until the key goes up, however long that takes. Then it dies away.

Notice who decides what. The four numbers are set once, for every note. How long a note lasts is not among them: the player decides that, by holding the key. Three of the four stages last as long as their own settings say, and the fourth, the sustain, lasts as long as the key does. That is why Sustain has to be a level. A time would be the wrong kind of answer.

Decay is the control this picture leaves out, to keep it to three. It is added in part 4, where each of the four is built in turn.

</Part>

<StageBlock tap="attackKit" n={2} title="Attack: aim past the top, stop at the top">
  The attack has to do two things that pull against each other. It should rise as natural things rise, fast at first and easing off. And it should be finished at a known time, because Attack is a time.

  <Controls ids="attack" />

  <Curve taps="attackKit:out attackAim:alt" xTitle="seconds after the key goes down" yTitle="level" min={0} max={1.35} height={300}>
    The kit's attack, run by itself with the key held, and the curve it is cut from, left to run on.
    Try Attack longer and shorter, and notice the picture stay the same while the numbers along the
    bottom change: the shape never changes, only how long it takes.
  </Curve>

  The curve that runs on is the answer to the first demand. On every sample the level moves a fixed share of the way towards a target:

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{L[n]} = L[n-1] + \at{stages-rise}{a}\,\bigl(1.3 - L[n-1]\bigr)`} />

  Here <M t="L[n]" /> is the level at sample <M t="n" />, and <M t="a" /> is the share. As step 2 of part 4 said, a level that moves this way never arrives. This is the one-pole filter of <Lesson id="one-pole" /> with a constant going in.

  The answer to the second demand is the target. It is <Src path="cpp/kit/env.h" at="static constexpr float kAttackTarget = 1.3f;">1.3</Src>, above the top, and the level is stopped as it passes 1. A curve that would take for ever to reach 1.3 passes 1 at a definite moment. The share is then chosen so that this moment is the Attack time:

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{coeffs-attack}{a = 1 - \left(\frac{0.3}{1.3}\right)^{1/N_A}}`} />

  <M t="N_A" /> is the Attack time counted in samples. Read it against the picture. After each sample, the distance to 1.3 is what it was times <M t="1 - a" />. After <M t="N_A" /> samples it has been multiplied by 0.3 over 1.3, and since it began at 1.3 it is now 0.3: the level is 1.3 less 0.3, the top. Measured on the kit with Attack where the lesson starts it, <Claim id="attack-aim">its level and the curve that runs on stay within 0.0001 of each other until the top, and left to run the curve stands at 1.27 after two and a half Attack times</Claim>.

  The part of the curve that is kept is its steep beginning, which is why a quarter of the time gave four tenths of the height in part 1.

  The stop does not fall exactly on the Attack time. The kit works the share out in single precision, and at long times the share is so small that its last digits are lost. Measured to the sample, <Claim id="attack-exact">with Attack at 0.2 s the level is at the top 9,602 samples after the key goes down, where 9,600 were asked; at 1 s it is there 45 samples early, and at 2 s 98 samples late; and at every setting the lab's test tries, from the shortest Attack to the longest, the top comes within 0.2 % of the time asked or within two samples of it</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="decayKit" n={3} title="Decay: close on the sustain level">
  From the top the level heads for the Sustain level, and holds it. There is no deadline to meet at this end, so the kit uses the plain curve and lets it run.

  </Curve>

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{L[n]} = S + \at{falls-fall}{d}\,\bigl(L[n-1] - S\bigr)`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{coeffs-decay}{d = 1000^{-1/N_D}}`} />

  <M t="S" /> is Sustain and <M t="N_D" /> is the Decay time in samples. Read the first line as the listing does: the distance from the level to Sustain is multiplied by <M t="d" /> on every sample. The second line sizes <M t="d" />. Multiply by it <M t="N_D" /> times and the distance has been divided by 1,000, which is 60 dB. The code writes the same number as an exponential, and its constant, <Src path="cpp/kit/env.h" at="static constexpr float kSixtyDb = 6.907755279f;">6.907755</Src>, is the natural logarithm of 1,000.

  The rule that ends the stage, which step 3 of part 4 copied, is the kit's: when the level is within <Src path="cpp/kit/env.h" at="static constexpr float kIdleLevel = 1.0e-5f;">0.00001</Src> of Sustain it is set there, and the stage becomes sustain, where nothing is worked out at all.

  On this page's note the rule is never reached. The level is a single-precision number, and near any one value such numbers stand a fixed small step apart. Close to Sustain, one sample's move becomes smaller than half of that step, and the level rounds back to where it was. Measured on the kit at the settings the lesson starts from, <Claim id="decay-rests">the level comes to rest 0.0001 above Sustain</Claim>, ten times further out than the rule asks, and the envelope is still in its decay stage when the key goes up.   The rule is reached when the numbers near the target are finer. With Sustain at 0 the level runs all the way down, passes the rule, and the note is over while the key is still held.

  A slow wave multiplying a sound, and what becomes of it when the wave is no longer slow, is the whole of <Lesson id="tremolo-and-ring-modulation" />, which opens this device stage by stage.

A note that is still dying when the next begins leaves the device no silence to sleep in: with a Release long enough for that, its oscillator turns all the time as well.

<Part id="ways" title="One slow wave, sent to different places.">

Most of what is done with an LFO is a choice of three things: how fast, what shape, and where to. Each card below changes one of them. The settings are places to start from, and each card's button plays them.

<Ways>
  <Way title="A trill" tag="a square wave on the pitch" set="trill">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="pitchNow"
      set="trill"
      span={1}
      caption="The tone's pitch over the first second. It stands at one of two levels and steps straight from one to the other."
    />

    A square wave has two values and nothing between them, so a pitch that follows it has two pitches and nothing between them. With the bend at a hundred cents, a semitone each way, the tone alternates <Claim id="trill">between 207.7 Hz and 233.1 Hz</Claim>: the two notes either side of the key that is held, which is itself never heard. Change the Shape to Sine and the same setting is a vibrato a semitone wide each way.

  </Way>
  <Way title="One sweep a note" tag="an LFO slower than the note" set="slow">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="sweep"
      set="slow"
      caption="How far the cutoff stands from where it is set, across the loop. It makes one slow cycle in the four seconds."
    />

    An LFO need not be fast enough to hear as a wobble. When one cycle lasts as long as a note, the LFO acts like a second outline, laid over the brightness in place of the loudness. The filter opens through the note and closes again. Because nothing ties the LFO to the key, each note of a real performance would meet the sweep at a different point. Here the cycle and the loop are the same length, so every pass is alike.

  </Way>
  <Way title="A new colour eight times a second" tag="a random shape on the cutoff" set="steps">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="sweep"
      set="steps"
      span={2}
      caption="Two seconds of the cutoff's distance from where it is set. It holds one value for each cycle of the filter's LFO and jumps to another, with no pattern to the heights."
    />

    The random shape draws one number each cycle and holds it. Sent to the cutoff with the resonance turned up, each cycle lands the cutoff somewhere else among the harmonics of the note. Listen for a run of bright steps with no pattern in it. On this page the run comes round with the loop. The Auto Filter's name for this shape is S&H, for sample and hold.

  </Way>
  <Way title="The note opens its own filter" tag="the follower on the cutoff" set="touch">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="sweep"
      set="touch"
      caption="How far the cutoff stands above where it is set, across the loop. It leaps up as the note starts and sinks back as the note dies away."
    />

    Set the cutoff low and let the follower lift it. The cutoff now rides on the loudness of the note itself, so the loud start of a plucked note is bright and its tail is dark, as it is on most instruments that are struck or plucked. A louder note opens the filter further. No LFO is involved, and nothing here repeats unless the playing does.

  </Way>
  <Way title="A level that throbs" tag="a triangle on the level" set="throb">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="gain"
      set="throb"
      span={2}
      caption="Two seconds of the gain the Tremolo puts on the note. It runs in straight lines between its top, which is 1, and its bottom."
    />

    Sent to the level, the same slow wave is tremolo. A triangle spends no time at the top or the bottom and turns at a point. Listen for a pulse slow enough to count. Change the Shape to Sine and listen for whether the throb is less marked, then try the Rate higher until the pulse can no longer be counted.

  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="What goes wrong, and what to do about it.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="A click at each end" set="click">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="note"
      set="click"
      from={0.005}
      span={0.03}
      caption="Thirty milliseconds around the start of a low note. The wave is at its full height almost from its first sample."
    />

    With Attack as short as it goes, the level is at the top <Claim id="click-attack">48 samples</Claim> after the key goes down. That is a small part of one cycle of the low note this card plays, so the outline is nearly the cliff of part 1 again, and this card's Release is as short as Release goes. Listen for a tick at each end. Lengthen the Attack and the Release until it goes. Low notes need more than high ones, because their cycles are longer.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A note that never arrives" set="short">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="gate envelope"
      set="short"
      span={1.5}
      max={1}
      caption="The start of the loop. The key is down only briefly, and the level has hardly left the floor when it goes up."
    />

    A slow attack suits long notes and loses short ones. The key in this card is let go a tenth of the way through the Attack time, and the level has reached only <Claim id="short-top">0.18</Claim> when the release takes over. Played fast, a part with this setting all but disappears. Shorten the Attack, or keep slow attacks for parts that hold their notes.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A release longer than the gap" set="overlap">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="gate envelope"
      set="overlap"
      max={1}
      caption="The key and the level. The level is still well above the floor at the right-hand edge, and the next pass of the loop begins from there."
    />

    When the Release is longer than the silence before the next note, the last note is still sounding when the next begins. Here <Claim id="overlap-start">the level stands at 0.16 when the key goes down again</Claim>. On one voice that is harmless, since the attack starts from where the level stands. Across the keys of a chord it is not: every note played keeps sounding under the ones that follow, and a changing harmony smears. Shorten the Release until the notes separate.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A wobble too fast to follow" set="fast">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="pitchNow"
      set="fast"
      span={0.5}
      caption="Half a second of the tone's pitch. It swings up and down many times, as far each way as the trill of part 6 stepped."
    />

    Speed an LFO up and listen for the point where the pitch can no longer be followed up and down, and what is left is a rougher tone that does not seem to move. That is no longer an LFO's work. A wave fast enough to be a tone, multiplying or bending another, makes new frequencies, and <Lesson id="tremolo-and-ring-modulation" /> follows that all the way. For vibrato, keep the Rate to a few cycles a second and the bend to a fraction of a semitone.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Two LFOs at one rate, out of step" set="apart">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="sweep tremLfo:alt"
      set="apart"
      caption="The cutoff's movement and the Tremolo's own LFO, both at one Rate. Their peaks do not fall together, and the Tremolo's line lies flat for a stretch where the device is asleep."
    />

    Two devices set to the same Rate are two clocks, and nothing makes them agree. In this card's loop <Claim id="apart-offset">the Tremolo's wave rises 0.06 s after the cutoff does, an eighth of their cycle</Claim>, because the Tremolo's oscillator stood still while the device slept and the Auto Filter's kept its time. If the two movements are meant to be one gesture, they need one LFO sent to both places, or two that are restarted together. Neither of these devices has a control that restarts its LFO. What can be done with this pair is to keep the Tremolo awake, on a sound that leaves it no silence to sleep in, as the end of stage 10 said. <Lesson id="tremolo-and-ring-modulation" cap /> has the same fault on a loop of clicks.

  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things people say about envelopes and LFOs.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="Sustain is how long the note is held.">
    Sustain is a level. The key decides how long the note is held, and the level stands at Sustain for exactly that long. The picture of part 2 shows it: move Sustain and the flat part goes up and down, and it gets no longer.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="When the Release time is up, the note is silent.">
    When the Release time is up, the note is 60 dB down, a thousandth of where it was. The kit calls it over at 100 dB under the top. On this page's note that takes more than half as long again, and stage 4 of part 5 has the measurement.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Decay is how long the level takes to reach Sustain.">
    It is how long the level takes to get a thousand times closer to Sustain. The stage itself lasts longer, and with Sustain at 0 it runs all the way down to 100 dB under the top. The plucked note of part 3 is over well after its Decay time is up, and stage 3 of part 5 has the measurement for the note of this page.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The attack is the release played backwards.">
    The release is a curve that would never arrive, cut off where it no longer matters. The attack is the steep start of a curve aimed above the top, cut off on the dot. Played backwards, a release would begin slowly and end fast. The attack begins fast and ends less fast. Stages 2 and 4 of part 5 show the two.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="An LFO starts again with each note.">
    The kit's does not. It has no idea that there are notes. In the lanes at the end of part 5 the LFO turns through the silence between one note and the next. Some instruments do restart an LFO with the key, and the kit's class has <Src path="cpp/kit/lfo.h" at="void reset(float phase = 0.0f)">a call for that</Src>. It is a choice the instrument makes.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="An envelope follower is a kind of envelope generator.">
    They share a word and a shape on the page. A generator is played: it starts when a key says so and draws an outline that was set beforehand. A follower listens: it has no key, and reports how loud a sound already is. Stage 9 of part 5 has the follower, and the first four stages have the generator.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
