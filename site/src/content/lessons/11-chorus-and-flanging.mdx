Play a sound and, a few thousandths of a second later, the same sound again, and the two do not simply add up to a louder one. At some frequencies the late copy arrives exactly in step with the first and the two reinforce. At others it arrives exactly out of step, crest on trough, and they cancel. Which frequencies cancel depends only on how late the copy is. The spectrum comes out striped at even distances, like the teeth of a comb, and the thing that does it is called a **comb filter**.

Everything else in this lesson is that comb put to use. **Flanging** is a comb whose delay is moved slowly back and forth by an oscillator, so the stripes slide up and down the spectrum. A **chorus** is the same delay made about five times longer and read at two or three moving points at once. Its stripes stand too close together to follow as a sweep, and the other thing a moving read point does comes forward instead: it plays the sound back a little sharp, then a little flat.

The delay line itself, reading it between samples and feeding it back are lesson 10's. This lesson takes them as given and looks at what a _short, moving_ delay does. The stages below are the Flanger's first and then the Chorus's. Press Play, then Listen on any stage that is sound.

## Stage by stage

### 1. A copy, a moment late: the comb

The whole filter is one line. With <M t="D" /> the delay in samples and <M t="m" /> the mix,

<M block t={String.raw`y[n] = (1-m)\,x[n] + m\,x[n-D]`} />

In the code that is `out[c] = in[c] + (wet - in[c]) * mix` in <Src path="cpp/devices/flanger/flanger.h">`Flanger::process`</Src>, where `wet` is what <Src path="cpp/kit/delay.h">the kit's `DelayLine`</Src> read. To see what it does to one frequency, put a sine through it. A delay leaves a sine's size alone and turns its phase, by <M t={String.raw`\omega D`} /> radians, where <M t={String.raw`\omega = 2\pi f / f_s`} /> is the sine's own turn per sample. So at each frequency the output is the input times

<M block t={String.raw`H(\omega) = (1-m) + m\,e^{-j\omega D}, \qquad |H(\omega)|^2 = (1-m)^2 + m^2 + 2m(1-m)\cos\omega D`} />

The cosine is the comb. Where <M t={String.raw`\omega D`} /> is a whole number of turns the copy is in step and the gain is 1. Where it is half a turn off, the copy is the input upside down and the gain is <M t={String.raw`|1-2m|`} />. With <M t={String.raw`\tau = D / f_s`} /> the delay in seconds, those places are

<M block t={String.raw`\text{peaks at } f = \frac{k}{\tau}, \qquad \text{notches at } f = \frac{2k+1}{2\tau}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots`} />

The first notch is the frequency whose half period is the delay, and the rest are its odd multiples, on up without end. At 2.5 ms that is 200 Hz, 600 Hz, 1,000 Hz. Measured on the noise above, the first three fall at <Claim id="notch-first">199 Hz, 598 Hz and 996 Hz</Claim>, each within one 11.7 Hz bin of the analysis. Set the delay to 5 ms and they stand at <Claim id="notch-moves">the odd multiples of 100 Hz</Claim>.

How deep a notch is depends on the mix alone. At equal parts the two cancel completely and the formula becomes <M t={String.raw`|H| = |\cos \pi f \tau|`} />. A 200 Hz sine through the stopped Flanger comes out <Claim id="notch-depth">more than 120 dB down</Claim>, which is the floor of the arithmetic, and a 400 Hz sine comes out <Claim id="tooth-top">as it went in</Claim>. Away from equal parts the cancelling is partial: at a mix of 0.25 the notch is <Claim id="notch-quarter">6.0 dB deep</Claim>, since <M t={String.raw`1 - 2 \times 0.25`} /> is one half. At a mix of 1 there is <Claim id="mix-one">no comb at all</Claim>, only a delay, because the notches need both signals. That is why the device's mix is a plain linear crossfade that starts at 0.5, and why turning a flanger all the way to its delayed side removes the flanging.

A comb at equal parts takes out as much as it leaves: white noise comes through it with half its power, <Claim id="comb-power">3.0 dB down</Claim>.

### 2. An oscillator moves the delay

A comb that stands still is a fixed filter. Flanging is the motion. A slow oscillator <M t="s(t)" />, running between −1 and +1, pushes the read point either side of the Delay setting:

<M block t={String.raw`d(t) = D\,\bigl(1 + 0.95 \cdot \text{depth} \cdot s(t)\bigr)`} />

Depth is a share of the delay itself, and the 0.95 (`kDepthScale`) keeps the read point from ever arriving at zero. At the starting settings, 2.5 ms and 50 %, the read point runs <Claim id="depth-half">from 1.31 ms to 3.69 ms</Claim>. At full depth it runs <Claim id="depth-full">from 0.125 ms to 4.875 ms</Claim>, the longest delay 39 times the shortest.

The oscillator is <Src path="cpp/devices/flanger/mod_lfo.h">`ModLfo`</Src>, the Flanger's own. (The kit has a general one in <Src path="cpp/kit/lfo.h">lfo.h</Src>; neither device here uses it.) It is a phase that climbs from 0 to 1 once a cycle and a shape read off the phase: a sine from <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h">the kit's table</Src>; a triangle, `1 - 4·|phase - 0.5|`; a ramp up, `2·phase - 1`, and its mirror; a square; and Random, one new value held for each cycle. The phase is a double, because at 0.01 Hz and 96 kHz its step per sample is under two steps of a float near 1, far too coarse to count a cycle with. Nothing the sound does resets it. The oscillator runs free, even while the device sleeps through a silence.

Rate starts at 0.25 Hz, one sweep in four seconds, which is the length of this loop.

Every notch follows the read point, at <M t={String.raw`f_k(t) = (2k+1) / 2d(t)`} />. That is one over the delay, so the motion in frequency is not the oscillator's shape. While the delay shortens the notches rise faster and faster and fan apart; while it lengthens they sink and close up.

The formula puts the lowest band at 136 Hz where the delay is longest and at 381 Hz where it is shortest, and on the noise it is found <Claim id="sweep-notch">within 12 Hz of both</Claim>, which is one point of the measurement.

A moving read point also replays the sound at a slightly different speed, which stage 5 works out. In the Flanger it is slight: at the starting settings the delayed copy is never more than <Claim id="flanger-cents">3.2 cents</Claim> off pitch. <Listen tap="flangerWet">Hear the delayed copy alone</Listen> and there is no sweep in it at all. The comb exists only in the sum.

### 3. Feedback sharpens the teeth

Feed some of the delayed copy back into the line, and every trip round adds another, later copy. The Flanger writes <M t={String.raw`x[n] + g \cdot \text{wet}[n-1]`} /> into its line, where <M t="g" /> is the Feedback setting, from −0.95 to +0.95. The copy it feeds back is the one it read on the sample before (`last_wet_`), so the loop is one sample longer than the delay, and the response becomes

<M block t={String.raw`H(\omega) = (1-m) + \frac{m\,e^{-j\omega D}}{1 - g\,e^{-j\omega (D+1)}}`} />

The denominator is lesson 10's geometric series, summed. Where a trip round the loop is a whole number of turns it shrinks to <M t="1-g" /> and the delayed path grows to <M t={String.raw`1/(1-g)`} /> times its size. Half way between it is <M t="1+g" /> and the path shrinks. With positive feedback the loud places are the comb's own peaks, and at a mix of 0.5 the two extremes are

<M block t={String.raw`\text{peak} = \frac{2-g}{2(1-g)}, \qquad \text{trough} = \frac{g}{2(1+g)}`} />

At 50 % feedback the peaks stand at <Claim id="feedback-peak">+3.5 dB</Claim>. The price is paid in the notches. The delayed path is now weaker there than the dry signal, the two no longer cancel, and the notch that went to nothing is only <Claim id="feedback-trough">−15.6 dB</Claim>. Feedback does not deepen a comb. It turns broad humps and needle notches into needle peaks and broad valleys. At the top of the range, 95 %, the delayed path alone reaches <Claim id="loop-26">+26 dB</Claim> and the sum <Claim id="feedback-95">+20.4 dB</Claim>.

The sign matters. Negative feedback turns each trip upside down, so the loop reinforces where a trip is _half_ a turn: the odd multiples, exactly where the dry signal and the copy cancel. The two effects pull against each other. At −50 % the old notches are filled to <Claim id="negative-half">−6.1 dB</Claim>. Near −62 % they meet, and below 1 kHz there is <Claim id="negative-flat">about 1 dB of ripple</Claim> where a comb with no feedback has notches to nothing. (With the extra sample left out, the formula has the same size at every frequency when <M t={String.raw`1 + |g| = 1/|g|`} />, which is <M t={String.raw`|g| = 0.618`} />.) Past that the loop wins and the odd multiples become the peaks: <Claim id="negative-peaks">+13.0 dB</Claim> at −90 %.

### 4. The chorus: a longer delay, read by several voices

The Chorus is the same drawing with three changes. Its delay runs from 5 to 30 ms and starts at 12, where the Flanger's runs from 0.5 to 10. Its sweep is a distance and not a share: Depth times 5 ms (`kMaxDeviationMs`), or times the delay less 1 ms where that is smaller. And its line is read by two or three **voices** at once, each a read point of its own:

<M block t={String.raw`d_v(t) = D + A \sin 2\pi\!\left(f t + \frac{v}{N}\right), \qquad A = \text{depth} \cdot \min(5\ \text{ms},\ D - 1\ \text{ms})`} />

Here <M t="N" /> is the number of voices and <M t="v" /> counts them from 0. One oscillator serves them all, and it is a sine only: <Src path="cpp/devices/chorus/chorus.h">the device</Src> looks up one sine and one cosine of its phase per sample and turns the pair by each voice's angle, so three voices on two sides cost two look-ups, not six. At the starting settings each of three voices runs <Claim id="voice-range">from 9.5 ms to 14.5 ms</Claim>, each <Claim id="voices-third">a third of a cycle, 0.417 s</Claim>, ahead of the one before. Two voices stand <Claim id="voices-half">half a cycle</Claim> apart. Sines spaced evenly round a cycle add to nothing, so while every voice moves, their delays add to the same <Claim id="voices-sum">36 ms</Claim> at every instant: the group as a whole stays put. With the delay at its shortest the cap comes in, and at 5 ms and full depth the sweep is <Claim id="chorus-cap">4 ms either way</Claim>.

### 5. A moving delay is a change of pitch

A voice puts out <M t={String.raw`y(t) = x\bigl(t - d(t)\bigr)`} />. While the delay grows, the read point is falling behind: it gets through less than a second of sound in each second, and everything it reads comes out slow and flat. While the delay shrinks it is catching up, and the sound comes out fast and sharp. The speed is

<M block t={String.raw`r(t) = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,d(t), \qquad \text{cents} = 1200 \log_2 r`} />

It does not depend on how long the delay is, only on how fast it is changing. A cent is a hundredth of a semitone. For a sine sweep the slope is steepest as the delay passes its centre:

<M block t={String.raw`\max \left| \frac{\mathrm{d}d}{\mathrm{d}t} \right| = 2\pi f A`} />

At the Chorus's starting settings that is <M t={String.raw`2\pi \times 0.8\ \text{Hz} \times 2.5\ \text{ms} = 0.0126`} />, a speed 1.26 % off. Read from the slope of the delay the device used, each voice goes <Claim id="chorus-cents">21.6 cents sharp and 21.9 cents flat</Claim>; the two differ because the logarithm is not even about 1. And measured from the sound itself, a 1 kHz sine comes back from one voice at anything <Claim id="chorus-cents-heard">from 987.4 Hz to 1,012.6 Hz</Claim>, counted from its zero crossings. Rate and depth multiply, so doubling either doubles the bend.

### 6. The sum, and two sides

The voices are added, scaled, and crossfaded with the input, on each side <M t="c" />:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{wet}_c[n] = \frac{1}{\sqrt N} \sum_{v} p_{v,c}\; x_c\bigl[n - d_{v,c}\bigr], \qquad \text{out}_c = x_c + m\,(\text{wet}_c - x_c)`} />

The scale is <M t={String.raw`1/\sqrt N`} /> and not <M t="1/N" /> because voices that have drifted apart are different signals, and different signals add in power. On white noise, three voices at the starting depth sum to the level of one, <Claim id="apart">within 0.05 dB</Claim>. With the depth at 0 the three are one signal and add in size: <Claim id="together">4.8 dB over one</Claim>. The linear mix dips for the same reason. Dry noise and delayed noise are different signals too, and at a mix of 0.5 the output is <Claim id="mix-dip">3.4 dB under the input</Claim>.

Spread makes the two sides differ, in two ways at once. It pans the voices, the first towards the left and the last towards the right, with a middle one staying in the centre. The gains <M t={String.raw`p_{v,c}`} /> are equal-power: <M t={String.raw`\sqrt2\cos\theta_v`} /> on the left and <M t={String.raw`\sqrt2\sin\theta_v`} /> on the right, with <M t={String.raw`\theta_v`} /> running from 45° less Spread × 45° to 45° more. And it runs the right side's oscillator ahead of the left's by Spread × 90°, so that even a centred voice reads a different point on each side. At 70 % the right side is 63° ahead, and rises through the centre <Claim id="right-ahead">0.219 s sooner</Claim>.

With two voices and full spread <Claim id="one-each">each ear gets one voice to itself</Claim>, the first on the left and the second on the right. The second voice is half a cycle from the first and the right side's oscillator a quarter of a cycle ahead, so the two do not simply mirror each other: the right ear's read point follows the left's <Claim id="two-sides">a quarter of a cycle, 0.31 s, behind</Claim>, and when one copy is furthest off pitch the other is in tune. At Spread 0 both sides get <Claim id="spread-zero">the same samples</Claim>. On white noise, at 35 % and above, the delayed halves of the two sides <Claim id="spread-apart">share nothing</Claim>: their correlation is under 0.02.

## What goes wrong

**The read point cannot reach zero.** The first notch stands at one over twice the delay, so it could only leave the top of the band if the delay went to nothing. It cannot. The interpolator needs a neighbour on each side, and both devices clamp every read to two samples. With the Flanger at 0.5 ms and full depth the read point stops at <Claim id="shortest-read">0.042 ms</Claim>, where the first notch is at 12 kHz.

**A jump in the delay is a click.** The square, the ramps and Random all jump. Moving a read point at once from one place in the line to another splices two unrelated bits of sound together. So the Flanger passes its oscillator through a one-pole lag of 3 ms (<Src path="cpp/devices/flanger/flanger.h">`kLfoLagSeconds`</Src>) before it touches the read point: a full jump of the square takes <Claim id="lag">3 ms to cover 63 %</Claim> of the way, and becomes a very quick glide. The Delay and Depth controls of both devices glide over 50 ms (`kGlideSeconds`) for the same reason, where <Src path="cpp/kit/device.h">every other control</Src> takes 5. A turn of the knob bends the pitch for a moment and does not zip.

**Feedback near 1 rings without end.** The range stops at 95 %, where the loop's own gain is already 26 dB. What is fed back passes a limiter (<Src path="cpp/devices/flanger/flanger.h">`loop_limit`</Src>, the same in both devices) that is exactly linear up to full scale and bends over above it, levelling off at twice full scale, so a resonance driven hard saturates and does not run away.

**Reading between samples dulls the copy.** A voice whose read point lies between samples is an interpolation, and interpolation loses a little at the top of the band (lesson 10). On white noise a moving voice of the Chorus comes out <Claim id="between-samples">0.9 dB quieter</Claim> than it went in, and one held on a whole sample loses nothing. It is also why the comb's highest notches are a little shallower than the formula says.

**A chorus held still is a comb.** With no depth, or with the rate so low that nothing seems to move, the Chorus is the filter of stage 1 with its teeth 83 Hz apart, and three coincident voices make it lopsided towards the delayed side. What makes it a chorus is the motion.
