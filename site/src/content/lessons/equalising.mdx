<Part id="why" title="A fader turns the whole sound down. The trouble is seldom the whole sound.">

Three instruments play together in the loop this page opens on: a drum kit, a bass and a strummed guitar. Each is fine by itself. Together they crowd one another, because they do not keep to separate parts of the range. The kick drum and the bass both live at the bottom. The body of the snare, the upper notes of the bass and the low strings of the guitar all sit in the low middle.

A wave drawn against time shows none of this. The picture that does is a spectrum. It sorts the sound by frequency: how much of it is low, how much is high, and how much lies between.

Listen to it first. <Hear set="mix" label="Hear the mix as it is" />

Measured over the loop, <Claim id="mix-tilt">the band from 40 to 100 Hz holds 17 dB more than the band from 2 to 5 kHz, and the band from 200 to 500 Hz holds 13 dB more</Claim>. A sound does not have one loudness. It has one for every part of its range.

Now suppose your ear says the middle of this mix is thick. Nothing is too loud as a whole. One region is. So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you turn down one part of a sound and leave the rest of it alone?

### The obvious answers fail

The first tool to hand is the fader. A fader multiplies every sample by one number. Every frequency in the sound is made of those same samples, so they all move together. Turn the mix down until the low middle is right, and the kick, the bass and the cymbals have come down by exactly as much. The balance between the regions is what it was.

The second tool is older than the mixing desk: a cut. A cut removes everything on one side of a frequency. It was the first tone control, because it is the cheapest filter to make. The engine's equaliser has one at each end, and its Low cut goes as high as <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/device.json" at={'"max": 500,'}>500 Hz</Src>. Here it is at the top of its range, on the mix.

It worked on the low middle. <Claim id="cut-takes-all">The band from 200 to 500 Hz comes out 11 dB lower. The band from 40 to 100 Hz comes out 64 dB lower</Claim>, which is to say that the bottom of the kick and of the bass is no longer in the mix. A cut cannot reach into the middle of a sound. It can only take an end off.

### Why it is called an equaliser

The name comes from the telephone. A long line loses more of the high frequencies than of the low ones, so a voice arrived dull at the far end. Engineers added a circuit at the end of the line that turned the highs back up by as much as the line had turned them down, until every frequency arrived at an equal level again. They called that equalising the line. The same was soon done for film sound, for records and for tape. Each of those bends the balance of a recording in a way that can be measured, and so can be undone.

Then the tool changed hands. People at mixing desks found that a circuit made to put a balance right could just as well change it on purpose. The first tone controls came as a pair, one for the bass and one for the treble. Each is what is now called a shelf. It raises or lowers everything on one side of a frequency by the same amount, as if that end of the spectrum stood on a shelf that can be moved up and down.

Two shelves and two cuts still cannot reach the middle. The tool that can is the bell. A bell turns one region up or down, most at its centre and less and less to either side, and leaves the far ends alone. It is named for the shape of its curve.

Measured on the mix, <Claim id="bell-takes-part">Body at −8 dB brings the band from 200 to 500 Hz down 6.9 dB, the band from 40 to 100 Hz down 0.8 dB and the band from 2 to 5 kHz down 0.3 dB</Claim>. The kick and the bass are still there.

Compare it with the two before. <Hear set="dip" label="Hear one band turned down" />

Every control of an equaliser answers one of three questions about a band.

- **Where?** The frequency at the centre of a bell, or at the corner of a shelf or a cut.
- **How much?** The gain in decibels, up or down. A cut has no gain: it takes everything.
- **How wide?** How far to either side a bell reaches. The usual measure of it is called Q, and part 2 shows what Q counts.

An equaliser that leaves all three free for every band is called parametric. The engine's own is simpler on purpose. It has four bands at fixed places with fixed widths, each with one control for how much, and a cut at each end. Its filters are the general ones all the same. This lesson also has one bell with all three controls free, to take apart.

About 1 dB. Measured with steady tones, <Claim id="bell-reach">Body at −8 dB takes 1.1 dB off a tone at 80 Hz and 8.00 dB off one at 320 Hz</Claim>.

A bell has no edges. It is deepest at its centre and fades away to either side without ever quite stopping, so the frequency printed beside a band is where it is strongest and not all that it touches. The engine's two bells are broad ones. Each moves a whole region of an instrument, which is what a tone control is for.

Here is a sentence to check against the picture. Leave the three controls where the lesson starts them, find 1 kHz on the bottom edge and go up. The bell is <Claim id="bell-centre">+6.00 dB at 1 kHz</Claim>, which is what Gain says. Now find the two places where the line is half that many decibels up. They are at <Claim id="bell-width">781.2 Hz and 1,279.6 Hz</Claim>, and the distance between them is the bell's width. Q is the centre frequency divided by that width, which here comes to 2. Double the Q and the bell is half as wide.

The bottom edge is spaced the way a keyboard is: each doubling of the frequency, an octave, takes the same distance wherever it falls. On that scale a bell is the same shape on both sides, and its reach is best counted in octaves. <Claim id="bell-two-octaves">Two octaves either side of its centre, at 250 Hz and 4 kHz, this bell still adds 0.11 dB</Claim>.

Notice what the picture leaves out. It says what happens to a steady tone, and music is not steady: there is no time in the picture. Nor does it say what the sound holds. A boost at a frequency the sound does not have boosts nothing. Both come back. Time is stage 7 of part 5, and the sound itself is in every card of the next part.

</Part>

<Part id="build" title="Start with one small filter that does nothing. Give it numbers. Use it again.">

An equaliser is not one machine. It is one very small filter used several times in a row, each copy with different numbers in it. This part starts with that filter doing nothing and builds the engine's whole chain out of it, a piece at a time. After each piece the lab sends one sample through what has been built so far and measures the curve again, and the listing shows the lines the piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these curves and plays "Hear it so far".

The small filter is called a biquad. Part 5 says where the name comes from and why it works. Here it is enough to use one.

    A bell comes back to 0 dB on both sides. A shelf, the tone control of part 1, does not: one side of its corner stays moved. It is the same biquad again with a third recipe, `shelf`. <StageBlock tap="bell" n={1} title="One biquad: three inputs, two memories">
  A fader has no memory. Each sample out depends on one sample in, and that is why it cannot tell a low note from a high one: frequency is a matter of how a wave changes from sample to sample, and a fader looks at one sample at a time. A filter has to remember. The biquad remembers as little as will do: the last two samples that went in, and the last two that came out.

  The picture shows that memory at work. One sample goes into the bell, alone in silence, and the picture leaves that sample out and draws everything else that comes out. With no memory there would be nothing to draw.

  <Controls ids="bellHz bellGain bellQ" />

  <Curve taps="bellRing" stems xMax={2} xTitle="milliseconds" yTitle="sample value">
    What the bell adds to one sample: the first two milliseconds of it, a stem for each sample.
    Notice that the stems swing above and below nothing at the bell's own frequency and shrink as
    they go. Try Frequency higher, and notice the swings crowd together.
  </Curve>

  That is the whole trick, seen from the side. The filter answers every sample with a short, fading note of its own. When the sound holds the same note, the answers pile up in step with it and that frequency comes out louder. Any other frequency finds answers that are out of step with one another and mostly cancel.

  The arithmetic that does it, with <M t="x[n]" /> the sample going in now and <M t="y[n]" /> the sample coming out:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} \sig{out}{y[n]} = {} & b_0\,\sig{in}{x[n]} + b_1\,\sig{in}{x[n-1]} + b_2\,\sig{in}{x[n-2]} \\ & - a_1\,\sig{out}{y[n-1]} - a_2\,\sig{out}{y[n-2]} \end{aligned}`}
  />

  Read it from the left. The first line is the input's part and the second the filter's own. The output is the input now, times <M t="b_0" />. To that is added the input of one sample ago times <M t="b_1" />, and of two samples ago times <M t="b_2" />. Then the filter's own last output is taken away, times <M t="a_1" />, and the one before it, times <M t="a_2" />. Those last two terms are the feedback. They are why the stems in the picture go on after the input has stopped: each output is partly made of the outputs before it. Two samples back is as far as this filter reaches, which makes it a filter of the second order. Stage 2 says where the name biquad comes from.

  The kit does not keep four old samples. It keeps two running sums, <M t="z_1" /> and <M t="z_2" />, each holding the part of the next outputs that is already known:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} \at{biquad-y}{y} &= b_0\,x + z_1 \\ \at{biquad-z1}{z_1} &\leftarrow b_1\,x - a_1\,y + z_2 \\ \at{biquad-z2}{z_2} &\leftarrow b_2\,x - a_2\,y \end{aligned}`}
  />

  The first line makes the output from the input and what was kept. The second makes ready what the next sample will need, and takes into it what the third line left the time before. The third makes ready what the sample after that will need. It is the long equation with its terms gathered up early, so that two numbers are kept in place of four. The lab runs the long equation beside the kit's three lines on your loop and reads the difference:

A bell placed lower has more of it: <Claim id="forms-low">with Frequency at 100 Hz the same reading is 82 dB under the sound</Claim>. The fault about rounding in part 7 says why.

<StageBlock tap="bellResponse" n={2} title="Zeros and poles: where the curve comes from">
  Five numbers go in and a curve comes out. This stage is the step between, and it is a picture of four points.

  Start with a way to draw a frequency. A steady tone goes round and round, and a circle is the natural place to draw it. Put 0 Hz at the right of a circle and half the sample rate, the highest frequency the samples can hold, at the left, and let every frequency between have its place along the top half. The angle from the right is

  <M block t={String.raw`\omega = 2\pi\,\frac{f}{f_s}`} />

  with <M t="f" /> the frequency and <M t="f_s" /> the sample rate. It is how far round a tone of that frequency goes in one sample: a small step for a low note, half a turn for the highest.

  On the same picture every biquad owns four points. Two are called zeros and two are called poles. The level the filter gives a frequency is then a matter of distances. Stand on the circle at that frequency. Measure the distance to each zero and multiply the two. Measure the distance to each pole and multiply the two. Divide the first product by the second.

  <PolesAndZeros roots="bellRoots" response="bellResponse">
    First the part of the circle where the bell's zeros and poles stand, with a small map of the
    whole circle in its corner. Then the bell's measured curve. Try Frequency looked at, and notice
    the point run along the circle while its mark runs along the curve: where the point is nearer
    a pole than a zero the curve is above 0 dB. Then try Gain below 0 dB, and notice the zeros and
    the poles change places.
  </PolesAndZeros>

  As a formula, with <M t="d_1" /> and <M t="d_2" /> the two distances to the zeros and <M t="e_1" /> and <M t="e_2" /> the two to the poles:

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{|H(f)|} = b_0\,\frac{\sig{alt}{d_1\,d_2}}{\sig{alt2}{e_1\,e_2}}`} />

  <M t="|H(f)|" /> is how many times larger a tone at <M t="f" /> comes out. Read the fraction against the picture. A zero pulls the curve down as the point on the circle comes near it, because a small distance on top makes a small result. A zero that stood on the circle itself would silence its frequency altogether. A pole pushes the curve up as the point comes near, because a small distance underneath makes a large result. A bell puts a pole and a zero close together, at the same angle and at different distances from the centre. Far away round the circle the two distances are nearly equal and cancel: 0 dB. Near their angle one of them wins. In a boost the pole is the nearer to the circle. In a cut the zero is.

  For the bell this page starts with, <Claim id="pole-radius">+6 dB at 1 kHz with a Q of 2, the poles stand 0.977 of the way from the centre to the circle and the zeros 0.955</Claim>.

  <Check id="poleRadius" />
  <Check id="zeroRadius" />

  The distances are not a manner of speaking. The lab works the curve out from the four points alone and lays it against the curve it measured through the kit's filter, and they agree <Claim id="roots-make-curve">to less than 0.001 dB</Claim> at every setting its test tries.

  Where do the four points come from? From the equation of stage 1. For a steady tone, a delay of one sample is a turn back by the angle <M t={String.raw`\omega`} />. Write <M t="z" /> for the tone's place on the circle and <M t="z^{-1}" /> for one sample of delay. Then the five numbers make a fraction, with the input's three on top and the output's two underneath:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} H(z) &= \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}} \\[6pt] &= b_0\,\frac{(z - \sig{alt}{q_1})(z - \sig{alt}{q_2})}{(z - \sig{alt2}{p_1})(z - \sig{alt2}{p_2})} \end{aligned}`}
  />

  The zeros <M t="q_1" /> and <M t="q_2" /> are the two places where the top is nothing. The poles <M t="p_1" /> and <M t="p_2" /> are the two places where the bottom is. Each bracket of the second line is a distance from <M t="z" /> to one of them, which is the distance formula above. The top and the bottom are each a quadratic, an expression that reaches to the second power. Two quadratics, one over the other, is the biquad's full name: biquadratic.

  Since the top and the bottom multiply, their decibels add, and the bell can be taken apart into the half its zeros make and the half its poles make.

    taps="bellResponse:out bellZeros:alt bellPoles:alt2"
    xLog
    xTitle="hertz"
    yTitle="decibels"
    min={-60}
    max={60}
  >
    The bell, the curve of its zeros alone and the curve of its poles alone. With the bell where
    the page starts it, the zeros' curve is the one far under the level line at the left edge and
    the poles' the one far over it. Notice that the two halves are huge and nearly mirror each
    other, and that the bell is the small difference where they fail to cancel.
  </Curve>

  At 20 Hz <Claim id="halves-add">the zeros alone are 35.5 dB down and the poles alone 35.5 dB up, and everywhere the two add to the bell within 0.001 dB</Claim>. This is worth holding on to. A gentle curve can be the difference between two violent ones, and a filter made that way asks a lot of the arithmetic. Part 7 shows where that is felt.

  One rule follows from the picture. A pole must stay inside the circle. The distance of a pole from the centre is how much of the filter's fading note is left after each sample, and at 1 or more the note would never fade. It is the pole of <Lesson id="one-pole" />, the number a filter's memory is multiplied by at every sample, now one of a pair that turns as it shrinks. Stage 7 comes back to this.

  Step 2 of the build took the bell's five numbers from a recipe. The recipe is short enough to read whole.

  The recipe first works out three helpers from what was asked for:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\at{prepare-w}{\omega = 2\pi\,\frac{f_0}{f_s}} \qquad \at{prepare-alpha}{\alpha = \frac{\sin\omega}{2Q}} \qquad \at{peak-amp}{A = 10^{\,g/40}}`}
  />

  <M t={String.raw`\omega`} /> is the centre frequency <M t="f_0" /> as an angle on the circle of stage 2. <M t={String.raw`\alpha`} /> carries the width: a larger Q makes it smaller. <M t="A" /> carries the gain <M t="g" />, which is in decibels. Dividing by 40 where 20 would be usual makes <M t="A" /> the square root of the gain asked for, and the reason is in the six numbers the recipe gives, the input's three on the left and the output's three on the right:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} b_0 &= 1 + \alpha A & a_0 &= 1 + \alpha / A \\ b_1 &= -2\cos\omega & a_1 &= -2\cos\omega \\ b_2 &= 1 - \alpha A & a_2 &= 1 - \alpha / A \end{aligned}`}
  />

  Read the left column against the right. They are the same but for one thing: where the left has <M t={String.raw`\alpha A`} />, the right has <M t={String.raw`\alpha / A`} />. The middle row matches, and it sets the angle, so the zeros and the poles stand at the same frequency. The outer rows set the distance from the centre. With no gain, <M t="A" /> is 1, the two columns are the same, and the filter is a wire. In a boost <M t="A" /> is more than 1: it pushes the zeros in towards the centre and lets the poles out towards the circle. Half the gain comes from each, which is why <M t="A" /> is the square root. At the centre frequency itself the fraction of stage 2 comes to exactly <M t="A^2" />, whatever the Q, and that is why the bell of part 2 measured +6.00 dB at its centre.

  Turn the gain over and <M t="A" /> becomes one over what it was. The left column becomes the right and the right the left, which is to say that the zeros and the poles change places. So a cut is the same filter as a boost, upside down, and one after the other they are a wire: measured, <Claim id="bell-mirror">the curve of a boost and the curve of the same cut add to 0 dB within 0.001 dB everywhere</Claim>.

  That is the low shelf, from the bottom of the range to the top. At 0 Hz it gives the whole gain.   The six numbers that keep the promise are longer than the bell's:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} \at{lowshelf-beta}{\beta} &= 2\sqrt{A}\,\alpha \\ b_0 &= A\,[(A+1) - (A-1)\cos\omega + \beta] \\ b_1 &= 2A\,[(A-1) - (A+1)\cos\omega] \\ b_2 &= A\,[(A+1) - (A-1)\cos\omega - \beta] \\ a_0 &= (A+1) + (A-1)\cos\omega + \beta \\ a_1 &= -2\,[(A-1) + (A+1)\cos\omega] \\ a_2 &= (A+1) + (A-1)\cos\omega - \beta \end{aligned}`}

<StageBlock tap="bellPhase" n={7} title="What the curve leaves out: time">
  The curve says how loud each frequency comes out. It says nothing about when. A filter with memory holds each frequency back a little, or lets it run a little ahead, and by a different amount at each frequency. That is called phase, and it is measured in degrees of a cycle.

  <Controls ids="bellHz bellGain bellQ" />

  <Curve taps="bellPhase" xLog xTitle="hertz" yTitle="degrees" min={-40} max={40}>
    How far the one bell turns each frequency, in degrees of a cycle. Notice that the line crosses
    nothing at the bell's centre, and is furthest from it on either side. Try Gain at 0 dB, and
    notice it go level: a bell that changes no level changes no timing.
  </Curve>

  This too comes off the picture of stage 2. Level was distances. Phase is directions: take the direction of the line from each zero, and from each pole, to the point on the circle.

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\varphi(f) = \bigl(\sig{alt}{\theta_1 + \theta_2}\bigr) - \bigl(\sig{alt2}{\psi_1 + \psi_2}\bigr)`}
  />

  Here <M t={String.raw`\theta_1`} /> and <M t={String.raw`\theta_2`} /> are the directions of the lines from the two zeros to the point, and <M t={String.raw`\psi_1`} /> and <M t={String.raw`\psi_2`} /> the directions of the lines from the two poles. The turn is the zeros' two directions added, less the poles' two added. Far from the bell, a zero and its pole are seen in nearly the same direction, and they cancel. At the bell's centre they are seen in exactly the same direction, and cancel again. In between, to either side of the centre, the directions differ most. Measured, the bell this page starts with turns a tone <Claim id="bell-phase">19.4 degrees forward at 0.78 kHz and 19.4 degrees back at 1.28 kHz, which are the two frequencies of part 2 where it is half its decibels up. At +12 dB the most it turns is 36.8 degrees</Claim>.

  By itself that does little: a steady tone turned by part of a cycle is the same tone, begun a moment sooner or later. It matters when the equalised sound is mixed with a copy that was not equalised, because then the two no longer line up, and their sum has dips that neither has alone. An effect is made of exactly that in <Lesson id="phasing" />.

  The other thing the curve leaves out is ringing, and part 7 has a card to hear it by. Stage 1 showed the fading note a bell adds to every sample. How long the note lasts is set by one number: how far the poles are from the centre of the circle.

  <Curve taps="bellRing" xMax={20} xTitle="milliseconds" yTitle="sample value">
    What the bell adds to one sample, over twenty milliseconds. Try Q at its highest and Gain at
    its highest, and notice the note last many times longer. Then take Gain to its lowest at the
    same Q, and notice it die away much sooner.
  </Curve>

  <M block t={String.raw`\tau = \frac{-1}{f_s\,\ln r}`} />

  <M t="r" /> is the distance of the poles from the centre, which is what is left of the note after each sample. <M t={String.raw`\ln r`} /> is its natural logarithm, a negative number that is close to nothing when <M t="r" /> is close to 1. <M t={String.raw`\tau`} /> is the time, in seconds, for the note to fall to 37 % of its size. The nearer the poles are to the circle, the longer the bell rings.

  Now recall the recipe of stage 3. The poles' distance comes from <M t={String.raw`\alpha / A`} />: a high Q makes <M t={String.raw`\alpha`} /> small, and a boost makes <M t="A" /> large. Both push the poles out. A cut does the opposite, because in a cut it is the zeros that go out, and zeros do not ring. So, by that measure, <Claim id="ring-boost">a +12 dB bell at 1 kHz with a Q of 10 rings for 6.4 ms</Claim>, and the cut of the same size and width for <Claim id="ring-cut">1.6 ms</Claim>. A narrow boost adds a note to the sound. A narrow cut hardly does. Part 6 makes a rule of that and part 7 lets you hear it.
</StageBlock>

<StageBlock tap="bandPower" n={8} title="Clear listens">
  The six bands do what they are told. The last part of the device decides for itself. A recording often has a few notes that ring on over everything else: a boomy note of the room, a string that answers every chord, a drum that sings. A person fixes that by hunting for the frequency and setting a narrow cut on it. Clear does the hunting and the cutting, and keeps doing it as the sound changes.

  It listens to a copy of the sound taken after the six bands. Every <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr int kHop = 1024;">1,024 samples</Src> it takes the last <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr int kFrame = 4096;">4,096</Src> and finds how much of each frequency they hold. That is the transform of <Lesson id="short-time-fourier" />. Then it adds the result up into <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr int kBands = 23;">23 bands</Src>, each <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="23 bands: four half an octave wide from 45 to 180 Hz, then thirds of an">a third of an octave wide, or half an octave at the bottom</Src>, and smooths each band's power over <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr float kPowerSeconds = 0.3f;">0.3 s</Src>, so that a single drum hit does not count as a note that rings on.

  <Curve taps="bandPower bandMargin" xLog xTitle="hertz" yTitle="decibels">
    What Clear hears in each band at the moment of the loop when one band stood furthest over
    its line, and the line a band has to rise above before any of it counts. Notice any band where the first line stands over the second: how far
    over is its excess. Try Low from end to end, and notice the left of the first line move against
    the second, because Clear listens after the six bands.
  </Curve>

  The line a band must rise above is made from its neighbours. For each band, Clear takes the mean power of <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="Each band is compared with the mean power of the bands two to four away">the bands two to four away on either side</Src>. The bands right beside it are left out, because a ringing note spills into them. Then:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\text{excess}_k = \at{listen-excess}{10\log_{10}\frac{P_k}{R_k} - 6}`}
  />

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} \text{cut wanted}_k = {} & \at{listen-wanted}{-\,\text{Clear}\times\text{bed}\times\text{around}_k} \\ & \times \min(\text{excess}_k,\ 12) \end{aligned}`}
  />

  In the first of these, <M t="P_k" /> is the power in band <M t="k" /> and <M t="R_k" /> the mean power of its neighbours. Ten times the logarithm of one over the other is how many decibels the band stands above them. The first <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr float kMarginDb = 6.0f;">6 dB</Src> are let go: a band has to stand well clear before it is called a resonance. What is left is the excess, and no more than <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr float kMaxExcessDb = 12.0f;">12 dB</Src> of it counts.

  The second is the cut wanted: that excess times three numbers between 0 and 1. The first is the Clear control. The other two keep Clear from mistaking music for a fault. A single note also stands far above the bands around it, and cutting it would be cutting the tune. So the bed says how much sound there is in all: it counts the bands <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="many bands lie within 25 dB of the loudest, nothing up to three and all of">within 25 dB of the loudest</Src>, and is 0 up to <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr int kLoneBands = 3;">three</Src> and 1 from <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr float kBedBands = 5.0f;">five more</Src>. The last number is the share of the band's own surroundings that hold sound, below it and above it, the smaller of the two. That has to have lasted <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr float kStaySeconds = 0.3f;">0.3 s</Src> before it counts. A resonance stands out of sound that lies all round it. A melody note stands alone.

  <Listing
    slot="listing"
    path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h"
    from="float target = 0.0f;"
    to="gain_[k].set_target(cut);"
    mark="target = -depth * around * kit::min(excess, kMaxExcessDb);"
    name="listen"
    at={{
      excess: 'const float excess = 10.0f * std::log10(power_[k] / reference) - kMarginDb;',
      wanted: 'target = -depth * around * kit::min(excess, kMaxExcessDb);',
      glide: 'cut = target + (cut - target) * (target < cut ? down : back);',
    }}
  />
</StageBlock>

  How deep can it go? On a steady tone set in a bed of noise, standing far above it, Clear at its highest <Claim id="clear-cap">cuts 12 dB and no further</Claim>, which is the cap of stage 8. With Clear at 0.4, where the device itself starts, it cuts <Claim id="clear-share">4.8 dB</Claim>, which is 0.4 of that. On the drum loop at this page's starting settings, <Claim id="clear-on-drums">Clear at its highest finds 1.1 dB to cut, near 107 Hz</Claim>, where a drum rings.

  A cut does not jump to what is wanted. Each time Clear listens, the cut moves a fixed share of the way there, which is the smoothing of <Lesson id="one-pole" /> run once a listen:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{ctl}{c} \leftarrow \at{listen-glide}{t + (\sig{ctl}{c} - t)\,k} \qquad\quad k = e^{-1/(T f)}`}
  />

  <M t="c" /> is the cut in force and <M t="t" /> the cut wanted, both in decibels. <M t="k" /> is the share of the remaining distance that is kept each time, a number just under 1. <M t="f" /> is how many times a second Clear listens, and <M t="T" /> is the time to cover 63 % of the way. Going down, <M t="T" /> is Clear time. Coming back it is <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="const float back = kit::time_to_coeff(2.0f * param(kClearTime), frames_per_second);">twice Clear time</Src>, so a cut arrives sooner than it leaves and does not flutter.

  <Curve taps="clearGlide" xTitle="seconds" yTitle="decibels" min={-13} max={3}>
    One cut by that rule: 12 dB wanted from the start, then nothing wanted from half way along.
    Notice that it goes down faster than it comes back. Try Clear time, and notice both halves
    stretch together.
  </Curve>

  By that rule alone, <Claim id="clear-glide">with Clear time at 0.2 s a cut asked for 12 dB at once is 63 % of the way down after 0.2 s, and takes 0.4 s to come 63 % of the way back</Claim>. In the device there is more to wait for, because stage 8 smooths what it hears and waits for the surroundings to last. When a tone and a bed of noise start together out of silence, <Claim id="clear-onset">with Clear time at 0.2 s the cut first moves 0.3 s after they start and is 63 % of the way down after 0.5 s; with Clear time at 1.5 s, after 1.7 s</Claim>. Clear is slow on purpose. It is for what rings on, and a thing has to last before it can be said to ring on.

  Between listens the cut is not held still and then stepped. Its gain is ramped, and each bell's recipe is worked out again <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" at="static constexpr int kClearPeriod = 64;">every 64 samples</Src>, for the reason stage 6 gave: a filter that jumps clicks. <Part id="ways" title="Six things people do with these controls.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the curves are doing. Two of the cards use the one bell of part 2 in place of the device, because the device's own bells have a fixed place and a fixed width.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Cut before you boost" tag="one band down, not three up" set="cutfirst">
    When a sound is dull, the first thought is to turn the top up. Often the same balance can be had by turning down what there is too much of, and the two are not equal in what they cost. A boost adds level, and there is a ceiling to level. A cut gives level back. On the mix, <Claim id="way-cut">Low and Air at +5 dB bring the loudest sample 2.9 dB nearer full scale, and Body at −5 dB takes it 1.2 dB further off</Claim>. Both settings make the low middle smaller beside the two ends. After the card, turn Body back to 0 dB, bring Low and Air up in its place, and compare.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Clear the bottom" tag="Low cut, raised until it is missed" set="bottom">
    Most parts of a mix carry something under their lowest note that nobody plays: a thump from a hand, a rumble from the room. One part's worth does no harm. A dozen parts' worth is a blur under the bass. On the strummed guitar, <Claim id="way-bottom">with the Low cut at 100 Hz what lies from 20 to 60 Hz comes out 14.8 dB lower and the loop 0.2 dB quieter</Claim>: almost nothing of the guitar has gone. After the card, raise Low cut until the guitar begins to sound thin, then come back a little.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Find it with a boost, then turn it over" tag="a narrow bell, swept, then cut" set="find">
    An ugly resonance is hard to place by ear and easy to find by making it worse. The card sets the one bell narrow and tall. After it, move Frequency slowly along and listen for the place where the guitar suddenly honks or booms. That is the frequency. Now take Gain below 0 dB, and widen the bell with Q until the cut stops sounding like a hole. The loud search is only a search: stage 7 of part 5 says why a bell that narrow and that tall is not left in a mix.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Broad for tone, narrow for trouble" tag="a high Q, a deep cut" set="narrow">
    A broad bell changes the character of a sound. A narrow one takes out a fault and leaves the character alone. Here the one bell sits where Body does, much narrower, and cuts deeper than Body did in part 1. <Claim id="way-narrow">A bell at 320 Hz, −9 dB and Q 4 brings 200 to 500 Hz of the mix down 2.5 dB and 40 to 100 Hz down 0.0 dB</Claim>. In part 1 the broad bell, cutting less at its centre, took far more out of the same band. After the card, lower Q and listen for the moment the mix as a whole starts to change.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Push a layer back" tag="less top, less bite" set="pad">
    Turning a part down makes it quieter, and it stays in the front row. The card is the pad setting of part 3, used as a habit: for a part that stands too far forward, take off its top and its presence before its level. After it, bring Presence back up to 0 dB and listen to the pad step forward without getting louder at the bottom.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Let Clear watch a held sound" tag="Clear up, on a bed of sound" set="ride">
    A pad or a long reverb builds up at whatever frequencies it favours, and they change with the chord, so no fixed cut is right for long. With the six bands flat and Clear all the way up, on the pad, <Claim id="way-clear">Clear finds 1.5 dB to cut, in one band between 200 and 300 Hz</Claim>. It is a small move and it is meant to be. After the card, watch the pictures of stage 9 while the loop plays, then lengthen Clear time and see the cut settle more slowly.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Faults>
  <Fault title="A narrow boost rings" set="ring">
    The card sends single clicks through the one bell, set narrow and tall. Each click comes out with a short note stuck to it, at the bell's frequency. That note is the fading answer of stage 1, lasting as long as stage 7 measured. On music, listen for a whistle or a honk that follows every hit, or for one note of a chord that will not stop. Use a broader bell, or less gain, or the cut of way 3 in place of the boost.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A boost is gain, and gain runs out" set="over">
    Turn your volume down before you play this one. Every boost raises the level of the sound that passes it, and nothing in an equaliser stops that at full scale. <Claim id="fault-over">Low and Air at +12 dB put the loudest sample of the mix 3.6 dB over full scale, where it went in 4.4 dB under</Claim>. Whatever comes next will clip it, and this page's player is next: <Src path="site/src/lab/player.ts" at="export const volumeGain = (volume: number) => 0.5 * volume * volume">with its volume all the way up it plays at half gain</Src>, so a sound past twice full scale clips in your ear and not in the pictures. No card here reaches that, and the controls can: <Claim id="loud-range">all four bands at +12 dB put the loudest sample of the drums at 2.1 times full scale</Claim>. After a boost, turn the sound down by about as much, or do the job with cuts.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Too much cut away" set="thin">
    Each cut seems to clean the sound up, so it is easy to keep going. This card is the device's own preset called <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/device.json" at={'"Thin": {'}>Thin</Src>, put on the whole mix, where it does not belong. <Claim id="fault-thin">The band from 40 to 100 Hz comes out 44 dB lower and the loop 7.3 dB quieter</Claim>. Play it, and listen for a small radio. On one part in a full mix the same setting can be right. The fault is to judge a cut with nothing playing beside it.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A bell near the top is squeezed">
    The circle of stage 2 ends at half the sample rate. A filter has no way to draw a curve beyond it, and a recipe that aims a bell near the top has to fit the bell's upper side into the room that is left. The picture over these cards shows it.

    <Claim id="cramp">Half an octave over its centre, a +6 dB bell of Q 2 holds 2.02 dB at 1 kHz and 0.62 dB at 12 kHz. An octave over, it holds 0.63 dB and exactly nothing</Claim>. The device's Presence is far enough down to show it only a little: <Claim id="presence-lopsided">at +6 it is 2.81 dB at 1.5 kHz and 2.67 dB at 6 kHz, lopsided by 0.14 dB</Claim>. It is also why the High cut of stage 5 fell faster than its formula. A device that needs true shapes at the top has to run its filters at a higher sample rate, as the Auto Filter of <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" /> does. This one does not, and its Presence and Air are a little squeezed on the side towards the top.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Rounding leaves a floor">
    Stage 2 showed a gentle bell as the small difference of two violent halves. For a band placed low, the halves are at their most violent: the filter's memories hold large numbers that all but cancel, and a sample has only so many digits. What is lost in the cancelling stays behind as a faint error. The lab's curves are made with one sample, which holds every frequency at once. The error is the rounding of that whole sample, and far down a cut it is larger than the little that is left. <Claim id="rounding-floor">With the Low cut at 500 Hz and the four bands where this page starts them, the device measures −70 dB at 44 Hz that way, where its bands add to −81 dB, and a steady tone there comes out at −81 dB</Claim>. So the curve is right and the one-sample measurement is not: under a certain depth it is measuring its own rounding. The floor is far below the sound that makes it. It is the reason stage 6 compared its two curves only where they are above a certain depth.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A filter that jumps">
    Stage 6 said why a filter whose numbers change all at once clicks. A quick sweep of such changes is the zipper noise of <Lesson id="one-pole" />. The device glides every value and fades each cut in and out of circuit, as stages 5 and 6 showed. If you hear a click when a setting changes, it is the sound meeting a filter that had no time to glide: a preset recalled in the middle of a held note is the usual case.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Equalising a part alone">
    A part played alone invites you to make it full: more bottom, more top, more of everything. Every part made full that way wants the same room, and the mix is worse for it. The strummed guitar of part 3 was set the other way, for what it leaves free. Set an equaliser with the other parts playing. Listen to the part alone only to find a fault.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Seven things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="The band marked 320 Hz changes 320 Hz.">
    It changes a region with 320 Hz at its centre. The answer to the question in part 1 has the number for a note two octaves away, and stage 3 has it for one octave.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A boost of 6 dB makes the sound 6 dB louder.">
    It makes one region up to 6 dB louder, and the sound gains only as much as it holds there. On the mix, <Claim id="boost-adds">Presence at +6 dB makes the mix 0.2 dB louder over the loop, and Body at +6 dB makes it 2.4 dB louder</Claim>, because this mix has much more in its low middle than higher up. An equaliser's curve is a statement about the equaliser. What you hear is the curve laid over the sound.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Cutting is a cleaner process than boosting.">
    It is the same filter. Stage 3 showed that a cut is a boost with its top and its bottom exchanged, and measured the two undoing each other. The advice to cut first is good for other reasons, level and ringing, which way 1 of part 6 and stage 7 measured.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="An equaliser only changes levels, so the timing is untouched.">
    Every band that changes a level also turns the phase of the frequencies round it, as stage 7 measured. Alone, that changes no level. Mixed with an unequalised copy of the same sound, it changes how the two add.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Even set flat, an equaliser colours the sound.">
    One that left its cuts in circuit would: step 5 of the build measured what a cut at the very end of its range still takes off the lowest notes. This one takes a band out of circuit when it has nothing to do, and with everything flat its output is its input to the last digit, as stage 6 showed. If a flat setting here sounds different, the difference is in the listening.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The narrower the band, the more exact the tool.">
    Narrower in frequency is longer in time. Stage 7 measured a narrow boost ringing for several cycles of its own note. A filter cannot be told to act on one frequency for one instant: the more closely it picks a frequency, the longer it has to listen and the longer it has to answer.
  </Myth>

    Loudness is not what it measures. It turns down a band that stands far above its own neighbours, in a sound that fills many bands, and only after that has lasted. Stage 9 showed it leaving a lone note alone at any level. For turning down what is loud there is the compressor of <Lesson id="compression" />.
