A sound in a computer is a list of samples: a height for each instant. The Fourier transform writes the same stretch of sound as another list: how much of each frequency it holds. Neither list says more than the other. N samples go in, N numbers come out, and the inverse transform turns those numbers back into the samples it was given.

It rests on a fact that is easy to state and hard to believe. Any N samples whatever, a drum hit, a chord, a burst of noise, are the sum of sinusoids at N/2 + 1 fixed frequencies, each with its own size and its own starting angle. The transform is the recipe that finds the sizes and the angles. Each of the fixed frequencies is called a *bin*.

A loop is long and what it holds keeps changing, so the transform is never run on all of it at once. It is run on a *frame*, a short run of samples cut from the loop. These are the stages one frame goes through, and at the end the whole loop taken frame by frame. Press Play, then Listen on the first and the last.

## Stage by stage

### 1. Cut a frame

The transform needs a fixed number of samples, N. The kit's is a template, `kit::Fft<N>`, and N must be a power of two (a `static_assert` at the top of the class refuses anything else; stage 7 shows why). The lab has four sizes ready: 64, 256, 1,024 and 4,096 samples, which at 48 kHz are 1.3, 5.3, 21 and 85 milliseconds of sound.

A frame is the samples <M t={String.raw`x[0] \dots x[N-1]`} /> starting at a place you choose. Nothing else is done to them yet.

### 2. One bin is a correlation

To ask how much of one frequency a frame holds, multiply the frame by a sinusoid of that frequency, sample by sample, and add the products up. Where the frame swings with the sinusoid, both are positive together and negative together, every product is positive, and the sum grows. Where it does not, the products come out positive and negative in equal measure and cancel. That sum is a *correlation*, and the transform is nothing but this sum, done once for each bin:

<M block t={String.raw`X[k] \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-j 2\pi k n / N}`} />

<M block t={String.raw`\phantom{X[k]} \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\cos\frac{2\pi k n}{N} \;-\; j \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\sin\frac{2\pi k n}{N}`} />

Here <M t="x[n]" /> is sample <M t="n" /> of the frame and <M t="k" /> is the bin. The sinusoid of bin <M t="k" /> turns exactly <M t="k" /> times across the frame, so bin 0 does not turn at all, bin 1 fits one cycle, and so on. <M t="X[k]" /> is a pair of numbers: the sum against the cosine (the real part) and the sum against the sine (the imaginary part, with a minus sign that is only a convention). In the code they are two arrays, `re` and `im`, and the cosines and sines are the tables `cos_` and `sin_` that `init` fills with the angle <M t={String.raw`-2\pi i/N`} />.

The lab does one of these sums in the open, at any frequency you like, not only at a bin's.

**Why a cosine and a sine.** A cosine alone can miss a sinusoid that is plainly there. If the frame's sinusoid crosses zero where the cosine peaks, their product is positive for a quarter of a cycle and negative for the next, and the sum is zero. The sine is the same wave a quarter of a cycle later, and it catches exactly what the cosine misses. Take a 750 Hz sine of amplitude 0.5 and a frame that starts as the sine crosses zero on its way down. The sums are 0 against the cosine and −0.5 against the sine. Cut the frame a quarter of a cycle later and they read <Claim id="quarter-turn">−0.5 and 0</Claim>. The two numbers trade places, and the length of the pair, <M t={String.raw`\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}`} />, stays 0.5. Against 1,500 Hz, which the sine does not hold, both sums end <Claim id="no-match">more than 120 dB down</Claim>.

### 3. Two numbers per bin: a level and an angle

The pair <M t={String.raw`(\operatorname{Re} X[k], \operatorname{Im} X[k])`} /> is a point on a plane, and a point can be given another way: how far it is from the centre and in which direction.

<M block t={String.raw`|X[k]| = \sqrt{\operatorname{Re}^2 + \operatorname{Im}^2} \qquad\qquad \varphi_k = \operatorname{atan2}(\operatorname{Im}, \operatorname{Re})`} />

The distance is the *magnitude*: how much of that frequency there is. The direction is the *phase*: where in its cycle that sinusoid stands at the frame's first sample. A picture called "the spectrum" is nearly always the magnitudes alone, in decibels.

The kit's forward transform does not divide by anything, so its numbers grow with the frame. A sine of amplitude 0.5 exactly on bin 100 of a 4,096-sample frame gives that bin a magnitude of <Claim id="scaling">1,024</Claim>, which is <M t={String.raw`\tfrac12 A N`} />, and leaves every other bin under −120 dB. The half is there because a real signal's spectrum is a mirror: bin <M t="N-k" /> always holds the same magnitude as bin <M t="k" /> with the opposite angle, so each sinusoid's size is shared between two bins. That is why every figure here stops at bin N/2, and why the lab's level reads 0 dB for a full-scale sine.

Phase is what the magnitudes leave out. Two frames of the same steady note, cut at different instants, have the same magnitudes and different phases, and the lesson on the short-time transform uses exactly that difference to measure a frequency more finely than the bins can.

Nothing is lost or gained on the way. The squares of all N bins add up to N times the squares of the N samples (Parseval's relation), and through the kit's transform the two sides agree to <Claim id="parseval">better than one part in a million</Claim> on noise at every frame length.

### 4. Bin spacing: time against frequency

Bin <M t="k" /> turns <M t="k" /> times in a frame that lasts <M t={String.raw`N/f_s`} /> seconds, so it stands at

<M block t={String.raw`f_k = k\,\frac{f_s}{N}, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N}, \qquad \Delta f \cdot T_{\text{frame}} = 1`} />

The bins are evenly spaced, <M t={String.raw`f_s/N`} /> apart, from 0 Hz to half the sample rate. At 48 kHz a frame of 64 has bins 750 Hz apart; 256 gives 187.5 Hz; 1,024 gives 46.9 Hz; 4,096 gives 11.7 Hz. The last term is the whole trade in one line: the spacing times the frame's length is always 1. To tell two frequencies 10 Hz apart you need a tenth of a second of sound, and for that tenth of a second the transform cannot say *when* anything happened.

  The same levels on a linear axis up to 1.5 kHz, where the saw's harmonics stand evenly. At 4,096 samples each is a separate peak. Step the frame length down and they melt into one slope.
</Spectrum>

In a frame of 4,096 the level between the saw's first two harmonics falls <Claim id="saw-resolved">more than 60 dB</Claim> below the second. In a frame of 1,024 <Claim id="saw-blurred">no bin between them reads lower than the second harmonic itself</Claim>: the frame is too short to tell 110 Hz from 220 Hz. At the other extreme sits a single click. One sample alone has no frequency at all, and the transform says so by giving every bin the same level, <Claim id="click-flat">within 0.001 dB</Claim>. A sine is one bin and all of time; a click is one instant and all of frequency. Every real sound is somewhere between, and the frame length decides which side the picture favours. The engine's Spectral Blur settles on 2,048 samples: 23.4 Hz between bins, 43 ms a frame (<Src path="cpp/devices/spectral-blur/spectral_blur.h" lines="58-59">`kFrame`</Src>).

### 5. Leakage, and the window

The saw's peaks above are not single bins, and the reason is the most important practical fact about the transform. Its sinusoids all turn a whole number of times in the frame. A sinusoid that does not, which is nearly every sinusoid in real sound, cannot be one bin. It has to be built from all of them.

The lab has a probe for this: a sine of its own in a frame of 64 samples, at a frequency you set in bins.

Exactly on bin 8 the bare sine is in that bin alone: every other bin is <Claim id="on-bin-plain">more than 120 dB down</Claim>. Half way to the next bin it is nowhere in particular. The two bins either side read <Claim id="between-plain">3.7 and 4.1 dB low, bin 13 is only 24 dB down, and no bin in the whole spectrum is below −38 dB</Claim>. (The two differ a little because a real sine has a mirror image, here at minus 8.5 bins, and that leaks too.) The spread is *leakage*. It is not noise and not an error of arithmetic: it is the true spectrum of a sine with a break in it every 64 samples.

A *window* is the cure. Before the transform, the frame is multiplied by a curve that is zero at both ends and one in the middle. The engine's spectral devices use the Hann window, a raised cosine (<Src path="cpp/devices/spectral-blur/spectral_blur.h" lines="69-71">written out in Spectral Blur's `init`</Src>):

<M block t={String.raw`w[n] = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos\frac{2\pi n}{N}`} />

With it, the sine half way between bins reads <Claim id="between-hann">1.4 dB low either side and 49 dB down at bin 13</Claim>. The price is paid where there was nothing to fix. The sine exactly on a bin, which was one clean point, now fills three: <Claim id="on-bin-hann">its neighbours stand 6.0 dB below it</Claim>. A window always trades these two against each other. The peak of every sinusoid (the *main lobe*) gets wider, here twice as wide, and in exchange its skirt (the *side lobes*) falls away far faster. No window at all is the narrowest peak there is, with the worst skirt.

### 6. Back again: the bins as turning vectors

The inverse transform is the same sum run the other way. Because bin <M t="N-k" /> mirrors bin <M t="k" />, the two can be taken together, and for a real frame the sum becomes one of plain cosines:

<M block t={String.raw`x[n] \;=\; \frac1N \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\,e^{\,j 2\pi k n / N}`} />

<M block t={String.raw`\phantom{x[n]} \;=\; \frac{X[0]}{N} + \sum_{k=1}^{N/2-1} \frac{2\,|X[k]|}{N}\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi k n}{N} + \varphi_k\Bigr) + \frac{X[N/2]}{N}(-1)^n`} />

Each term is a vector. Its length is <M t={String.raw`2|X[k]|/N`} />, the size of that sinusoid. It starts at the angle <M t={String.raw`\varphi_k`} />, the bin's phase, and for every sample it turns by <M t={String.raw`2\pi k/N`} />, so bin 1 goes round once in the frame and bin <M t="k" /> goes round <M t="k" /> times. Lay the vectors tip to tail, and the height the last tip reaches is the sample.

**The kit's scaling.** The <M t={String.raw`1/N`} /> in the equation has to go somewhere, and the kit leaves it to the caller. `forward` does not divide and neither does `inverse`, so a frame sent there and back returns <Claim id="inverse-times-n">N times as large</Claim>: 4,096 times at the longest frame. Once divided by N it matches the frame to <Claim id="round-trip">more than 130 dB</Claim>. The check below is the measured gain with that N taken out, so it reads 1.

<Check id="unscaled" />

A device folds that division into a gain it needs anyway. Spectral Blur multiplies each frame it adds to its output by `kOutputScale`, <M t={String.raw`1/(1.5\,N)`} />: the N for the unscaled inverse, the 1.5 for its overlapping windows (<Src path="cpp/devices/spectral-blur/spectral_blur.h" lines="214-215">the constant and its comment</Src>).

**The whole loop.** The last stage does to every frame what the figure does to one: transform, keep the first bins, transform back, divide by N. With the Hann window the frames are taken half a frame apart, where two neighbouring windows add up to exactly one, and the pieces are summed. Keeping the lowest bins is then a filter with a wall for an edge. With 48 bins of 4,096 kept (everything below 562 Hz), a 440 Hz sine passes within 0.01 dB and a 1 kHz sine comes out <Claim id="cut-hann">more than 95 dB down</Claim>.

### 7. How the kit does it fast

Written out as in stage 2, the transform is N sums of N products. For a frame of 4,096 that is 16.8 million multiplications by a sinusoid. The kit's <Src path="cpp/kit/fft.h" lines="221-251">`transform`</Src> does the same job in 24,576 steps, 683 times fewer, and that saving is what makes it practical to work on bins while the sound plays.

The saving comes from one observation. Take the even-numbered samples as one signal and the odd-numbered ones as another. Each has N/2 samples, and their transforms <M t="E" /> and <M t="O" />, with <M t={String.raw`W = e^{-j 2\pi / N}`} /> for one step of the turning, give the transform of the whole:

<M block t={String.raw`X[k] = E[k] + W^k\,O[k], \qquad X[k + N/2] = E[k] - W^k\,O[k]`} />

Two outputs come from two inputs with a single multiplication. That pair of lines is a *butterfly*, and it is the inner loop of the kit's code, term for term: `xr` and `xi` are <M t={String.raw`W^k O[k]`} />, the element at `b` becomes the difference and the one at `a` the sum. The halves are split the same way, and theirs again, down to single samples, which are their own transforms. That is why N must be a power of two, and it leaves <M t={String.raw`\log_2 N`} /> passes of <M t={String.raw`N/2`} /> butterflies each: 12 passes of 2,048 for a frame of 4,096.

The kit runs the recursion from the bottom up, with no recursion in the code. First it puts the samples in the order all that halving would leave them in. Splitting by even and odd over and over sorts the samples by the bits of their index read backwards, so `init` fills a table, `reverse_`, and `transform` begins by swapping each sample with the one at its bit-reversed index. Then the outer loop, `size = 2, 4, 8 … N`, joins neighbouring transforms into ones twice as long. The powers of <M t="W" /> come from the tables: one table of N/2 angles serves every pass, read at a `stride` of `N / size`. The inverse is the same loop with the sign of the sine turned round.

A real frame needs only half of this, since half of its spectrum is the mirror of the other. <Src path="cpp/kit/fft.h" lines="37-89">`forward_real`</Src> works out bins 0 to N/2 alone in half the butterflies, and it is what Spectral Blur calls. Its bins and the complex transform's agree to <Claim id="real-same">more than 130 dB under the largest</Claim>.

## What goes wrong

**Leakage reaches where you cut.** Switch the window to None and keep 48 bins. A 1 kHz sine, far above the bins kept, now comes through only <Claim id="cut-plain">29 dB down</Claim>, where the window held it under −95 dB. Its skirt reached all the way down into the bins that were kept. With real sound, listen for a buzz at the frame rate: frames laid end to end with nothing to join them.

**The edges of a frame are deaf.** The window is zero at the frame's first sample and close to it nearby. A click that falls exactly there is <Claim id="edge-click">multiplied away altogether</Claim>, and the spectrum of that frame is silence. This is why frames overlap: what one frame barely sees, the next has in its middle.

**Long frames smear time.** A bin holds one size and one angle for the whole frame, so anything done to a bin is done to all 85 ms of it at once. Change the bins of a frame with a hit in it and the change spreads before the hit as well as after. Cut everything above 562 Hz in frames of 4,096 (48 bins kept) and a single click half a second into the loop begins to sound <Claim id="pre-echo">73 ms before it happens, with ripples 43 dB under its peak 20 to 40 ms ahead</Claim>. The same cut in frames of 256 (3 bins kept) lets <Claim id="pre-echo-short">nothing through more than 4 ms early</Claim>. Short frames keep the hit in its place and cannot tell the notes apart. No frame length does both.

**Lateness.** A frame cannot be transformed until its last sample has arrived, so anything that works on bins answers at least a frame late. The device on the bench below is 2,304 samples behind its input, 48 ms: a frame of 2,048 and 256 more that it uses to spread its work (<Src path="cpp/devices/spectral-blur/spectral_blur.h" lines="58-61">`kLatency`</Src>). It delays its dry signal by the same amount so that the two stay in step.

**The floor.** The kit's transform is single precision. Its round trip ends a little more than 130 dB under the signal, which is far below hearing but is not zero, and it is why the checks on this page stop near −135 dB.
