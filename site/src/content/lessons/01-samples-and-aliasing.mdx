A sound in the air is a pressure that changes smoothly in time. A computer cannot hold "smoothly". It holds numbers. So the wave is measured at even instants, 48,000 times a second in this engine, and each measurement is kept as one number, a _sample_. Everything the engine does to sound, it does to lists of these numbers.

The surprise is how little that loses. As long as the wave holds nothing faster than half the sample rate, the samples hold all of it: there is exactly one such wave through them, and it can be drawn back. The trap is what happens otherwise. A wave faster than half the sample rate is not dropped. Its samples are also the samples of a slower wave, and the slower wave is what comes out. That is _aliasing_: one frequency going by the name of another.

An oscillator has to mind this more than anything else in the engine, because the shapes worth making have sharp edges, and a sharp edge holds frequencies without end. This lesson runs two of the kit's oscillators bare: its sine, asked for frequencies it has no business making, and its saw, beside a saw written the obvious way. Nothing goes in. Press Play, which starts on the kit's saw, then Listen on any stage.

## Stage by stage

### 1. A sample is a number at an instant

A sine at frequency <M t="f" />, measured <M t="f_s" /> times a second, is the list

<M block t={String.raw`x[n] = \sin\!\left(2\pi\,\frac{f}{f_s}\,n\right), \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots`} />

The only thing in it besides the count <M t="n" /> is the ratio <M t={String.raw`f/f_s`} />: how much of a cycle goes by between one sample and the next. The kit calls it the _increment_, and every oscillator in the engine is built on it. <Src path="cpp/kit/osc.h" lines="84-95">`SineOsc`</Src> keeps a _phase_, a number from 0 to 1 that says how far through the cycle the wave is. Each call of `next` reads the sine at the phase, adds the increment, and takes 1 off when the phase passes 1. At 2 kHz the increment is 1/24, so the phase goes round once every 24 samples.

The sine itself is not worked out each time. <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" lines="104-140">`SineTable`</Src> holds one cycle as 4,096 steps (4,097 points, so that the last step has an end), filled once, and `lookup` draws a straight line between the two points either side of the phase. A straight line is not a sine, but with 4,096 of them to the cycle the difference is small:

<Check id="tableError" />

At 2 kHz the table's sine stands <Claim id="table-error">133 dB under</Claim> the exact sine of the same phase.

### 2. A little more than two samples a cycle is enough

Between the dots there is room for any curve at all. The _sampling theorem_ says which one to draw: if the wave that was measured held no frequency at or above half the sample rate, then it is the only such wave through those samples, and so the samples lose nothing. Half the sample rate, 24 kHz here, is the _Nyquist frequency_. Hearing reaches about 20 kHz at best, so a rate of 48 kHz has room for everything audible and a little to spare.

"Enough" is hard to believe from the picture of the samples. Set the frequency to 23,952 Hz, one step of the slider under 24 kHz. A cycle now gets 2.004 samples, and the dots at the top of the page look like a slow swell of something else. The spectrum disagrees. It shows how much of each frequency the loop holds, worked out by the kit's own Fourier transform (lesson 2), and it finds one line, <Claim id="near-nyquist">within a bin of 23,952 Hz and at full level</Claim>. The sine is all there.

### 3. Past half the sample rate, a frequency folds

Nothing in `SineOsc` stops the increment at one half, so the lab can ask it for 30 kHz. What comes out is a sine at <Claim id="fold-30k">18 kHz, at full level</Claim>. The rule is

<M block t={String.raw`f_{\text{out}} = \left|\, f - f_s \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right) \right|`} />

the distance from <M t="f" /> to the nearest multiple of the sample rate. It follows from the list in stage 1. Add a whole cycle to the increment and no sample changes, because the sine of an angle and of that angle plus a full turn are the same number: <M t="f" /> and <M t={String.raw`f - f_s`} /> give the same samples. And a negative frequency is the same sine upside down. So 30 kHz has the samples of −18 kHz, which are the samples of 18 kHz <Claim id="fold-same-samples">with the sign turned</Claim>, number for number. Drawn against the frequency asked for, the rule is a zigzag that turns back at 24 kHz, like paper folded there. Hence _folding_. At 47 kHz the sine comes out at <Claim id="fold-47k">1 kHz</Claim>.

### 4. A saw, the obvious way

A saw rises in a straight line and drops back. The phase already does that, from 0 to 1, so the obvious saw is the phase stretched to run from −1 to 1:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{naive}[n] = 2\,\varphi[n] - 1`} />

As a sum of sines (lesson 2 shows why any repeating wave is one) a saw is every multiple of its frequency at once, the <M t="k" />th at one <M t="k" />th of the first:

<M block t={String.raw`2\varphi - 1 = -\frac{2}{\pi} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(2\pi k \varphi)}{k}`} />

Measured one partial at a time, the naive saw has exactly these levels: <Claim id="naive-levels">−3.9 dB at the fundamental and 6 dB less at twice it</Claim>. The sum has no last term, because the drop takes no time at all. On paper that is harmless. In samples, every partial above 24 kHz folds by the rule of stage 3, and partial <M t="k" /> of a saw at <M t="f" /> lands at the distance from <M t="kf" /> to the nearest multiple of 48 kHz.

At the 3,840 Hz this lab starts on, a cycle is twelve and a half samples and six partials fit under 24 kHz. The seventh, at 26,880 Hz, lands at 21,120; the eighth at 17,280; and so on down, 3,840 Hz at a time, until the twelfth, at 46,080 Hz, lands at 1,920 Hz: a tone an octave below the note, belonging to no saw. Measured by itself that partial stands at <Claim id="naive-fold-12">−25.5 dB</Claim>. The thirteenth lands at 1,920 Hz as well, from the other side of 48 kHz, and the ones after it climb back up the same six places, half way between the harmonics. In the loop as it sounds, each of those six lines is the sum of everything that lands there, and each reads <Claim id="naive-lines">between −19.7 and −21.9 dB</Claim>.

### 5. The kit's correction, term by term

The ramp is not the trouble. The drop is. <Src path="cpp/kit/osc.h" lines="17-20">`BlepOsc::saw`</Src> keeps the ramp and changes the drop into one that takes two samples to happen:

```cpp
advance(increment);
return 2.0f * phase_ - 1.0f - blep(phase_, inc_);
```

`advance` steps the phase. The first two terms are the naive saw. The third is <Src path="cpp/kit/osc.h" lines="54-65">`blep(t, dt)`</Src>, short for band-limited step, with `t` the phase and `dt` the increment. It asks where the nearest drop is, in samples, and answers with a parabola:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{blep} = \begin{cases} -(1 - x)^2, \quad x = \dfrac{t}{dt} & t < dt \\[10pt] \phantom{-}(1 + x)^2, \quad x = \dfrac{t - 1}{dt} & t > 1 - dt \\[10pt] \phantom{-}0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}`} />

- **`t < dt`**: the phase is smaller than one step, so it wrapped on this very step and the drop is behind this sample. `t / dt` is how far behind, from 0 to 1 sample. The code writes the answer as `t + t - t * t - 1`, which is <M t={String.raw`-(1-x)^2`} />. Taken away from the naive value, it lifts the sample: by 1, to the middle of the drop, when the sample falls on the drop itself, and by nothing a whole sample later.
- **`t > 1 - dt`**: the next step will wrap, so the drop is ahead. `(t - 1) / dt` is how far ahead, from −1 to 0. The code's `t * t + t + t + 1` is <M t={String.raw`(1+x)^2`} />, and taking it away lowers the sample.
- **otherwise** the sample has no drop beside it and the naive value stands.

The parabolas come from one idea. Take the saw on paper and replace each instant of it by a weighted average of the two samples' worth of time around it, the weight a triangle: most at the instant, none one sample either side. A straight ramp averaged evenly about each point is the same ramp. A drop becomes the running total of a triangle, which is one parabola curving away and another curving back. What `blep` returns is that curve less the bare drop, the _residual_, and it is zero everywhere but the two samples next to the drop.

### 6. What the correction does to each partial

Averaging over a triangle two samples wide does a simple thing to a sine: it leaves the frequency alone and scales the size by

<M block t={String.raw`H(f) = \left( \frac{\sin(\pi f / f_s)}{\pi f / f_s} \right)^{2}`} />

<M t="H" /> is 1 at 0 Hz, 0.41 at 24 kHz, and exactly zero at 48 kHz and at every multiple of it. Put that beside the folding rule. A partial lands low when it started near a multiple of 48 kHz, which is just where <M t="H" /> is near zero. A partial lands near 24 kHz when it started near 24 or 72 kHz, where <M t="H" /> is not small. So the correction is strongest on the folds that land lowest, and weakest on the ones that land at the top of the band.

## What goes wrong

**The folds near the top stay.** <M t="H" /> is not a wall at 24 kHz. In the kit's loop the line at 21,120 Hz reads <Claim id="kit-line-top">−30.6 dB, only 10.9 dB under</Claim> the naive saw's. At a 4 kHz fundamental the folds that land at or under the fundamental are <Claim id="fold-4k-low">41.9 dB lower or more</Claim>, but all the folds added together are just <Claim id="fold-4k-all">13.1 dB lower</Claim>, because the ones near the top carry most of what is left. The header of `osc.h` puts the kit's aliasing below −60 dB for fundamentals under a tenth of the sample rate. Partial by partial, that describes the folds that land low: at 440 Hz every fold under 12 kHz is <Claim id="promise-low">below −63 dB</Claim>. The strongest fold of all is at <Claim id="promise-top">−46.8 dB, near 23,750 Hz</Claim>, against −38.7 dB for the naive saw. For notes bright enough that this matters, the header points to a voice that runs at a multiple of the sample rate.

**The top of the saw is dulled.** The triangle cannot tell a fold from a harmonic. It scales everything by <M t="H" />, so the saw's own partials lose a little too: <Claim id="droop-fundamental">0.18 dB at the fundamental</Claim> and <Claim id="droop-top">7.2 dB at the sixth partial</Claim>, just under 24 kHz.

**Exactly half the sample rate.** The theorem says _below_ half, and means it. Set the sine to 24 kHz: it gets two samples a cycle, and since this one starts at zero both of them fall where the wave crosses zero. The loop is <Claim id="nyquist-silence">silence</Claim>.

**Folds that hide.** At exactly 4 kHz a cycle is 12 samples, the naive saw is <Claim id="hidden-4k">one pattern of 12 numbers repeated</Claim>, and every fold lands on a harmonic. The spectrum shows no stray peak. The folds are still there, changing the levels of the harmonics they sit on. Move the frequency a little and they walk out.

**The kit's shaped oscillator will not be asked.** <Src path="cpp/kit/osc.h" lines="48-52">`BlepOsc::advance`</Src> clamps the increment to 0.49, so a saw, a pulse or a triangle from the kit never has its fundamental above 23,520 Hz. `SineOsc` has no such clamp, which is the only reason stage 3 could be shown with the kit's own code.

## What to try

- Listen to the sine and move its frequency slowly from 2 kHz to the far end. Listen for the pitch rising out of hearing and then falling back in. At 47 kHz it is a 1 kHz tone.
- Set the sine to 30 kHz and look at the samples in stage 3: 18 kHz, drawn in blue through dots taken from 30.
- Set it to 24 kHz exactly, then one step lower. Silence, then a full-level sine.
- Listen to the naive saw, then the kit's. Listen for the tone an octave under the note in the first, and for it gone in the second.
- Listen to the correction by itself. It is all the kit adds: two samples at each drop.
- Take the saw down to 110 Hz. More than two hundred partials now fit under 24 kHz before the first one folds, and the folds are far smaller. Compare the two saws again.
- Move the saw a step off 3,840 Hz with the last spectrum in view. The six tidy lines of folds break into dozens, each fold on a frequency of its own.
- Then set it near 4 kHz and watch the grey peaks slide under the harmonics, where they hide.
