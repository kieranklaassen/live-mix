<Part id="why" title="A fader moves under a held note, and the note clicks.">

A note is held, and a hand pulls its fader down. In a room with a mixing desk nothing could be plainer. Inside a computer it is less plain, because the fader's position does not reach the sound on every sample. The program that hosts a device sends a new value when the fader has moved, so many times a second, and the device holds that value until the next one comes. Somewhere between the hand and the sound, the smooth move has become a staircase.

This lesson has such a fader. It goes down to nothing and back up to full <Src path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp" at="the place of a triangle, two" to="seconds a cycle, at the last moment a value was sent">once every two seconds</Src>, and its position is sent <Src path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp" at='lab::Control updates{"updates", "Fader values"'>ten times a second</Src>. Under it is <Claim id="tone-220">a steady tone at 220 Hz</Claim>, a sound with nowhere to hide anything.

<Still
  taps="fader"
  set="arrives"
  caption="The whole loop: the fader goes down to nothing and up to full twice. Each value that arrives is held until the next, so both slopes are staircases."
/>

Each stair is a jump in the gain, and a gain multiplies the wave. The gain moves a whole stair in one sample, so the wave it is put on jumps too. A jump is heard as a click, because a sudden edge holds every frequency at once, and a fader on the move makes a row of them. The rattle has a name: zipper noise.

  from={0.37}
  span={0.16}
  caption="The tone under that fader, where the fader reaches the bottom. The wave drops to half its height in one sample, and at the next value it is cut off altogether."
/>

Listen to it. <Hear set="arrives" label="Hear the fader as it arrives" />

### The obvious answer fails

Send the values more often. Smaller stairs must click less, and with enough of them a staircase is a slope.

<Predict
  question="The fader now sends two hundred values a second in place of ten, and still nothing smooths them. What happens to the clicks?"
  choices={[
    'They are gone: the stairs are too small to hear',
    'They are quieter, and there are twenty times as many',
    'They are as loud as before',
  ]}
>

Quieter, and many more. Each stair is a twentieth of the height it was, and there are twenty times as many. <Claim id="more-often">From 2 kHz upwards, far above the tone, the tone under two hundred values a second carries 16 dB less than under ten</Claim>. That is less, and it is not gone.

<Still
  taps="zipper"
  set="often"
  view="spectrum"
  caption="The spectrum of the tone under two hundred values a second. The tone is the peak at the left. Everything to the right of it was put there by the stairs."
/>

Listen for the buzz that rides on the tone. <Hear set="often" label="Hear two hundred values a second" />

Sending more only moves the trouble. The stairs would have to come once a sample to vanish, and a host does not send a control that often: it has the whole sound to compute in the same time.

</Predict>

### What is wanted

Something has to stand between the values that arrive and the gain that is applied, and turn each jump into a short glide. It cannot know where the fader is going next. It can only know where the gain is now and which value came last.

One rule is enough. On every sample, move the gain a fixed share of the way from where it is to the value that came last. Far from that value the gain moves quickly. Close to it the gain moves slowly, so a jump becomes a curve that leaves at once and eases in.

<Still
  taps="fader smoothed"
  set="glided"
  from={0.25}
  span={0.5}
  caption="Half a second around the bottom of the fader's move. The fader as it arrives is a staircase. Smoothed, each stair starts at once and bends into the value it is heading for."
/>

The clicks go with the corners. <Claim id="gain-step">As it arrived, the gain moved by 0.1 in one sample. Smoothed, its largest move in one sample is more than 240 times smaller</Claim>. On the tone, <Claim id="click-level">from 2 kHz upwards the smoothed fader leaves 40 dB less than the fader as it arrived</Claim>. <Hear set="glided" label="Hear the fader smoothed" />

### The same rule on a sound

Now put a sound into that rule in place of a fader's values. A drum loop has slow parts and quick parts at once: the swing of the kick is slow, and the tick of a hat is quick.

A number that moves only part of the way to each new sample cannot keep up with anything quick. A slow swing drags it along. A quick wiggle is over before the number has moved. So what the number holds is a running average of the sound, in which the newest samples count the most and older ones fade. That average is the slow part of the sound, its low end. The rule is a low-pass filter.

<Still
  taps="in low"
  set="kick"
  from={0}
  span={0.12}
  caption="The first hit of the drum loop, a kick, and the low-pass of it. The low-pass follows the slow swing of the kick a little behind it, and rounds off the sharp start of the hit."
/>

Take that average away from the sound and what is left is the quick part. That is a high-pass filter, and it costs one subtraction more.

This is the one-pole filter. It has one number of memory and one multiplication a sample, and it does two jobs that look unrelated. On a sound it takes one end off. On a control it turns a jump into a glide. In this engine the second job is everywhere: <Claim id="smoothed-devices">more than 100 of its devices</Claim> keep one of these on their controls.

It is also where filters begin. The three lessons after this one, <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" />, <Lesson id="equalising" /> and <Lesson id="phasing" />, build on what this one sets out: what a pole is, what a cutoff marks, and what the slope of a filter, counted in decibels an octave, means. Stages 1 and 3 of part 5 say each plainly, and measure it.

</Part>

Three things in this picture come back all through the lesson. The output does not jump when the input does. It moves fastest when it is furthest away. And on the way down it follows the same rule as on the way up, so the two curves are the same shape turned over.

One thing the picture cannot show at this size: covering a share of the distance every time is a way of getting closer for ever. Whether the output arrives at all is a real question, and part 5 and part 7 both come back to it.

<Part id="uses" title="On a sound it takes one end off. On a control it takes the click out.">

A one-pole filter is gentle. It does not cut a band out of a sound, and it leaves no mark of its own. It leans on one end. That makes it the filter to reach for when a sound only needs tilting, and the one that sits unseen inside other devices.

Each card below shows one such job, lists its setting and plays it. A card's button moves the whole page to its setting, so every figure further down shows it too, and the play bar offers the way back to where you were.

<Part id="build" title="From an average that keeps every sample to one that keeps a single number.">

Part 1 called the low-pass a running average. This part starts from the plainest average there is and changes one thing at a time until it is the kit's filter. Then it adds the high-pass, a fader and the fader's smoothing. After each piece the lanes are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these lanes and plays "Hear it so far".

The first step and the fifth are wrong on purpose. What is wrong with each is the reason for the piece after it.

<Build>
  <Step title="A running average" controls="cutoff">
    The slow part of a sound is what is left when its quick wiggles are averaged away. So take the mean of the last few samples, and do it again at every sample:

    <M
      block
      t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[n]} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \sig{in}{x[n-k]}`}
    />

    Here <M t="x" /> is the sound going in, <M t="y" /> is the average and <M t="N" /> is how many samples the average spans. To keep the sum up to date the build takes the oldest sample out as the newest goes in, and for that it has to remember all <M t="N" /> of them.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp"
      from="// A running average: the mean of the last"
      to="return static_cast<float>(sum[c] / window); }"
      name="average"
    />

    Cutoff <Src path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp" at="window = kit::clamp_int(">sets the span</Src>: the lower it is, the longer the average. <Claim id="build-window">With Cutoff at 500 Hz the average spans 31 samples. One sample put in comes out as 31 equal samples, and then nothing</Claim>.

    It works, and it has two faults. The first is the memory. The build sets aside <Src path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp" at="constexpr int kMaxWindow = 1024;">room for 1,024 samples a channel</Src>, where the filter it is heading for keeps one number. The second fault can be heard on the right sound. An average is blind to any tone that fits its span a whole number of times, because such a tone adds up to nothing over the span. <Claim id="build-holes">A tone at 1,548 Hz fits the 31 samples exactly once and comes out more than 60 dB down. A higher tone, at 2,323 Hz, comes out only 13.4 dB down</Claim>. A low-pass should not let a higher tone through louder than a lower one.

    <Lanes slot="picture" taps="in built">
      The sound going in and its running average. Try Cutoff at its lowest, and notice the hits melt
      into slow humps.
    </Lanes>

  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep one number, not every sample" controls="cutoff">
    The average is itself one number. Suppose that is all that is kept. When a new sample comes the old ones are gone, so the oldest cannot be taken out of the sum. What can be done is to move the average part of the way towards the new sample, by a share chosen so that the average looks about as far back as the <M t="N" /> samples did.

    <M
      block
      t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[n]} = \sig{in}{x[n]} + \at{follow-keep}{a}\,\bigl(\sig{out}{y[n-1]} - \sig{in}{x[n]}\bigr)`}
    />

    Read it from the right. The bracket is how far the old average stands from the new sample. The number <M t="a" />, between 0 and 1, is the part of that distance that is kept, and the rest is covered. With <M t="a" /> near 1 the average hardly moves and its memory is long. The build gets <M t="a" /> from the span of the step before: the newest sample is given the share <M t="2/(N+1)" />, and <M t="a" /> is what is left of 1.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp"
      from="// One number of memory: the average so far"
      to="return 1.0f - 2.0f / static_cast<float>(window + 1); }"
      mark="low[c] = flush_denormal(x + (low[c] - x) * keep);"
      at={{ keep: 'low[c] = flush_denormal(x + (low[c] - x) * keep);' }}
      name="follow"
    />

    Nothing is ever dropped from this average. A sample's weight shrinks by the same factor at every step and does not reach nothing, so the holes go. <Claim id="build-one-number">The tone at 1,548 Hz now comes through, 10.34 dB down, and the tone at 2,323 Hz is 13.6 dB down</Claim>: the higher tone is the quieter one again. And the memory is one number a channel, whatever the Cutoff.

    <Lanes slot="picture" taps="in built">
      The sound going in and the average kept as one number. Try Cutoff at its highest, and notice
      the average follow the sound so closely that the two lanes look alike.
    </Lanes>

  </Step>
  <Step title="Work the share out from a time" controls="cutoff">
    A span is a whole number of samples, so the step before could only be set in jumps, and near the top of Cutoff's range the jumps are coarse. The kit asks a different question: how long should the memory be, as a time?

    Its answer is to keep, at every sample, exactly as much of the distance as makes that distance fall to <M t="1/e" /> of itself, a little over a third, in the time asked for. That time is the filter's time constant. For a filter set by a cutoff it is one over <M t={String.raw`2\pi`} /> times the cutoff, and stage 2 of part 5 has the picture and the formula.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp"
      from="// How much to keep, from a time."
      to="lab::kSampleRate * 0.49f) / lab::kSampleRate); }"
      mark="return std::exp("
      name="time"
    />

    <Claim id="build-exact">At 500 Hz the weights now fall with a time constant of 15.3 samples, where the step before gave 15.5 samples. And 5 kHz and 6 kHz, which the step before made into one filter because both came to a span of three samples, are two filters</Claim>.

    On most sounds this step is hard to hear. It is the step that makes the build's low half the kit's own filter, line for line.

    <Lanes slot="picture" taps="in built">
      The sound going in and the low-pass, now set by a time. Try Cutoff anywhere, and notice the
      lane look as it did a step ago: what this step changes is too small to see at this size.
    </Lanes>

  </Step>
  <Step title="The high-pass: take the average away" controls="cutoff">
    The average is the slow part of the sound. So the quick part is the sound with the average taken away, and no second filter is needed to get it.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp"
      from="// The high-pass by subtraction: what is left of a sample"
      to="static float rest(float x, float average) { return x - average; }"
      mark="static float rest(float x, float average)"
      name="rest"
    />

    <Claim id="build-high">With Cutoff at 500 Hz a tone at 50 Hz, far below it, now comes out 20.3 dB down, where the step before passed it less than 0.1 dB down</Claim>. Listen on the drums for the weight of the kick going and the hats staying.

    <Lanes slot="picture" taps="in builtLow built">
      The sound going in, the low half as built, and the sound so far, which is now the rest. Try
      Cutoff higher, and notice more of each hit move out of the bottom lane and into the middle
      one.
    </Lanes>

  </Step>
  <Step title="A fader between the two halves" controls="cutoff updates">
    Two halves that add back to the sound invite a fader between them. All the way up is the low half alone, all the way down is the high half alone, and anywhere between is some of each. The fader of part 1 is put there, with its values applied as they arrive.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp"
      from="// A fader between the two halves: the low half alone at 1"
      to="return highs + fader * (lows - highs); }"
      mark="static float blend(float lows, float highs, float fader)"
      name="blend"
    />

    In the middle the halves are back in equal parts, which is the sound itself at half its size. <Claim id="build-fader">For the first tenth of a second of the loop the fader stands at one half, and a 750 Hz tone comes out 6.0 dB down, where the high-pass alone passed it 1.9 dB down</Claim>.

    This is what the Tremolo's Harmonic mode does, with the lab's fader in the place of the device's slow oscillator. It is also wrong in the way part 1 was wrong. The fader arrives as a staircase, and each stair is a jump in the sound. On the drums the hits may cover the jumps. Choose the pad or the saw in the row of sounds and listen again.

    <Lanes slot="picture" taps="in builtFader built" from={0.25} span={0.5}>

    </Lanes>

  </Step>
  <Step title="Smooth the fader" controls="cutoff updates smoothing">
    The cure is the line of step 2 again. This time what goes in is the fader's value as it arrives, and what comes out is the gain to use: a running average of where the fader has been. One thing is added. Once the gain is so close to the value that the rest could not be heard, it is put exactly on it, so a fader at rest leaves the gain at rest.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/one-pole.cpp"
      from="// Smoothing the fader: follow() again, on the fader's value"
      to="return gain; }"
      mark="const float moved = asked + left * gain_keep;"
      name="glide"
    />

    <Claim id="build-smooth">The largest move of the fader in one sample was 0.1. Smoothed, it is less than 0.001, and what the stairs put above 2 kHz on a 220 Hz tone comes down by 40 dB</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="low" n={1} title="One line: an average that keeps one number">
  The filter is for averaging a signal without keeping the samples it averages. On a sound that average is the low-pass the page opens on. Put one sample of 1 into the filter, then nothing, and it reads its weights out one after another: first the weight it gives the newest sample, then the weight of a sample one step old, and so on. That read-out is called the filter's impulse response.

    memory.
  </Curve>

    This is the line of step 2 of the build, and the listing below is the kit's own. <M t="x" /> is the sample going in, <M t="y" /> is the output and <M t="a" /> is the part of the old distance that is kept. Multiplied out it reads <M t={String.raw`(1-a)\,x[n] + a\,y[n-1]`} />: a little of the new sample and most of the old output. Follow that back through time and it unrolls into a sum over everything that ever went in.

    <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[n]} = (1-a)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a^{k}\,\sig{in}{x[n-k]}`} />

    Read it against the stalks. The newest sample has the weight <M t="1-a" />, the first stalk. Every older sample has <M t="a" /> times the weight of the sample after it, so each stalk is the same fraction of the one before. The weights add up to 1, so a steady input comes out at its own level. <Claim id="newest-weight">With Cutoff at 500 Hz the first stalk is 0.063</Claim> and <Claim id="weight-ratio">each stalk after it is 0.937 of the one before</Claim>.

    That ratio is what the filter is named after. Take the input away and the line above becomes <M t={String.raw`y[n] = a\,y[n-1]`} />: left alone, the filter multiplies what it remembers by <M t="a" /> at every sample. A number that a filter's memory is multiplied by in this way is called a pole of the filter. A pole smaller than 1 dies away, as the stalks do. A pole of exactly 1 would hold its value for ever, and a larger one would grow without end. This filter has one number of memory, so it has one pole, and the pole is <M t="a" />. The filter of <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" /> remembers two numbers and has two poles, which together can do what one cannot: ring.

    The lab's check holds what the filter put out against <M t={String.raw`(1-a)\,a^{n}`} /> worked out directly. They agree to <Claim id="impulse-error">less than 0.0000001 at every cutoff tried</Claim>.

  </Fragment>

    slot="listing"
    path="cpp/kit/filters.h"
    from="// One-pole lowpass, y += (1 - a)(x - y)"
    to="float highpass(float x) { return x - lowpass(x); }"

<Part id="ways" title="Tilt, do not cut; listen to what was removed; and think in time.">

One pole has few settings, so the ways of using it are habits more than recipes. Each card with a button plays its setting.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Take the edge off, and stop there" tag="a high cutoff" set="soften">
    Set high, one pole is a tilt and not a cut. On a bright sound it rounds the top and leaves the body alone. <Claim id="way-soften">On the saw, what is above 5 kHz comes out 8.1 dB lower and what is below 1 kHz less than 0.1 dB lower</Claim>. Try Cutoff lower from here, and listen for the point where the saw stops sounding like a saw.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Listen to what you are taking away" tag="the other half" set="removed">
    The high-pass at the same Cutoff is exactly what the low-pass removed, so playing it alone is a quick way to judge a low-pass. If the removed half holds something the sound needs, the Cutoff is too low. On the drums, at the Cutoff the lesson starts with, <Claim id="way-removed">the removed half comes out 11 dB under the drums and the low-pass less than 0.5 dB under them</Claim>: by level, nearly all of this loop is in the half that was kept.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Round the edges of a chopped sound" tag="a long smoothing time" set="swell">
    Smoothing need not be too short to notice. A gain that jumps between a few values chops a sound into blocks, and smoothed over a longer time each edge of a block becomes a short fade. <Claim id="way-swell">With two values a second and Smoothing at 20 ms, 63 % of each jump is covered 20 ms after it and 95 % after 60 ms</Claim>. The Tremolo does the same to its own square wave, with two poles in a row: <Src path="cpp/devices/tremolo/tremolo.h" at="The slew is two poles and never less than 1 ms each">its notes say that is what keeps the square from clicking</Src>.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Send more values, and smooth them too" tag="both cures at once" set="fine">
    Part 1 found that sending values more often is not enough by itself. With smoothing it is worth having: the stairs are small, and what is left of them is rounded. <Claim id="way-fine">With 200 values a second and Smoothing at 5 ms, what the fader leaves above 2 kHz on the tone is 50 dB lower than the unsmoothed fader left at 10 a second, and 10 dB lower than the smoothing alone left</Claim>.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Think of it as a time" tag="when it is not on a sound">
    On a control, or inside a detector that follows a level, nobody hears a cutoff. What matters is how long the filter's memory is. Ask for that, let the formula of stage 2 turn it into a pole, and allow the three to five time constants that stage measured.
  </Way>

<Part id="wrong" title="Clicks, stairs, lag, a soft slope, a cutoff that is only a name, and a filter that does not arrive.">

Most of what goes wrong with one pole is one of its virtues seen from the other side. It is gentle, so it does not stop much. It eases in, so it is late. It covers a share of the distance, so it does not get there.

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Zipper noise" set="zipper">
    A control that is applied as it arrives puts a jump into the sound at every value. With Smoothing at 0 the smoother is not there at all: <Claim id="smoothing-zero">the tone under it is the tone under the stepped fader, sample for sample</Claim>. Smooth every control that can move while sound is passing: in this engine, with the kit's `Smoother` at the time stage 6 gives.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Stairs with soft corners" set="stairs">
    Smoothing rounds the corner of each stair. It does not join the stairs up. When values come seldom and the smoothing is short, the gain glides for a moment and then stands still until the next value. <Claim id="fault-stairs">With four values a second and Smoothing at 5 ms the gain stands exactly on a stair 76 % of the time, although what is left above 2 kHz on the tone is 40 dB lower than unsmoothed</Claim>. The clicks have gone, and the level still moves in steps. Send the values more often, or lengthen the smoothing until one glide runs into the next.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A fader that arrives late" set="late">
    Smoothing is lag. A gain that is always on its way has not reached the fader's value when the fader moves on, so a fade to nothing does not get to nothing. <Claim id="fault-late">With 200 values a second and Smoothing at 20 ms, the gain gets no lower than 37 dB down where the fader reaches nothing. With Smoothing at 5 ms it gets 51 dB down</Claim>. Use the shortest time that does not click. Where a move has to end on time, the linear ramp of stage 7 ends on time, and where a value should jump, as a mute should, `snap` jumps.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="One pole is no wall" set="gentle">
    A low-pass is often expected to remove what is above its cutoff. One pole only turns it down, a little more with each octave. <Claim id="fault-gentle">With Cutoff at 200 Hz the drums above 2 kHz come out 26 dB lower, and with Cutoff an octave lower, at 100 Hz, only 6 dB lower still</Claim>. Listen for the hats: turned down, and still there. Two one-poles in a row take off twice as many decibels at every frequency, so they fall twice as steeply and their corner is as soft. A steep filter with a firm corner needs two poles that work together, which is <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" />.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Set high, the cutoff is only a name">
    The kit takes the pole from the time constant, so the time is the exact part and the frequency is the part that bends. Write a frequency <M t="f" /> as the angle a tone at it turns through in one sample, <M t={String.raw`\omega = 2\pi f/f_s`} />, and the Cutoff <M t="f_c" /> in the same way, <M t={String.raw`\omega_0 = 2\pi f_c/f_s`} />. <Claim id="corner-formula">The response is 3 dB down where <M t={String.raw`\cos\omega = 2 - \cosh\omega_0`} />, and the measured corner lands on that to less than 0.001 %</Claim>. <Claim id="corner-2pc">That corner is within 2 % of the Cutoff for every setting up to 3.6 kHz</Claim>. Higher up it drifts: <Claim id="corner-8k">set to 8 kHz the filter is 3 dB down at 8.86 kHz</Claim>, and <Claim id="corner-never">set to 13.5 kHz or higher it does not fall 3 dB anywhere below half the sample rate</Claim>. <Claim id="corner-20k">Set to 20 kHz it is 1.2 dB down at 20 kHz</Claim>.

    <Claim id="exact-pole">A pole placed so that the corner falls on the Cutoff itself</Claim> would be <M t={String.raw`a = b - \sqrt{b^2 - 1}`} /> with <M t={String.raw`b = 2 - \cos\omega_0`} />. The kit does not place it so: it takes the pole from the time, as stage 2 showed. For a gentle roll-off the name is enough, and a filter that has to be in tune is not built from one pole.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="It does not arrive">
    The filter covers a share of the distance, and a float cannot hold a distance smaller than its own step. <Claim id="never-arrives">With Cutoff at 500 Hz the low-pass stops 7 float steps short of a steady 1</Claim> and stays there, <Claim id="never-silent">which leaves the high-pass 127.6 dB down for good, and not silent</Claim>. A slower pole stops further out: <Claim id="stall">the bare filter with a time constant of 5 ms ends 120 float steps short, 103 dB down</Claim>.

    <Check id="stall" />

    That is what the landing of stage 7 is for. Falling towards zero has the opposite trouble. The number shrinks into a range where arithmetic is slow, so the filter passes what it remembers through <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h" at="inline float flush_denormal(float x) {">`flush_denormal`</Src>, which makes anything smaller than <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h" at="constexpr float kDenormalThreshold = 1.0e-15f;">a millionth of a billionth</Src> an exact 0.

  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things people say about a simple filter.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="The cutoff is where the filter stops the sound.">
    At the cutoff a tone has lost half its power, and it is plainly still there. Above the cutoff each octave takes off more, at the slope stage 3 measured. The first picture of stage 3 is that slope, and the card "One pole is no wall" in part 7 plays it.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A smoothing time is how long the control takes to arrive.">
    It is the time constant: the time to cover <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" at="// A time constant as a one-pole coefficient: the pole that covers 63 % of a">63 % of the way</Src>. <Claim id="lag-15">At 5 ms a control is 95 % of the way 15 ms after its value was sent</Claim>, and stage 7 measures how much longer it takes to land. The step picture of stage 2 shows the curve still climbing long after it has crossed that level.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A high-pass is a different filter from a low-pass.">
    Here it is the same filter and one subtraction. Step 4 of the build adds it in a line, and stage 4 shows the two responses crossing.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Smoothing a control removes its steps.">
    It rounds their corners. The picture of stage 6 shows a staircase before and after: the corners are gone, and the stairs are still there. The card "Stairs with soft corners" in part 7 plays one. The steps go when the values come about as often as the smoothing is long.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Split a sound at a crossover and the two halves are equally loud there.">
    That is the textbook's split. With this one the halves add back exactly, sample by sample, and at the cutoff the high half is a little further down than the low half. Stage 4 has both numbers.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="6 dB an octave means the filter takes 6 dB off above the cutoff.">
    It is a slope and not an amount: that much again for every doubling of the frequency. The loss goes on growing with every octave above the cutoff, until the top of the band, where stage 3 shows the slope flatten.
  </Myth>
</Myths>
