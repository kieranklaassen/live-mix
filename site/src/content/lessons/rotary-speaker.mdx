<Part id="why" title="A held chord stands still. A loudspeaker that turns makes it move.">

A chord is held on an organ, or on a synthesiser's pad, and nothing in it changes. Every note keeps the pitch it began with and the level it began with, and the whole chord comes from one place between the loudspeakers. A singer never holds a note like that, and nor does a violinist: the pitch leans a little, the level swells, the head turns.

Listen to one. <Hear set="held" label="Hear the chord as held" />

<Still
  taps="in"
  set="held"
  caption="The held chord, as it goes in. Its outline keeps one height for as long as a chord lasts: nothing in it moves."
/>

So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you make a sound that stands still move, without touching the keys?

### The obvious answer fails

Ride its level up and down. That is tremolo, the subject of <Lesson id="tremolo-and-ring-modulation" />: one slow wave multiplies the sound, which gets louder and quieter in time with it. Here the chord's level is ridden <Claim id="ridden-level">over 12.0 dB</Claim>, a little less than once a second.

<Still
  taps="ridden"
  set="ridden"
  caption="The same chord with its level ridden. The outline swells and sinks, and that is all that happens to it."
/>

Listen to that. <Hear set="ridden" label="Hear its level ridden" />

It moves, but in one way only. <Claim id="ridden-pitch">A tone put through the same ride comes out less than 0.001 cents from the pitch it went in at</Claim>, where a cent is a hundredth of a semitone, and both loudspeakers are sent the same sound, so the chord stays where it was between them. It is what a hand on a fader does.

### Then the loudspeaker was turned

The answer that lasted is made of wood and a motor. Put the loudspeaker in a cabinet, point it into a horn, and turn the horn. The sound now leaves from a mouth that swings round in a circle: towards the listener, past, away, and back again.

Three things then change together, and they are three things that ears are made to notice.

- **Pitch.** While the mouth swings towards you, each wave has a little less far to come than the one before it, so the waves arrive closer together and the pitch is higher. While it swings away the pitch is lower. This is the Doppler effect, which bends the note of a siren as it goes by.
- **Loudness.** A horn throws its sound forwards. Pointing at you it is loud. Pointing at the back of the cabinet it is quieter, and duller as well, since the highest frequencies are the ones a horn beams most narrowly.
- **Side.** With two ears, or two microphones, the mouth passes one before the other. The sound leans left and then right. What makes a sound seem to come from one side is the subject of <Lesson id="stereo" />.

The engine's cabinet is listened to through two microphones, and the lab reads what each one hears of the horn. At the settings this page starts from the horn turns <Src path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h" at="static constexpr float kHornHz[2] = {0.8f, 6.7f};">0.8 times a second</Src>. As the left microphone hears it, <Claim id="turned-three">the horn's pitch swings between 4.8 cents flat and 4.8 cents sharp and its loudness over 12.0 dB, and its level at the left microphone against its level at the right one swings over 19.1 dB</Claim>: for part of each turn the left microphone has much the louder horn, and for another part the right one has.

<Still

  set="turned"
  caption="How loud each microphone hears the horn. The two lines have one shape, and the right microphone's runs behind the left's: the mouth passes the left microphone first."
/>

Listen to the chord through it. <Hear set="turned" label="Hear it through the turning speaker" />

A swing of five cents is small. Listen again, and notice which of the three you follow: the pitch, the swell, or the lean from side to side. Now let Speed be Fast. The horn turns <Src path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h" at="static constexpr float kHornHz[2] = {0.8f, 6.7f};">6.7 times a second</Src>, and the mouth covers the same circle that much faster. <Claim id="fast-pitch">The loudness still swings over 12.0 dB, and the pitch now swings between 40.6 cents flat and 39.6 cents sharp</Claim>, about two fifths of a semitone each way. The two numbers differ because cents count a ratio. The speed the sound is played back at goes as far below 1 as above it, and the same step is a larger ratio going down than going up. <Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" cap /> finds the same of its own moving delay.

A quarter of a turn before. The pitch rises with the speed at which the mouth is coming closer, and it comes closer fastest while it is moving straight at the microphone, which is when the horn points across the line to it. When the horn points straight at the microphone the mouth is as near as it gets, and for a moment it is neither coming nor going. Measured on the lab at Slow, <Claim id="quarter-turn">the pitch is at its highest 0.25 of a turn before the loudness is, and at the loudest moment the pitch is less than 0.01 cents from the pitch that was played</Claim>.

So the pitch, the loudness and the side never peak together. A tremolo with a vibrato added would move in step with itself. A horn that turns moves in a round, one motion handing over to the next, as a thing going past does.

</Predict>

The cabinet has a second rotor. A horn is too small to throw low notes about, so the lows go to a loudspeaker of their own that fires into a turning drum with an opening in its side. It does to the lows a gentler version of what the horn does to the highs, at a speed of its own. Part 4 builds both.

The engine's device is that cabinet as arithmetic: an amplifier, a filter that parts the sound in two, two rotors and two microphones. This lesson starts it with both depths full, the amplifier clean and the microphones close, so that each motion is easy to see. Part 9 puts those settings beside the device's own.

</Part>

Check this against the picture. Leave the three controls where the lesson starts them and move the instant until the wedge points straight at the left microphone. That is where the picture opens. The loudness row then reads <Claim id="picture-top">3.4 dB</Claim>, its highest, and the pitch row is passing through its middle on the way down. Move the instant back by a quarter of a turn, to where the wedge points across the picture and its mouth is coming straight at the microphone. The pitch row is at its top, <Claim id="picture-sharp">4.8 cents</Claim>, and the loudness is on its way up.

Three settings shape the picture, and each belongs to one part of it.

- **Speed** sets how often the rows repeat. It also sets how tall the pitch row is, since the pitch follows how fast the mouth moves, while the loudness row keeps its height.
- **Horn Depth** scales all three rows together. At 0 the horn might as well stand still.
- **Spread** is the angle between the two microphones. It moves the two circles apart, and the further apart they stand, the longer the mouth takes to get from one to the other and the further the sound leans.

Notice the loudness row. Its top is above 0 dB and its bottom is much further below. The device does that on purpose: it sets the size of the swing first and then raises the whole by one fixed amount, so that the power over a full turn is the power that went in. Stage 6 of part 5 has the sum.

Notice also what the picture leaves out. The short arc inside the circle is the second rotor, the drum, which carries the lows and turns the other way. The three rows say nothing of it. And the rows hold only as long as the speed holds: what happens when Speed changes is the most recognisable sound a rotary speaker makes, and part 4 gets to it at step 6.

</Part>

<Part id="build" title="Two bands and two rotors, a piece at a time.">

The quickest way to understand a rotary speaker is to make one. This part starts with a filter that parts the sound in two and adds seven pieces to it, one at a time. After each piece the lanes are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these lanes and plays "Hear it so far".

Until the last step the build has no amplifier and no blend with the plain sound: what you hear is what the two microphones pick up. Each piece is a function, and the last listing of step 8 is the loop that calls them in the order a sample meets them.

<Build>
  <Step title="Part the sound in two">
    A horn small enough to spin cannot throw low notes, and a drum heavy enough to carry them cannot be spun about like a horn. So the cabinet has two loudspeakers, and the first thing it does to a sound is share it out: the highs to one, the lows to the other. One filter gives both at once. It is the filter of <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" />, set at <Src path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h" at="static constexpr float kCrossoverHz = 800.0f;">800 Hz</Src>.

    Each band is written into a short delay line of its own, which the later pieces will read from. For now both lines are read straight back and added. <Claim id="bands-800">A tone at 800 Hz comes out of each band 6.0 dB down, and the two bands added carry it less than 0.001 dB from the level it went in at</Claim>. The sum holds its level at every other frequency too, which is why the highs are turned over on their way in: without that, the two bands would cancel where they meet.

    Press "Hear it so far". It is the loop, and nothing in it moves.

    Now the horn turns. Its angle moves on by its speed on every sample, and each microphone asks one question of it: how nearly does the mouth point at me? The answer is a cosine, 1 when the mouth points straight at the microphone and −1 when it points straight away. Spread says where the two microphones stand, so each gets a cosine of its own.

    The loudness follows that cosine. With this piece taken out again it falls by nothing. One fixed gain after it brings the whole back up, so that a deeper swing is not a quieter sound.

    The two microphones hear the same swell at different moments. At Slow, with Spread where the lesson starts it, <Claim id="mics-apart">the right microphone's loudest moment comes 0.35 s after the left's</Claim>.

    It is a tremolo so far, one for each side. Press "Hear it so far" and listen for the sound crossing from left to right.

  <Step title="Make it darker when it faces away" controls="speed hornDepth spread">
    A horn beams its highest frequencies in a narrow cone and lets the lower ones spill round its sides. So when the mouth turns away, the sound does not only get quieter: it loses its top first.

    The build keeps a second copy of what the microphone hears, with the top taken off by a one-pole low-pass filter at <Src path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h" at="static constexpr float kShadeHz = 2200.0f;">2,200 Hz</Src>, and mixes it in as the mouth turns from the microphone: none of it at the front, <Src path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h" at="static constexpr float kHornShade = 0.8f;">four fifths</Src> of the sound at the back. The swing now depends on the frequency. <Claim id="shade-swing">A tone at 6 kHz in the horn falls by 19 dB between front and back and a tone at 3 kHz by 16 dB, where before this piece each fell by 12.0 dB</Claim>.

  <Step title="Let the distance change, which bends the pitch" controls="speed hornDepth spread">
    So far the mouth has turned on the spot. In a cabinet it travels: it swings out towards the microphone and back, and sound takes time to cross the gap. The build reads the horn's delay line further back the further the mouth is from the microphone. At Horn Depth 1 <Claim id="path-swing">the delay swings over 1.1 ms</Claim> between the nearest point of the circle and the furthest, which the device's source gives as <Src path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h" at="// - Horn Depth 1 swings the horn's path by ±0.55 ms, the mouth of a 19 cm">the mouth of a horn 19 cm long</Src>.

    A delay that holds still changes nothing but the time of arrival. A delay that is getting shorter plays the sound back a little fast, and one that is getting longer plays it a little slow: <Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" /> is built on that. With this piece taken out again the pitch does not move at all.

    Until now Speed has been obeyed at once. A real rotor is a thing with mass on the end of a belt, and it takes time to get going. This is the sound most people know a rotary speaker by: the player changes the speed at the end of a phrase, and the chord gathers itself up, or winds down.

    The page cannot press a control in the middle of a loop, so the lab has one of its own for this. Second half names the Speed the lab changes to at the middle of the loop, and it changes back at the loop's start, so both runs are in every pass.

    Three things are left, and none of them turns.

    The cabinet has an amplifier of its own, and players push it. Drive sends the sound through a soft curve before the filter parts it. <StageBlock tap="driven" n={1} title="Both sides become one, and the amplifier">
  A cabinet has one amplifier and one horn, so the first thing the device does is add the left side and the right side of what comes in and halve the sum. Whatever was different between the two sides is gone from here on. The two sides that come out are made by the two microphones and by nothing else.

  Then the amplifier. It is a soft curve: nearly straight for small values, and bending over as they grow, towards a ceiling it never reaches.

  <Controls ids="drive" />

  <Curve taps="driveCurve" diagonal xTitle="value in" yTitle="value out" min={-1.2} max={1.2} height={300}>
    What the amplifier makes of each value, from the device's own function. With Drive at 0 the
    drive's curve lies on the line of no change. Raise Drive, and notice the top bend over sooner
    than the bottom: the curve is not the same on both sides of zero.
  </Curve>

  <M block t={String.raw`s(u) = \frac{u}{\sqrt{1 + u^2}}, \qquad u = G\,\sig{in}{x} + \beta`} />

  Here <M t="x" /> is the sample going into the amplifier, which is the two sides added and halved, <M t="G" /> is a gain that rises with Drive, from 1 to <Src path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h" at="const float u = (1.0f + 5.0f * drive) * x + kBias;">6</Src>, and <M t={String.raw`\beta`} /> is a small fixed offset, <Src path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h" at="static constexpr float kBias = 0.2f;">0.2</Src>. The offset is what makes the curve lopsided: a wave pushed through it is flattened more on one side than on the other, which adds harmonics at even multiples of each frequency as well as odd ones. Curves of this kind are the subject of <Lesson id="saturation" />.

  A curve like this makes new frequencies, and some of them would land above half the sample rate and fold back down. So the device does not read the curve at one value. It takes the mean of the curve over the step from the sample before to this one, which for this curve can be written out exactly:

  <M block t={String.raw`\bar s = \frac{u + u_{-1}}{\sqrt{1 + u^2} + \sqrt{1 + u_{-1}^2}}`} />

  where <M t={String.raw`u_{-1}`} /> is the value of <M t="u" /> one sample ago. The mean of a curve over a step is smoother than the curve, and that takes the edge off the new frequencies before they can fold.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/devices/rotary/rotary.h"
    from="float tube(float x, float drive, float level) {"
    to="return x + ((shaped - kBiasOut) * level - x) * amount; }"
    name="tube"
  />

  The last line takes away what the offset alone would put out, so that silence stays silence, and multiplies by a level that undoes most of the gain. Quiet playing comes out louder than it went in and loud playing is held back, as step 8 of part 4 measured. A filter after the function removes whatever steady offset is left. At Drive 0 the function hands the sample back untouched.

<StageBlock tap="loudness" n={6} title="Louder when it faces you">
  Now the level. The device multiplies what the microphone hears by a gain that is highest when the mouth points at it.

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{loop-loud}{\sig{out}{g} = \bigl(1 - a\,(1 - q)\bigr)\,M}`} />

<StageBlock tap="out" n={9} title="Balance and Mix">
  Two blends finish the device. Balance weighs the horn against the drum, and Mix weighs what the microphones hear against the plain sound that came in.

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{loop-wet}{\sig{alt}{y} = \sqrt{2}\,\sin\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2} B\bigr)\,h + \sqrt{2}\,\cos\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2} B\bigr)\,l}`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{loop-left}{\sig{out}{o} = \cos\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mu\bigr)\,\sig{in}{x_L} + \sin\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mu\bigr)\,\sig{alt}{y}}`} />

  Here <M t="h" /> is the horn at the left microphone and <M t="l" /> is the drum there, <M t="B" /> is Balance, <M t={String.raw`\mu`} /> is Mix, <M t="x_L" /> is the left side of the sound as it came in, and <M t="o" /> is what goes out on the left. The right side is made the same way from the right microphone and the right side of the input.

  Both blends follow a quarter of a circle, so the squares of the two weights always add to the same sum. <Lesson id="stereo" cap /> moves a sound from side to side by the same law. It keeps the power level where the two things blended are equally strong and have nothing to do with each other, and neither is so here. The horn and the drum are sent bands of different strength, so Balance is a control of tone: more of the highs towards the horn, more of the lows towards the drum. The plain sound and the turned one are one sound a moment apart, so with Mix between its ends they cancel at some frequencies, as the first card of part 7 shows.

  At Balance one half both weights are 1, and <Claim id="balance-ends">at either end one band goes out alone, 3.0 dB up, with what is left besides it more than 140 dB under the output</Claim>.

  The listing's comment calls the two weights of Mix exact at both ends. Measured: <Claim id="mix-zero">at Mix 0 the output is the input, sample for sample, on every loop</Claim>. At Mix 1 the weight of the plain sound is the sine table's cosine of a quarter of a turn, which the table holds as a number a little above nothing. The fourth card of part 7 measures what gets through.

<Part id="ways" title="Five things people do with it.">

A rotary speaker has few settings and one of them is played. These are the habits worth knowing. Each card lists settings to try and plays them.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Play the change of speed" tag="Slow for the phrase, Fast for its last chord" set="wayRun">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="pitch"
      set="wayRun"
      caption="The pitch of the horn at the left microphone over the loop. At the start the horn is still slowing down from Fast, and the swings shrink and spread out. From the middle they grow tall and crowd together as it gathers speed again."
    />

    The run between the speeds is the instrument. Change at a moment that means something in the music: up on the last chord of a phrase, down as the next begins. Lower Acceleration for a heavier cabinet that takes longer over it, and raise it when the run gets in the way.

  </Way>
  <Way title="Keep the bass still" tag="Drum Depth at nothing" set="wayStill">
    On a whole mix, or a part with a bass line in it, a low end that swells and leans makes the ground move. Drum Depth at 0 lets the lows through the cabinet without turning them, while the horn goes on moving everything above the crossover. Balance towards the drum does something different: it makes the still part louder than the moving part.

  </Way>
  <Way title="Step back from it" tag="Distance raised" set="wayFar">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="loudness"
      set="wayFar"
      min={-10}
      max={5}
      caption="The loudness of the horn's straight sound at the left microphone, with the microphones far off. The swells are shallow: the axis is tall enough to hold those the lesson starts with."
    />

    Close microphones give the pulse: every pass of the horn is a beat. Far ones give the wash, since the walls fill in between the passes. Close suits a part that should be noticed, and far suits one that should sit behind others.

  </Way>
  <Way title="Use one microphone" tag="Spread at nothing" set="wayOne">
    At Spread 0 both microphones stand in one place and the two sides are the same sound. The pitch and the loudness still move and the lean from side to side is gone. That is the setting for a part that has its own place in a mix, or one that will be heard through a single loudspeaker.

  </Way>
  <Way title="Push the amplifier" tag="Drive raised" set="wayDrive">
    The cabinet's amplifier is part of its sound. With Drive up the amplifier holds loud playing back and brings quiet playing up, as step 8 of part 4 measured, so the loud and the quiet of a part come closer together. Listen for loud chords thickening. The new harmonics are made before the crossover, so those above it go to the horn and are thrown about with the rest.

  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Five ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Half plain, half turned" set="faultMix">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="outPicture"
      set="faultMix"
      view="spectrogram"
      top={8000}
      caption="A steady bright tone with Mix at one half, as a spectrum over time. Arcs of quiet sweep up and down through the harmonics as the horn turns: each is a frequency that the plain sound and the turned one cancel at for that moment."
    />

    Mix at one half looks like the safe setting, and it makes a flanger. The plain sound and the horn's sound reach the output a moment apart. The gap is the moving delay of step 4 of part 4: almost nothing when the mouth points at the microphone, and a little over a thousandth of a second when it points away. Two copies of a sound a moment apart cancel at some frequencies, as <Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" /> shows, and here the cancelled frequencies rise as the mouth comes round to the microphone and fall as it goes away. <Claim id="mix-cancels">Each of six tones from 2 kHz to 12 kHz, with Mix at one half, comes out at its quietest moment in the turn more than 20 dB under the level it went in at, and with Mix at 1 none of them does</Claim>. A cabinet in a room has no such control. Use Mix at 1, and if the plain sound is wanted beside it, bring it in on a channel of its own, where a longer gap can be put between the two.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Stopped facing the wall" set="faultBrake">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="loudness loudnessRight"
      set="faultBrake"
      caption="How loud each microphone hears the horn. From the middle of the loop the swells slow down and stop, and the two lines stay where the horn left them."
    />

    Brake lets the rotors stop wherever they happen to be, and wherever they stop is how the part sounds from then on. On this card <Claim id="brake-parks">the horn comes to rest with the left microphone hearing it 8.0 dB louder than the right</Claim>: it has stopped nearly facing away from the right microphone, which gets the dull back of the horn, and the picture between the loudspeakers leans left until Speed is changed. Ask for Slow and Brake again to try another place, or do not stop it.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A bass that hardly moves" set="faultBass">
    A bass line has little for the horn. On this card <Claim id="bass-horn">the band the horn is sent is 16 dB under the band the drum is sent, and it is still 16 dB under with Drive at 1</Claim>: the harmonics the amplifier adds do not change the share. The drum has the sound, and moves it gently: <Claim id="bass-drum">its loudness swings over 2.1 dB on this card, and over 4.8 dB with Drum Depth at 1</Claim>. Raise Drum Depth for as much motion as the lows can have. The cabinet's larger motions are for what lies above the crossover.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A wide sound goes in, and one sound comes out">
    The device adds the two sides of its input into one before it does anything else. A part that was wide comes out with the cabinet's own width and none of its own, and whatever was only in the difference between the sides is gone altogether: <Claim id="sides-cancel">a tone that is the same on both sides but turned over on one comes out more than 300 dB under the level it went in at</Claim>. The microphones hear nothing of it at all. What does come out is the plain sound, through the trace of a weight that Mix leaves it at 1. Put the cabinet on the single part it is meant for, before any widening, and not across a finished stereo picture.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The rotors stop in a rest">
    When nothing has come in for a tenth of a second and the output has died away, the device stops working, and its rotors stop with it. <Claim id="sleeps">For tones from 200 Hz to 6 kHz, a tone that stops leaves the horn standing still 0.2 s later</Claim>, which is the tenth of a second and the time the output takes to die away before it. When the next note arrives the horn starts again from where it stood. In a part with long rests the motion therefore does not keep time through the gaps. If that matters, keep some sound going into the cabinet.

  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Five things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A rotary speaker is a tremolo.">
    A tremolo moves the level and nothing else. The horn moves the pitch, the loudness, the tone and the side, each at a different point of the turn. Part 1 plays the level alone beside the whole, and the three rows of part 2's picture never peak together.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The pitch is highest when the horn points at you.">
    It is highest a quarter of a turn earlier, while the mouth is coming straight at you. When the horn points at you the pitch is the played pitch and the loudness is at its top. The prediction of part 1 measures it, and the curve of stage 4 in part 5 shows where the delay is steepest.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Fast is Slow, sped up.">
    The loudness swings as far at either speed, but the pitch swing grows with the speed: about eight times as far at Fast, as step 4 of part 4 measured. And the two rotors change speed at different paces, so the run between the speeds is a sound that neither speed has.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The whole sound spins.">
    Only the part above the crossover goes through the horn. The lows go to a drum that turns the other way, more slowly, with half the swing in level and about a third of the swing in pitch. Stage 2 of part 5 shows where the line is drawn, and stage 8 the drum beside the horn.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Brake stops it.">
    Brake lets it coast. The horn takes more than a second to come to rest from Slow and the drum more than two, as step 6 of part 4 measured. From Fast both take longer: <Claim id="brake-fast">the horn stands still 2.25 s after Brake is asked for, and 4.0 s after it the drum is still turning</Claim>. And where they stop is left to chance, as the second card of part 7 shows.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
