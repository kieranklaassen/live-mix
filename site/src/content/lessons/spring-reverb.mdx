<Part id="why" title="A room will not fit in a box, so somebody tried a spring.">

A drum is struck with a microphone a hand's width away. The microphone hears the drum and almost nothing of the room around it. On the loop this page opens with, each hit stops dead, and the quietest fifth of a second between two hits measures <Claim id="dry-gap">−85 dB</Claim> against full scale, the largest number a sample can be. That is silence.

<Still
  taps="in"
  caption="Four seconds of the drum loop as it was played. Between the hits the line lies flat on zero: nothing follows a hit."
/>

Listen to it first. <Hear set="dry" label="Hear the drums as played" />

In a hall each of those hits would be followed by a tail. The sound reaches a wall and comes back, reaches another and comes back again, until thousands of reflections arrive so close together that the ear hears one long dying wash. The tail tells a listener how big the place is, and it joins each note to the next.

A close microphone has no tail. An electric guitar has none either: its sound is a voltage in a wire and has never been in a room. So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you put a tail behind a sound when there is no room, and whatever makes the tail has to be carried in one hand?

### The obvious answer fails

Play the sound again a moment later. That is a delay line, the subject of <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" />: a memory that hands back what it was given, some time on.

The picture below is what one delay line does to a click. A click is one sample that stands up from silence. It holds every frequency at once, so in a picture of frequency against time it is an upright stroke. The click goes in at the left edge, where the picture starts. One stroke comes out, later and still upright.

<Still
  taps="builtChirps"
  view="spectrogram"
  caption="One click through a delay line and nothing else. Time runs across and frequency runs up. The click comes out as it went in: one upright stroke, all of it at the same moment. The stroke stops short of the top of the picture, and part 2 says why."
/>

That is an echo, and an echo is a copy. Feed the end of the line back into its start and there is a row of copies, evenly spaced. A row of drum hits a fixed time apart is a rattle. It does not sound like a place.

A tail is a different thing from a copy. In a tail no single reflection can be picked out, because each one has been smeared and they overlap. A delay line does not smear. It does one thing well, which is to say the same sound again.

### A spring is a delay line that smears

The answer that fitted in a box was a steel spring: a loose coil, stretched between two small magnets. At one end a coil of wire twists the spring in time with the sound. The twist travels along the spring as a wave, and it travels slowly, taking hundredths of a second to cover a length a hand can span. Further along, a second coil of wire turns the movement back into a voltage. The ends of the spring reflect, so the wave runs back and forth and the pickup hears it pass again and again.

So far that is the delay line with its end fed back. The difference is in how the wave travels. In a coiled spring the speed of a wave depends on its frequency. Some frequencies run ahead and others fall behind, so a sound that goes in with all its frequencies together comes out with them spread along time. This is called dispersion.

Dispersion is what turns a row of copies into a tail. The first echo of a hit is already smeared. The second has been through the spring again and is smeared further. After a few trips the echoes are longer than the gaps between them, they run into one another, and what is left is a wash with a fizz at its top. It is not the sound of a room. It is a sound of its own, and it became part of how an electric guitar is expected to sound.

Here are the drums again with the springs behind them, at the settings the engine's device starts with. The stretch that measured −85 dB now measures <Claim id="sprung-gap">−33 dB</Claim>. <Hear set="sprung" label="Hear the drums with the springs" />

The device this lesson opens is the engine's Spring. It is a model in arithmetic of the tank just described: a delay line for the length of the spring, a chain of small filters for the dispersion, and a reflection at the far end. The lesson starts it with the springs alone, so that every picture shows what the springs do and nothing else. The dry sound, and the two controls that colour what goes into the tank, come back as controls. Part 9 puts the lesson's starting settings beside the device's own.

Check this against the picture. Leave the three controls where the lesson starts them, and find where each stroke meets the bottom edge. <Claim id="strokes">The feet of the first three, measured round 300 Hz, stand at 24 ms, 65 ms and 106 ms. At 3 kHz the same three strokes have reached 29 ms, 74 ms and 120 ms</Claim>. The bottom edge counts in seconds, so 24 ms is 0.024 on it.

Four things can be read off those strokes, and each is a piece of the machine.

The feet are evenly spaced. The gap between two feet is the time a wave takes to run down the spring and back, which for the first spring of this device is <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="static constexpr float kRoundTrip[kSprings] = {0.0412f, 0.0356f, 0.0479f};">41.2 ms</Src>. The first foot stands about half of that from the click, because the pickup sits half way round.

Each stroke leans. That is the dispersion: the higher a frequency, the longer the spring holds it back.

Each stroke leans further than the last. Every trip adds the same lean again. Measured on this answer, <Claim id="stretch">3 kHz trails 500 Hz by 9.3 ms in the second echo and by 13.9 ms in the third</Claim>, where in the first it trailed by 4.8 ms.

Each stroke is fainter than the last, and the top fades sooner than the foot. Decay sets how much every trip keeps. Above the tops of the strokes the picture is empty: the tank does not carry the highest frequencies, and stage 2 of part 5 says why, and where it stops.

Notice what the picture leaves out. It is the answer to one click, and music is not clicks. Every sample of a played sound sets off a row of chirps of its own, and all of those rows are added together. The picture also shows one side only. On Two, where the lesson starts, the left side is one spring and the right side is another, a little shorter. Stage 7 of part 5 sets them side by side.

</Part>

<Part id="uses" title="Four kinds of sound, and what the springs give each.">

A spring answers the front of a sound differently from its body. A sharp front is full of high frequencies, which the spring holds back longest, so it comes out as a splash. A steady body comes out as a wash. What a spring reverb does to a sound therefore depends on how much front the sound has.

Each card below shows a sound with what the springs add drawn over it, lists a setting to start from, and plays it. A card's button moves the whole page to its setting, so every figure further down shows it too, and the play bar offers the way back to where you were.

  <Step title="Feedback from the far end" controls="tension decay">
    A wave that reaches the end of a spring is reflected and runs back. The build reads the line at its far end, multiplies what it finds by a gain a little under 1, changes its sign, and adds it to what goes in. Now a click goes round and round.

    <Claim id="build-echoes">It comes back every 41 ms round 500 Hz, each time 1.0 dB lower, and every 46 ms round 3 kHz</Claim>. The two times differ because every trip passes the hundred all-passes again, and they hold 3 kHz longer. That is the reason each stroke of part 2 leans further than the one before.

    The sign matters to the wave and not to the ear. A reflection at a clamped end turns a wave over, so <Claim id="build-inverts">each echo is the one before turned upside down</Claim>.

    The gain comes from Decay: it is the gain at which the echoes have fallen 60 dB after that many seconds. The length of the line comes from the round trip, less the time the all-passes already hold the lowest frequencies, so the feet of the strokes stay a round trip apart wherever Tension is. Both are worked out in a function shown at the end of this part.

    That length is not a whole number of samples. So the far end is read between two samples, by one more small all-pass whose delay is the part left over. In the listing, the two lines that ask for pieces not yet built do nothing yet.

  <Step title="Damping" controls="tension decay">
    Something is wrong with the last step, though a third of a second of one click is too short to show it. Every trip costs every frequency the same share, and a high frequency takes longer over each trip. So it makes fewer trips in a second, and the top of the tail outlasts the bottom.

    Steel does the opposite. It takes more from a fast vibration than from a slow one. So the build puts a one-pole low-pass, the filter of <Lesson id="one-pole" />, in the way back from the far end. <Claim id="build-damping">The tail of a click round 3 kHz now falls 40 dB a second where it fell 22 dB a second. Round 600 Hz it falls 25 dB a second where it fell 24 dB. Each rate is the level of the tail from 0.2 s to 0.6 s after the click, less its level a second later</Claim>.

    The piece is one line. How hard the filter leans on the treble is tied to Decay, so that a short decay is also a dull one. Stage 6 of part 5 has the formula.

<StageBlock tap="driven" n={1} title="The drive circuit: what the tank is given">
  A spring is not fed the sound as it stands. In the hardware a small transformer drives the coil that twists the spring, and that circuit has a character of its own. The device does five things to the sound on its way to the tank. It adds the two sides together, because a spring has one input. It holds the sum back by Pre-delay. It removes what lies below <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="rumble_.set_cutoff(60.0f, sr);">60 Hz</Src>, which a small transformer does not pass. It pushes the treble up by Drip. And it squashes the loudest moments by Drive.

  Pre-delay moves the springs' whole answer later and changes nothing else in it: <Claim id="predelay-shift">with Pre-delay at 60 ms the first echo comes 60.0 ms later</Claim>.

  <Controls ids="predelay drip drive" />

  <Spectrum taps="in driven" min={-96} max={0}>

    the right-hand half of the second line rise while its left-hand half stays where it was.
  </Spectrum>

  Drip first. With <M t="x" /> the sound after the bass has been removed, the circuit makes

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{x_d} = \sig{in}{x} + \at{drive-drip}{4\,d\;\mathrm{hp}(\sig{in}{x})}`}
  />

  <Fragment slot="readback">
    Read it against the picture. <M t="d" /> is Drip, from 0 to 1. <M t={String.raw`\mathrm{hp}`} /> is a one-pole high-pass with its corner at <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="static constexpr float kDripHz = 2200.0f;">2,200 Hz</Src>: it lets the treble through and holds the bass back. Measured with steady tones, <Claim id="drip-lift">Drip at 1 sends a tone at 3 kHz into the tank 11.2 dB louder and a tone at 500 Hz 2.8 dB louder</Claim>. The treble is where the chirp is longest, so more of it is more splash.

    Then Drive:

    <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y} = \at{drive-tanh}{\frac{\tanh(D\,x_d)}{D}}`} />

    <M block t={String.raw`D = \at{amount-drive}{1 + 6\,\text{Drive}}`} />

    The curve <M t={String.raw`\tanh`} /> is a straight line through zero that bends over and goes flat at 1. Multiplying by <M t="D" /> first pushes the sound further up the bend. Dividing by <M t="D" /> afterwards puts small sounds back where they were. So a quiet sound goes through unchanged, and a loud one is flattened at one over <M t="D" />, which at the top of Drive is <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="static constexpr float kMaxDrive = 7.0f;">a seventh</Src>. The device uses the kit's quick stand-in for the curve, <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" at="inline float fast_tanh(float x) {">`fast_tanh`</Src>.

<StageBlock tap="lowered" n={2} title="Down to a fifth of the sample rate">
  The hundred all-passes of each spring are, by the note in the device's own source, <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="// loop is most of the device's cost.">most of what it costs to run</Src>. The dispersion they model also has a top. Above a frequency called the transition frequency a real spring behaves differently, and its pickup gives little back. The device puts that frequency near <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="static constexpr float kTransitionHz = 4600.0f;">4,600 Hz</Src> and uses it to save work: the tank runs at a sample rate just high enough to carry sound up to there, and no higher.

  Samples cannot simply be dropped. Anything above half the new rate would fold back down as tones that were not played, which is the aliasing of <Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" />. So before the tank the sound goes through a steep low-pass: <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="static constexpr int kDownSections = 8;">eight</Src> two-pole sections in a row, with its corner <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="const float corner = 0.8f * 0.5f * low_rate_;">eight tenths of the way</Src> to half the tank's rate. Measured with steady tones, it takes <Claim id="down-corner">3.0 dB from a tone at 3,840 Hz and 32.7 dB from one at 4,800 Hz</Claim>.

  One all-pass first. It remembers one number, <M t="s" />, and does two things with each sample <M t="x" /> that comes in:

  <M block t={String.raw`s[n] = \at{cascade-state}{x[n] - a\,s[n-1]}`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`y[n] = \at{cascade-out}{a\,x[n] + (1 - a^2)\,s[n-1]}`} />

  <M t="y" /> is what goes on to the next filter. <M t="a" /> is the coefficient, a number between 0 and 1. With <M t="a" /> at 0 the two lines say that what comes out is what went in one sample ago: a delay of one sample for everything. As <M t="a" /> grows, low frequencies get through sooner than that and high ones later. A hundred of them hold a frequency <M t="f" /> back by

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{\tau(f)} = \frac{N\,(1 - a^2)}{1 + 2a\cos\omega + a^2}`}
  />

  <Fragment slot="readback">
    Read it against the picture. <M t={String.raw`\tau`} /> is the hold, in samples of the tank, and <M t="N" /> is the hundred. <M t={String.raw`\omega`} /> is the frequency as an angle, <M t={String.raw`2\pi f / f_r`} />, with <M t="f_r" /> the tank's rate from stage 2. At the left edge <M t={String.raw`\cos\omega`} /> is 1, the bottom of the fraction is as large as it gets, and the hold is at its shortest. At the right edge <M t={String.raw`\cos\omega`} /> is −1, the bottom is as small as it gets, and the hold is at its longest. In between it only rises, which is why low frequencies come first whatever the setting. With Tension at 0.5 the chain holds <Claim id="held">500 Hz back 2.5 ms and 3 kHz back 7.0 ms</Claim>.

    Tension is <M t="a" />. It runs from <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="static constexpr float kSlack = 0.45f;">0.45</Src> at 0 to <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="static constexpr float kTaut = 0.80f;">0.80</Src> at 1. A larger <M t="a" /> shrinks the top of the fraction and makes the bottom swing further, so the left of the curve sinks and the right-hand end shoots up. The spread moves out of the middle of the band and into its top: <Claim id="tension-ends">the chain holds 3 kHz back 5.6 ms longer than 500 Hz with Tension at 0, and 2.5 ms longer with it at 1</Claim>.

    The measured line is the device's own tank with its loop cut: the lab sends one sample into it, lets nothing come back from the far end, and works out from what the all-passes hand on how long each frequency was held. It lies on the formula's line to <Claim id="held-formula">less than 0.001 ms</Claim> at every setting of Tension the test tries.

    In the engine the three springs' chains run side by side, a stage at a time, which lets the processor work on all three at once.

  Tone is a two-pole low-pass, the filter of <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" />. The tank's band ends where stage 2 cut it, so the top of Tone's range has little left to remove. Lower down it does a great deal: <Claim id="tone-500">with Tone at 500 Hz the first echo loses 3.0 dB round 500 Hz and 31 dB round 3 kHz, against Tone at 6,000 Hz</Claim>. The chirp lives in the treble, so Tone is also how much chirp is heard.

  The level is there because a tank that rings longer holds more sound for the same input:

  <M slot="equation" block t={String.raw`\ell = \at{level-set}{1.5\,\bigl(1 - g^2\bigr)^{1/4}}`} />

  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t="g" /> is the trip gain of stage 6. A row of echoes that each keep <M t="g" /> of the last adds up to an energy that grows as one over <M t={String.raw`1 - g^2`} />. A square root of <M t={String.raw`1 - g^2`} /> would cancel that exactly, and the device takes the fourth root, which cancels half of it in decibels. So a longer Decay is still a little louder, as a longer tail should be, and not by much: on the drums, <Claim id="level-holds">from Decay at 0.5 s to Decay at 6 s the pickup rises 6.7 dB and what the springs add rises 3.0 dB</Claim>. The <Src path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h" at="static constexpr float kWetGain = 1.5f;">1.5</Src> in front is a fixed gain.

    <Listing
      path="cpp/devices/spring-reverb/spring_reverb.h"
      from="const float kept = spring_[0].gain * spring_[0].gain;"
      to="level_.set(kWetGain * std::sqrt(std::sqrt(kit::max(1.0f - kept, 1.0e-3f))), started_);"
      name="level"
      at={{ set: 'level_.set(kWetGain * std::sqrt(std::sqrt(kit::max(1.0f - kept, 1.0e-3f))), started_);' }}
    />

    Last, a limit. A note held on one of a spring's resonances can ring far above the level it went in at, so the device passes the springs through the kit's <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" at="inline float soft_clip(float x) {">`soft_clip`</Src>, scaled so that it changes nothing up to full scale and lets nothing past twice that.
  </Fragment>

  Two controls decide how the springs meet the dry sound. Width sets how far apart the two sides of the springs stand. Mix sets how much of the springs and how much of the dry sound comes out.

  <Controls ids="width mix" />

  <Lanes taps="in added out">

    notice the middle lane shrink while the last lane turns into the first.
  </Lanes>

  Width works on the sum and the difference of the two sides:

  <M block t={String.raw`m = \tfrac12\,(l + r), \qquad s = \at{mix-side}{\tfrac12\,w\,(l - r)}`} />

  <M t="l" /> and <M t="r" /> are the left and right of the springs, and <M t="w" /> is Width. The sum <M t="m" /> is what the two sides share. The difference <M t="s" /> is what sets them apart, and Width scales it. The left output gets <M t="m + s" /> and the right <M t="m - s" />. At 1 that gives each side back as it was. At 0 the difference is gone, and <Claim id="width-zero">both sides are the same, sample for sample</Claim>. <Lesson id="stereo" cap /> has more on sums and differences.

  Mix then blends each side with the dry sound:

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y} = \at{power-dry}{\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,\mu\right)}\,\sig{in}{x} + \at{power-wet}{\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,\mu\right)}\,(m \pm s)`}
  />

  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t={String.raw`\mu`} /> is Mix, from 0 to 1, <M t="x" /> the dry sound and <M t="y" /> what comes out. The two gains are the cosine and the sine of the same angle, so their squares add up to 1 wherever Mix stands, and the whole stays about as loud as it crosses from dry to springs. Half way, each is the square root of a half: <Claim id="mix-half">with Mix at 0.5 the dry sound is 3.0 dB down and the springs are 3.0 dB down</Claim>. At 0 the cosine is 1 and the sine is 0, and what comes out is <Claim id="mix-zero">the input, sample for sample</Claim>.

<Part id="ways" title="Six things people do with a spring.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the strokes and the lanes are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Splash on the attack" tag="Drip up, Tension down" set="splash">
    Drip sends more treble into the tank, and a slack Tension spreads each echo into a long sweep. Together they make every picked note set off a splash. After the card, lower Drip a little at a time and listen for the splash shrinking while the wash behind it stays.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Slack or taut" tag="Tension, from one end to the other" set="slack">
    Tension decides whether an echo is a sweep or a slap. <Claim id="way-slack">With Tension at 0 the part of the first echo round 3,500 Hz trails the part round 500 Hz by 10.2 ms, and with Tension at 1 by 5.8 ms</Claim>. The card is the slack end on the drums. After it, raise Tension to the top and listen for each sweep closing up.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Keep the note clear" tag="a pre-delay" set="clear">
    A spring answers at once, and on a busy part its splash lands on top of the note that made it. A pre-delay moves the whole answer later and leaves the front of the note alone. On this card <Claim id="way-clear">the foot of the first echo stands at 104 ms, where without the pre-delay it stood at 24 ms</Claim>. After the card, lengthen Pre-delay and listen for the point where the springs turn into an echo of the part.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A dark wash" tag="three springs, Tone down, Decay up" set="dark">
    Tone takes the chirp away and leaves the body of the tail. With all three springs on and a long Decay, what remains is a dark wash under held chords. After the card, raise Tone and listen for the fizz coming back.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Drive the tank" tag="Drive up" set="driven">
    Drive flattens the loud moments before they reach the springs, so the tail of a loud hit is no bigger than the tail of a moderate one. On the drums <Claim id="way-driven">the springs add 4.3 dB less than the same card with Drive at 0, and the loud moments lose the most: the loudest tenth of a second of what they add falls 5 dB and the quietest 3 dB</Claim>. Listen to the tails of the loudest hits against the quietest.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Put the springs in the middle" tag="Width at 0" set="narrow">
    Two springs that differ make a wide sound, and wide is not wanted everywhere: on one instrument among many, or where the mix may be heard on a single speaker. With Width at 0 the springs come out <Claim id="way-narrow">the same on both sides, sample for sample</Claim>, and the dry sound keeps whatever width it had.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Five ways it goes wrong, four of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Every hit answered by a chirp" set="boing">
    A sharp hit is nearly a click, and a click is what shows a spring's chirp most plainly. With Tension slack and Drip full, each drum hit comes back as a row of loud sweeps that the ear follows one by one. If that is not the effect wanted, raise Tension so the sweeps close up, lower Drip so less treble goes in, or lower Tone so less of it comes out.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="One spring's beat" set="flutter">
    A single spring has a single round trip, so every hit is followed by echoes at one fixed spacing. On this card the springs are heard alone, on One, and what comes out is <Claim id="fault-flutter">the same on both sides, sample for sample</Claim>. Listen for a quick regular patter behind each hit. More springs with different round trips break the pattern up: put Springs on Three.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="No top end" set="dull">
    The tank carries next to nothing above its band, whatever goes in. <Claim id="fault-dull">Noise comes out of the springs 50 dB lower round 6 kHz than it went in</Claim>. Tone cannot give that back, because it only takes away. This is how the device is made and how a real tank behaves, so the cure is not in the springs: keep Mix under 1 and let the dry sound carry the top.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Low notes that will not stop" set="mud">
    The drive circuit removes only the very lowest bass. Everything above that goes into the tank, and a long Decay keeps it there. A bass line through the springs rings on under the notes that follow, and the notes blur. Shorten Decay, lower Mix, or keep the springs off the bass.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Loud and quiet come back alike">
    With Drive high, the drive circuit has a ceiling, and everything loud is pressed against it. Stage 1 of part 5 measures the ceiling. <Claim id="fault-level">With Drive at 1, a 440 Hz tone that goes in 20 dB under full scale leaves the circuit 21 dB under it, and one at full scale leaves it 17 dB under: 20 dB of playing have become 4 dB</Claim>. The tails of a part that swings between loud and soft then follow the playing far less. Lower Drive, and the springs answer a soft note softly again.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A spring reverb is a small room.">
    In a room, sound of every frequency travels at the same speed, so each reflection is a copy of what was played. In a spring the speed depends on the frequency, and each reflection is a chirp. The picture of part 2 is nothing a room does. A spring is its own instrument, and is used for what it is.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A spring's echoes are copies of the sound, as a delay's are.">
    Each trip passes the dispersion again. The second echo is stretched twice as far as the first and the third three times, as the measurement under the picture of part 2 shows, and after a few trips the echoes overlap into a wash. Step 3 of the build is where that begins.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Decay is how long everything rings.">
    Decay sets what one trip keeps at the bottom of the band. The treble is damped on top of that, and the two ends of the band fall at different speeds: stage 6 of part 5 measures both, with Decay where the lesson starts it. The number on the control is close for the low end and long for the high end.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="For a bright spring, turn Tone all the way up.">
    Tone is a low-pass, so it can only remove. Above the tank's band there is nothing for it to let through, as the fault of the missing top shows. What makes a spring sound bright is Drip, which sends more treble in, and the dry sound, which still has its own.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Mix at one half is half of each.">
    The two gains are a cosine and a sine, not two halves. With Mix at 0.5 each is about seven tenths of its size, not a half, and stage 9 has the measurement in decibels. The curve is chosen so that the whole stays about as loud all the way across.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="More springs make a longer reverb.">
    All three springs take their gain from the same Decay. With Decay at 2.5 s, <Claim id="myth-springs">on every setting of Springs the tank takes at least 2.4 s and at most 2.5 s to fall 60 dB below 1 kHz</Claim>. What more springs change is how many echoes there are in each moment, and how different the two sides are.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
