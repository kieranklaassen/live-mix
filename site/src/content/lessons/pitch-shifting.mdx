<Part id="why" title="A recording played faster is higher. It is also over sooner.">

A guitarist has played a line and wants a second voice over it: the same line an octave up, note for note, at the same tempo. Nobody is going to play it again. The second voice has to be made from the first.

So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you change the pitch of a sound and leave its length alone?

### The obvious answer fails

Recorded sound has always had one way to change pitch: play the recording faster. Every wave then goes by in less time, so more of them fit in a second, and the pitch rises. At twice the speed each wave takes half as long, and that is an octave.

Here the lab does exactly that and nothing else. It reads the recorded loop at twice its speed, taking a smooth curve through the samples wherever it lands between two.

<Still
  taps="tape"
  set="tape"
  caption="The same recording read at twice the speed. The whole line has gone by in the first half of the picture, and then it goes by again."
/>

It is an octave higher, as asked. <Hear set="tape" label="Hear the recording at twice the speed" /> And the line is over <Claim id="tape-half">after 2.00 s</Claim>, half of the four it took to play. To fill the loop the lab has to go through the recording twice.

That is the trouble with speed. A recording has one dial, how fast it is read, and that one dial sets pitch and length together. They are one thing seen two ways. A second voice that runs through the line in half the time is no harmony.

For a recording the trouble is at least only musical. For a sound that is being played now it is worse. To read a live sound faster than it arrives, the head would have to read ahead of the present, in sound that nobody has played yet.

### Reading the last moment again

The way out is to stop reading the whole recording and to keep a short memory of the sound instead: its last moment, written afresh all the time. That memory is the delay line of <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" />, and this lesson calls it the ring.

A head reads the ring. If the head moves at the speed the sound is written, it stays a fixed distance behind, and that is a plain delay. If it moves faster, what it reads comes out higher, exactly as the fast tape did. It also gains on the place where the sound is being written, and it cannot pass that place. So before it arrives it jumps back, and reads again a stretch that it has read already.

That repeat is where the missing length comes from. An octave up, the head gets through each stretch in half the time and plays every stretch twice. Twice a half is the whole: the pitch has doubled and the length has not moved. Going down is the mirror of it. The head reads slower than the sound is written, falls behind, and jumps forward over a stretch that is then not played at all.

A jump lands in the middle of a wave, and that is a click. So there are two heads, half a stretch apart. Each fades to nothing before it jumps and comes back up afterwards, and the other is at its loudest just then.

This is the engine's Pitch Shifter doing it to the line. <Hear set="octave" label="Hear it an octave up, at its own length" />

### Where it is weak

That is the whole method, and its weak point is its last step. What comes out is made of overlapping pieces, and where two pieces overlap, two different moments of the sound lie one over the other. Before going on, here is a question about the simplest sound there is.

Its level dips. With the page as it opens, the level of the shifted sine falls by <Claim id="ripple-depth">1.84 dB</Claim> and comes back, <Claim id="ripple-rate">33.3 times a second</Claim>.

The two heads do read the same tone. They read it at two places in the ring, half a stretch apart, and at the settings the page opens on that distance is 13.2 cycles of the tone, which is not a whole number of them. So while one head hands over to the other, the two copies are out of step, and two waves out of step add up to less than two waves in step. Gains that add up to one are not enough.

That handing over is called the splice, and most of this lesson is about it: what it does to a tone, why, and what can be done about it.

The engine's Pitch Shifter has four ways of working, which it calls modes. This lesson starts it in the one called Vintage, which is the plain method and nothing more, so that the splice is in plain view. Part 5 then turns to Smooth, which looks at the sound before it splices. The device itself starts in a fourth mode, Chords, which leaves the heads behind altogether. Part 9 sets the lesson's starting settings beside the device's own.

<Part id="picture" title="A delay that shrinks at a steady speed, and jumps back.">

<Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" cap /> shows that a delay which is changing bends the pitch of whatever passes through it, for as long as it goes on changing. While the delay shrinks the sound comes out sharp, and while it grows the sound comes out flat. There the delay swings to and fro, so the pitch wobbles about the true one and comes back.

A pitch shifter is that bend made to go one way and stay there. Its delay shrinks at a steady speed, so the pitch is raised by a steady interval. No delay can shrink for ever, so this one is a sawtooth: a ramp down, and a jump back up to where the ramp began.

Check this against the picture, with both controls where the lesson starts them. Each head comes down from <Claim id="delay-span">61.5 ms behind to 1.5 ms behind</Claim>, and one ramp takes <Claim id="sweep-octave">60 ms</Claim>, so the picture holds five ramps of each head. The two heads are <Claim id="half-apart">30.00 ms apart</Claim> all the way along, which is half of Size.

The slope of a ramp is the interval. With Pitch A at +12 a head comes <Claim id="slope-up">1.000 ms nearer for every millisecond that passes</Claim>: it gets through two milliseconds of sound in each one. At −12 it falls <Claim id="slope-down">0.500 ms further behind</Claim> in each millisecond, and one ramp takes <Claim id="sweep-down">120 ms</Claim>. At 0 the line lies flat and the device is a plain delay. At +1, a slight detune, the head creeps: one ramp takes <Claim id="sweep-semitone">1.01 s</Claim>, so a jump comes about once a second where an octave brings nearly seventeen.

The height of the teeth is Size, the length of the stretch a head covers before it jumps. A larger Size makes the jumps rarer, and puts the two heads further apart.

The picture cannot show what is heard while a line jumps. That depends on the other head, and on how well the two sounds they are reading agree. The rest of the lesson is about that.

</Part>

<StageBlock tap="ratioA" n={1} title="From an interval to a ratio">
  A musician counts pitch in semitones, and a head needs a speed. So the first thing the device does with Pitch A is turn the one into the other. Twelve semitones double a frequency, and every semitone multiplies it by the same amount, so <M t="s" /> semitones multiply it by

  <M block t={String.raw`r = \at{ratio-exp}{2^{\,s/12}}`} />

  <M t="r" /> is the ratio: how many samples a head reads for each one that is written. Over 1 the pitch rises, under 1 it falls, and at 1 the head keeps its distance and nothing is shifted.

  <Controls ids="pitchA detune" />

  <Curve taps="ratios" uprights="pitchA" xTitle="interval, semitones" yTitle="ratio">
    The ratio for each whole number of semitones, two octaves either way, from the kit's own
    function, with Pitch A drawn up it. Try Pitch A at 12 and at −12, and read off 2 and a half. The
    curve bends because equal steps of pitch are equal multiplications, not equal additions.
  </Curve>

  The curve gives the musical intervals their numbers. Measured on the sine through the running device, <Claim id="fifth">+7 semitones is a ratio of 1.4983</Claim>: the fifth of the keyboard, a shade under the one and a half of two strings tuned until they stop beating.

  Every frequency in the sound is multiplied by that one ratio, and that is what makes this a change of pitch. A saw wave is a note and its harmonics, which stand at whole multiples of the note. After a shift of a fifth they still do: <Claim id="harmonics">the second and the third harmonic come out at 2.000 and 3.000 times the first, and at the places where adding the same number of hertz to each would have put them, what stands there is more than 90 dB under</Claim>. The device of <Lesson id="frequency-shifting" /> does that other thing. It adds one number of hertz to every frequency, the harmonics stop being multiples of anything, and a note turns into something nearer a bell. A pitch shifter multiplies, and a note stays a note.

  Three more things happen to the number on its way. <StageBlock tap="delayA" n={3} title="Where a head reads">
  A head is a distance: how far behind the newest sample it reads.   <M block t={String.raw`\varphi[n] = \varphi[n-1] + \at{sweep-phase}{\frac{1 - r}{W}}`} />

  and then its whole part is <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ShiftVoice.h" at="sweep_phase_ -= std::floor(sweep_phase_);">dropped</Src>, which is the jump. <M t="n" /> counts samples and <M t="W" /> is Size, in samples. Head A reads at

  <M block t={String.raw`d_A[n] = \at{sweep-delay}{d_0 + \varphi[n]\,W}`} />

  samples behind, and head B at the same with <M t={String.raw`\varphi + \tfrac{1}{2}`} /> in place of <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} />, wrapped round to stay under 1.

<StageBlock tap="match" n={6} title="Smooth: look before you splice">
  The formula of stage 5 says what a good splice needs: the two heads in step where they cross. Vintage leaves that to luck. Smooth looks.

  In Smooth a head lives for one Size under the same raised cosine, and a new one starts <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ShiftVoice.h" at="static constexpr float kSmoothHop = 0.5f;">every half Size</Src>, so two sound at a time as before. What changes is where the new head starts. The device takes what the old head is about to play, <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ShiftVoice.h" at="static constexpr float kSpanSeconds = 0.016f;">16 ms</Src> of it sampled at <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ShiftVoice.h" at="static constexpr int kPoints = 48;">48 points</Src>, and holds it against the ring at one place after another, through as much as <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ShiftVoice.h" at="static constexpr float kSearchSeconds = 0.02f;">20 ms</Src> further back. For each place, <M t={String.raw`\ell`} /> samples back, it works out how alike the two are:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\rho(\ell) = \at{splice-score}{\frac{\sum_i w_i\, a_i\, c_i(\ell)}{\sqrt{\sum_i w_i\, a_i^2 \;\sum_i w_i\, c_i(\ell)^2}}}`}
  />

  <M t="a_i" /> are the points of what the old head is playing, <M t={String.raw`c_i(\ell)`} /> the points of the ring at the place being tried, and <M t="w_i" /> a weight that is largest in the middle of the comparison and falls to nothing at its ends. The top of the fraction is large when the two rise and fall together. The bottom takes their loudness out, so that a loud passage does not win for being loud. <M t={String.raw`\rho`} /> is 1 when the two are the same shape, 0 when they have nothing to do with each other, and under 0 when one is the other upside down.

  The new head starts at the place with the highest <M t={String.raw`\rho`} />, and from then on both heads move along the ring at the same speed, so a match at the start is a match for the whole crossing.   ### When there is no good place

  The search can only find what is there. <Claim id="match-found">On the sine the match is more than 0.999 at every splice. On the noise it is 0.5 on average. On the strummed chords it is 0.9 on average, and at its least under 0.3</Claim>. Noise never repeats, so no place in it is like another. A chord is several notes, each with a cycle of its own, and the place that lines one of them up may leave another half a cycle out.

  Where the heads do not agree, the sum dips, as stage 5 showed, and the device knows by how much, because it has just measured the agreement. So it divides the dip out. With <M t="u_A" /> and <M t="u_B" /> what the two heads read, the voice is

  <M
    block

  Under the root is the formula of stage 5 with the match found standing where <M t={String.raw`\cos\Delta`} /> stood. The quarter is a limit: the voice is never lifted to more than twice its height, however badly the heads agree.

  Spread puts the two apart. Each voice is given a place <M t="p" /> between −1, the left, and 1, the right:

  <M block t={String.raw`p_A = \at{place-pan}{-\,S\,\min(1,\ b)}, \qquad p_B = \at{place-pan}{S}`} />

  <M t="S" /> is Spread and <M t="b" /> is Voice B. Voice B goes right by the whole of Spread. Voice A goes left only as far as there is a voice B to balance it, so a single voice stays in the middle wherever Spread stands.

  <Controls ids="pitchB levelB detune spread" />

  <Trace taps="panA panB" min={-1} max={1}>
    Where each voice stands, from −1 at the left to 1 at the right, worked back from the device's
    own gains. Raise Spread, and notice voice B's line go up at once and voice A's stay in the
    middle. Then raise Voice B, and notice voice A's line go down as far as Voice B has come up.
  </Trace>

  Measured on the sine, <Claim id="placed">with Spread at 1 and Voice B at 1, voice A is in the left channel alone and voice B in the right alone, each more than 100 dB under in the other channel, and with Voice B at nothing the one voice comes out equally in both</Claim>.

  A voice is stereo before it is placed, because the ring has two channels and each head reads both. The device splits each voice into its middle and its side, the sum and the difference of the two channels. The middle is placed with the kit's <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" at="inline void pan_gains(float pan, float* left, float* right) {">equal-power pan</Src>. The side is <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/pitch_shifter.h" at="side_[v].set(1.0f - away, !settle_);">turned down by as much as the voice is moved aside</Src>. A voice left in the middle keeps the whole stereo picture of what went in, and a voice moved all the way to one side has none left.

  <Listing
    slot="listing"
    path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/pitch_shifter.h"
    from="// A to the left by as much as B is there to balance it, B to the right."
    to="side_[v].set(1.0f - away, !settle_); }"
    name="place"
    at={{ pan: 'const float pan[2] = {-spread * kit::min(1.0f, level_b), spread};' }}
  />
</StageBlock>

<StageBlock tap="out" n={9} title="Mix">
  Last, the shifted sound is blended with the sound that went in. With <M t="x" /> the input, <M t="z" /> the voices as placed, and <M t="m" /> the Mix control:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y} = \at{mix-dry}{\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2} m\bigr)}\,\sig{in}{x} + \at{mix-wet}{\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2} m\bigr)}\,z`}
  />

  At 0 the cosine is 1 and the sine nothing: the input alone. At 1 it is the other way round. Half way, both are one over the square root of two, so that two sounds that have nothing to do with each other come out as loud together as either was alone. <Claim id="mix-zero">With Mix at 0 the output is the input, sample for sample</Claim>, and <Claim id="mix-half">at 0.5 the dry sine and the shifted sine each come out 3.0 dB down</Claim>, measured in Smooth, where the shifted sine is one line.

  <Controls ids="mix" />

  <Wave taps="in out" from={1} span={0.02}>
    A fiftieth of a second of the input and the output. With Mix all the way up the output is the
    shifted voice alone. Bring Mix down to the middle, and notice the output become the sum of
    the wave that went in and the faster one.
  </Wave>

  The two halves of the blend are not in time with each other. The shifted voice has been through the ring, and it is late by wherever its heads were reading. With no shift at all that is one fixed distance: <Claim id="unison-out">at 0 in Vintage the guitar comes out nearest its input 1,513 samples later, with −30 dB between the two</Claim>. That is the distance of stage 4 and one sample more, since the heads read before the newest sample is written. The two are no closer than that because the band limit and Tone have each turned the top of the sound a little on the way. Part 7 comes back to what that lateness does when the two halves are heard together.

  <Listing
    slot="listing"
    path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/pitch_shifter.h"
    from="const float mix = mix_.next();"
    to="out_right_[i] = in[1] * dry_gain_ + wet[1] * wet_gain_;"
    name="mix"
    at={{
      dry: 'dry_gain_ = kit::SineTable::cos_lookup(0.25f * mix);',
      wet: 'wet_gain_ = kit::SineTable::lookup(0.25f * mix);',
    }}
  />
  <Fragment slot="listen">Hear what comes out</Fragment>
</StageBlock>

### The other two modes

Mode has two more choices, and each is a lesson of its own elsewhere on this site.

**Grain** overlaps <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ShiftVoice.h" at="static constexpr int kGrainOverlap = 4;">four heads</Src> at a time where the other modes overlap two. Each lives for one Size under a raised cosine, and a new one starts every quarter of Size. That is the method of <Lesson id="granular" />, used here to shift. Four evenly spaced heads reading one steady tone are four copies of it at four evenly spaced delays, and whether they add or cancel depends on Size, exactly as with two. Jitter is the cure: it scatters when each grain starts, where it reads, its pitch by up to <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ShiftVoice.h" at="static constexpr float kGrainCents = 40.0f;">40 cents</Src> either way with Jitter all the way up, and its place between left and right, and grains that are scattered stop agreeing or disagreeing all together. <Claim id="grain-comb">With Jitter at 0 the sine comes out more than 13 dB quieter than it was recorded at a Size of 60 ms, and 4 dB louder at 45 ms. With Jitter at 0.2 it is less than 0.5 dB from its level at both</Claim>.

**Chords** has no heads. A chord is several notes sounding at once, and stage 6 showed what that does to a splice: no one place suits every note. So Chords works on the spectrum, with the transform of <Lesson id="fourier-transform" /> and the methods of <Lesson id="spectral-processing" />. It takes a frame of the ring, <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/PeakShifter.h" at="class PeakShifter {">finds the peaks of its spectrum</Src>, and moves each peak, with the points round it, to the ratio times its frequency. Then it turns the result back into sound. It <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ChordEngine.h" at="class ChordEngine {">does this twice over</Src>, with a long frame for everything under a split, which for intervals up to an octave stands at <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ChordEngine.h" at="static constexpr float kSplitHz = 1357.0f;">1,357 Hz</Src>, where the notes of a close chord lie too near each other for a short frame to tell apart, and a short frame for everything above.

Two notes at once show the difference. <Claim id="two-notes">Of two sines a major third apart, shifted up an octave, Smooth puts the upper one out 0.2 Hz flat and more than 2 dB under its level. Chords puts both out less than 0.01 Hz from their place and less than 0.1 dB from their level</Claim>.

The price is time, since a long frame has to be heard before it can be moved: the device's own notes say Chords answers <Src path="cpp/devices/pitch-shifter/ChordEngine.h" at="190 ms late.">about 190 ms late</Src>, and part 7 measures it. And a method that looks for peaks is at its best on sounds that have them. <Claim id="chords-noise">In Chords the noise comes out 3.9 dB under what was recorded and the drums 2.5 dB under, where the strummed chords and the pad stay within 0.5 dB</Claim>.

  <Way title="Thicken without transposing" tag="no interval, a little Detune, Spread" set="doubler">
    The commonest use of a pitch shifter changes no note. Two voices a few cents either side of the sound, one to each side, a short Delay behind it. The card is the doubler of part 3. After it, bring Detune down to nothing and listen to the width close up, then take it far up and listen for the point where two voices stop sounding like one thick one and start sounding out of tune.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A chord from one note" tag="two voices, two intervals" set="triad">

      slot="picture"

      set="triad"
      view="spectrum"

      min={-60}

    With both voices up and Mix half way, the dry note and two shifted copies make three. <Claim id="triad">On the sine the card puts out three lines, at 440 Hz and at 4 and 7 semitones over it, each 3.0 dB under the sine that went in</Claim>: a major chord. It is the same chord on every note played into it, which is right for one note held and wrong for most tunes, as part 3 said of harmonies.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Choose Mode by what is played" tag="Chords for chords" set="chordsMode">

<Part id="wrong" title="Six ways it goes wrong, four of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="A held note flutters" set="flutter">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="levelIn level"
      set="flutter"
      from={1}
      span={0.3}
      min={-48}
      max={0}
      caption="The level of the input and the level of voice A on this card, over three tenths of a second. The first lies flat. The second falls into a deep notch wherever the heads cross."
    />

    The splice of stage 5, at its worst. The card sets Size so that the two heads are exactly half a cycle of the sine apart, and at every crossing they cancel: <Claim id="fault-flutter">the sine falls more than 40 dB at each crossing, 32.6 times a second</Claim>. A real note is never this bad for long, because its harmonics are not all out of step at once, but a flute or a held voice in Vintage has a flutter of this kind in it. Smooth is the cure. Where Vintage is wanted for its sound, a longer Size makes the flutter slower, and small changes of Size move it on and off a given note.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Every hit comes out more than once" set="doubled">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="in voice"
      set="doubled"
      source="clicks"
      from={0.95}
      span={0.4}
      caption="One click of the clicks going in, and voice A after it, with this card's Size. One click goes in. A row of clicks comes out, evenly spaced and of different heights."
    />

    Shifting up, the heads read every moment of the sound more than once: that is where the length came from in part 1. On a held sound nobody can tell. A hit is a moment that cannot be mistaken, and each reading of it is heard. <Claim id="click-copies">At +12 with Size at 60 ms, Vintage plays one click 4 times, 15.0 ms apart</Claim>: twice by each head. That close, the copies smear into one dull hit. <Claim id="click-long">With Size at 400 ms the copies are 100 ms apart</Claim>, and a drum becomes a roll. The card is the drums at a Size between the two.

    Smooth does not help: <Claim id="click-smooth">it plays the click 4 times too</Claim>. Lining up the cycles of a wave does nothing for a moment that happens once. The cures are a short Size, a smaller interval, or a method that keeps hits apart from held sound, which this device does not have.

    Shifting down, the fault turns over. The heads now read each moment once and skip some. <Claim id="click-down">At −12 in Vintage no click is played more than once, and 2 of the 4 in the loop come out at less than a hundredth of their height</Claim>, because they fell just where a head was jumping and its gain was next to nothing. A hit can go missing.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A short Size buzzes" set="buzz">
    Size sets how often the heads splice, and a rate of splices is a frequency. When that rate is slow it is heard as flutter. When it is fast enough to be a pitch of its own, the voice has a buzz in it that does not move with the notes. <Claim id="buzz-rate">On this card each head jumps every 10 ms, and with the sine in place of the guitar the level dips 200 times a second</Claim>. The lines of stage 5 are now so far apart that the tallest is nowhere near the octave. Lengthen Size until the buzz slows to a flutter and then to nothing much.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Late, in Chords" set="late">
    Chords has to hear a long frame before it can move it. <Claim id="chords-late">A tone that starts from silence reaches half its height 193 ms after it began in Chords, where Smooth takes 25 ms</Claim>. The card plays drums through Chords with Mix half way, so each dry hit is followed by its shifted copy, and they are plainly two events. On a pad nobody hears it. On anything with a beat, use Smooth or Grain, or turn Mix all the way up and move the part earlier.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Dull, going up">
    The band limit of stage 2 takes off the top of whatever is shifted up, and the further up, the more it takes. <Claim id="band-two-octaves">At +24 a tone is recorded 3.0 dB down at 5.4 kHz</Claim>, and everything above that is fading fast. After the heads have multiplied it by four the voice reaches as high as it ever did, but it is made of what was the bottom quarter of the sound, and it has the thin shine of a recording played too fast. That is the method. The step of the build with the band limit shows what would be there in its place.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Hollow against the dry sound">
    A voice with little or no interval is nearly the dry sound over again, late by wherever its heads are reading, as stage 9 measured. With Mix half way the two add, and a sound added to a late copy of itself is a comb: some frequencies doubled, others cancelled. Because the heads move, the comb moves. That is the sweep of <Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" />, wanted there and an accident here. Raise Delay until the copy is late enough to be heard as a second voice, which is what the doubler does, or turn Mix all the way up.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A pitch shifter plays the sound faster.">
    It reads faster, and then reads again. Speed alone changes pitch and length together, as the fast tape of part 1 showed. The shifter keeps the length by playing every stretch of the sound more than once going up, and by leaving stretches out going down. Part 7 has the cases where the repeats and the gaps can be heard.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="An octave up is exactly twice the frequency.">
    In Smooth, on one steady note, it is. In Vintage the tone comes out as a row of lines counted from the frequency that went in, spaced by Size, and the tallest is the one that happens to lie nearest the octave. Stage 5 has the formula and the measurement. The pitch of the plain method has a little of Size in it.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Gains that add up to one keep the level.">
    They keep it when the two sounds under them are in step. Out of step, two waves add up to less than their heights, and with nothing in common they add in power, which sags in the middle of every crossing. This is the answer to the question of part 1, and stage 6 measures what the noise loses.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A longer Size is always cleaner.">
    It makes the splices rarer, so a held note flutters more slowly. It also puts the readings of each moment further apart in time, so a hit that was smeared becomes a hit that is plainly repeated. The second fault of part 7 measures both ends. Size is a choice between two faults, made for the sound in hand.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Smooth mends every splice.">
    Smooth finds the best place to splice, and the best place is only as good as the sound allows. On one note it is nearly perfect. On noise, and where the notes of a chord disagree, the match it finds is far from 1, as stage 6 shows, and what it does then is hold the level steady across a splice that can still be heard. And it reads a hit as many times as Vintage does.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A shifter set to a third gives a harmony in the key.">
    It adds the same number of semitones to every note. A harmony in a key needs a major third over some notes and a minor third over others, and nothing in this device knows which note is being played, let alone the key. That is why the uses of part 3 keep to octaves, fifths and fourths for whole lines, and why the chord of part 6 is the same chord on every note.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
