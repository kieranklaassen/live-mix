<Part id="why" title="One player is one sound. A section is not that sound made louder.">

A guitarist strums a chord and lets it ring. Recorded, it is one guitar. Twelve guitarists strumming the same chord are not that recording turned up. Anyone who has stood near a string section or a choir knows the difference, and knows it is not a difference of loudness.

What is in the sound of several players that the sound of one lacks, and can a machine put it there?

### Playing the recording twice does nothing

The obvious thing to try is a second copy of the recording, played along with the first. Two copies of the same samples add up to the same samples, each twice as large. That is a fader moved, and nothing else.

### A copy that comes late is worse

Real players do not start together, so make the copy late. The loop for this is a saw wave, a plain buzz that holds every harmonic of its note. Beside it is the same wave mixed in equal parts with a copy of itself a few thousandths of a second behind.

The late copy did not add a player. It took notes away. <Claim id="saw-odd">With the copy 4.55 ms late, the saw's fundamental at 110 Hz comes out more than 50 dB down, and its harmonics at 220 Hz and 440 Hz come out less than 0.1 dB from where they were</Claim>. What is left are the even harmonics, and they are the harmonics of the same buzz an octave higher. Hear the saw as it is <Hear set="plain" label="Hear the saw" /> and with the copy <Hear set="late" label="Hear it with a late copy" />.

The reason is in the timing of the two waves. Take any one frequency in the sound. If the copy is late by a whole number of that frequency's cycles, the copy and the original rise and fall together and add. If it is late by half a cycle, one is at its crest while the other is in its trough, and they cancel. How late the copy is decides which frequencies meet which fate, and the result is a spectrum with gaps at even distances, like the teeth of a comb. The thing that makes it is called a comb filter.

Down, to half the frequency. A notch falls where the delay is half a cycle long, and a cycle that takes twice as long belongs to a note an octave lower. <Claim id="notch-moves">With the delay at 5 ms the first notch stands at 100 Hz, and the rest at its odd multiples</Claim>.

So the delay time is the one number that sets the comb. A short delay puts a few notches high up and far apart. A long one packs many of them in from the bottom.

### What the players do that the copy does not

A comb that holds still is a tone control, and an odd one. It is not a second player. A second player is never late by a fixed amount. They drift ahead and fall behind, a little, all the time, and they are never quite in tune with the first. A copy whose lateness changes gets both at once. While its lateness shrinks it is catching up, so the recording comes out a little faster than it was played, which is a little sharp. While its lateness grows the recording comes out a little slow, and flat.

So the thing to try next is a delay that moves.

<Still
  taps="voice1Ms voice2Ms voice3Ms"
  caption="How late each of three copies is, across the four seconds of the loop, in the engine's Chorus as it starts. No copy holds still, and no two are late by the same amount for long."
/>

Here are strummed chords with three copies that hold still <Hear set="chordsLate" label="Hear the copies held still" /> and with the same three moving as the picture shows <Hear set="chordsMoving" label="Hear the copies move" />. Listen for the first being one guitar in a small hard room, and for whether the second sounds like more than one.

A short delay that moves, mixed with the sound it was made from, is the whole machine, and it has two settings with names. Kept very short and read once, its comb has a few broad teeth, and the thing to listen for is the comb sliding. That is flanging. The name is from tape: two machines played the same recording, and an engineer slowed one with a finger on the rim of its reel, the flange. Made several times longer and read at two or three places at once, the comb's teeth crowd together, and the thing to listen for is the drift in time and pitch. That is a chorus.

The rest of this lesson builds a flanger a piece at a time, then opens the engine's Flanger and its Chorus. The delay line itself, and how it is read between two samples, belong to <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" />, and are taken as given here.

</Part>

Check this against the picture. Leave Delay where it starts, at <Src path="cpp/devices/flanger/device.json" at={'"default": 2.5,'}>2.5 ms</Src>, and find the first three notches. <Claim id="notch-first">The formula of part 5 puts them at 200 Hz, 600 Hz and 1,000 Hz. Measured on white noise through the stopped Flanger, they fall at 199 Hz, 598 Hz and 996 Hz</Claim>, each as close as that measurement's points allow. The first notch is the note whose half cycle is as long as the delay, and the others are its odd multiples, on up without end.

All of it can be worked by hand from the delay. <Claim id="notch-even">Half a cycle in 2.5 ms is a whole cycle in 5 ms, and a cycle of 5 ms is a note of 200 Hz. Each notch after it fits one more whole cycle into the delay, so neighbours stand one over the delay apart: 400 Hz, at every step up</Claim>. Even steps in hertz are what make it a comb. A phaser's notches are few, and each gap between them is wider than the one below. The two are set side by side in stage 3 of <Lesson id="phasing" />.

The picture is drawn at fixed points along the bottom, so a notch shows only as deep as the nearest point happens to land in it. At equal parts the true notch goes all the way down, as part 5 measures with a single tone.

Nothing about the machine changes between a flanger and a chorus except how long the delay is and how many times the line is read. The names go with what there is to listen for. With one broad notch, listen for it sliding: that moving hollow is the flange. With twelve narrow ones moving at once, listen for copies of the sound and not for a filter: that is the chorus. Longer still, listen for the copy as a second strike, which players call a slap: that is the echo of <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" />, arriving from the short end.

Where one becomes the next is for the ear to judge, and no test on this page measures it. <Part id="uses" title="Five kinds of sound, and which of the two to reach for.">

Both effects need a sound with something in it to work on. A comb can only take out what is there, so a flanger says most on sounds that fill the spectrum: noise, cymbals, a pad, a whole mix. A chorus needs a steady pitch to bend, so it says most on notes that are held or left to ring.

Each card below shows a sound and a setting to start from, lists that setting, and plays it. A card's button moves the whole page to its setting, so every figure further down shows it too, and the play bar offers the way back to where you were.

<Part id="build" title="Start with a delay. Move it. Mix it. Feed it back. Read it more than once.">

The quickest way to understand a flanger is to make one. This part starts with a delay and adds four pieces to it, one at a time. After each piece the pictures are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

The first four pieces are the engine's Flanger. The fifth is the step from a flanger towards a chorus.

    A delay that holds still is only lateness. This piece moves the read point with an oscillator: a slow wave that runs between −1 and +1 and pushes the point either side of the Delay setting.   <M block t={String.raw`\rho[n] = 1 - \bigl(\sig{ctl}{d[n]} - \sig{ctl}{d[n-1]}\bigr)`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`1200\,\log_2 \rho[n]`} />

### The Chorus: the same drawing, longer, and read by several voices

The Chorus is a device of its own. Part 9 has the Flanger's plate, and under it a table of the Chorus's controls. It has eight. Voices is <Src path="cpp/devices/chorus/device.json" at={'"choices": ["2", "3"],'}>two or three</Src>. Rate is the oscillator's, as the Flanger's is, and starts at <Src path="cpp/devices/chorus/device.json" at={'"default": 0.8,'}>0.8 Hz</Src>. Depth is how far each voice swings, and Delay is the centre they swing about, which starts at <Src path="cpp/devices/chorus/device.json" at={'"default": 12,'}>12 ms</Src>. <StageBlock tap="voice" n={7} title="One voice is a slow vibrato">
  Each voice is a delayed copy whose delay moves, so by stage 3 each is played a little fast and then a little slow. In the Chorus that is the point of the device. The swing is larger than the Flanger's and so is the Rate, and the speed depends on both.

  <Controls ids="chorusRate chorusDepth" />

  <Trace taps="cents1:ctl cents2:alt cents3:alt2" unit="cents">
    The pitch of each voice against the pitch that went in. Each is furthest out where its delay,
    in the figure of stage 6, crosses the centre, and in tune where its delay turns round. Try
    Chorus rate higher, and notice every line grow by as much as Chorus depth would make it.
  </Trace>

  At any instant one voice is sharp while another is flat. Several copies, none in tune with the input or with each other for long, are the machine's stand-in for several players on one part.

  At the Chorus's starting settings, read from the slope of the delay the device used, each voice goes <Claim id="chorus-cents">21.6 cents sharp and 21.9 cents flat</Claim>. The two differ because cents are a logarithm, and a logarithm is not even about 1. Measured from the sound itself, <Claim id="chorus-cents-heard">a 1 kHz sine comes back from one voice at anything from 987.4 Hz to 1,012.6 Hz</Claim>, counted from its zero crossings.

  A voice whose read point lies between two samples is an interpolation, and interpolation loses a little at the top of the band, as <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" /> shows. On white noise a moving voice of the Chorus comes out <Claim id="between-samples">0.9 dB quieter than it went in, and one held on a whole sample loses less than 0.01 dB</Claim>.

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\max_n\,\bigl|\,d_v[n] - d_v[n-1]\,\bigr| = \frac{2\pi R\,A}{f_s}`}
  />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    This is the steepest a sine-shaped delay gets, which is where it crosses its centre. The left side is the most the delay changes in one sample, the quantity that stage 3 turned into a speed. On the right, <M t="R" /> is the Rate and <M t="A" /> the swing, and they multiply: doubling either doubles the bend. The length of the delay, <M t="D" />, is not in it at all.
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="listen">Hear one voice alone</Fragment>
</StageBlock>

<StageBlock tap="chorusWet" n={8} title="The voices are added, and scaled">
  The voices are added into one delayed sound for each side, and the sum is scaled so that two voices and three come out equally loud.

  <Wave taps="voice:alt chorusWet:out" from={1} span={0.008} fit>
    Eight thousandths of a second of the loop: one voice alone, and all the voices added and scaled. Try
    Chorus depth at 0, and notice the sum become the one voice made larger, since every voice then
    reads the same place.
  </Wave>

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{w_c[n]} = \at{sets-norm}{\frac{1}{\sqrt N}} \sum_{v} \at{read-pan}{p_{v,c}}\; x_c\bigl[n - d_{v,c}[n]\bigr]`}
  />

  <M t="c" /> is the side, left or right. <M t={String.raw`w_c`} /> is the delayed sound of that side, the Chorus's counterpart of the Flanger's one copy. Each voice reads its side's line at its own point and is weighed by a gain <M t={String.raw`p_{v,c}`} />, which stage 9 explains. Then the sum is scaled by one over the square root of the number of voices.

  The square root is there because voices that have drifted apart are different signals, and different signals add in power, not in size. On white noise, voices at the starting depth sum to the level of one, <Claim id="apart">less than 0.05 dB</Claim> from it, with two voices and with three. With Chorus depth at 0 and Spread at nothing, the three are one signal on each side and add in size, to <Claim id="together">4.8 dB over one</Claim>.

  That is why the Chorus's combs in part 2 had shallow notches. Held still, the delayed side outweighs the dry side, and the two cannot cancel completely.

<StageBlock tap="chorus" n={9} title="Two sides, and the mix">
  Spread makes the two sides differ, in two ways at once.

  It places the voices. The first goes towards the left and the last towards the right, and a middle one stays in the centre. The gains are the usual pair for placing a sound without changing its power:

  <M block t={String.raw`p_{v,\text{left}} = \at{geometry-left}{\sqrt 2\,\cos\theta_v}`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`p_{v,\text{right}} = \at{geometry-right}{\sqrt 2\,\sin\theta_v}`} />

  <M t={String.raw`\theta_v`} /> is the angle of voice <M t="v" />. In the centre it is an eighth of a turn, where the cosine and the sine are equal and both gains are 1. Spread carries the first voice from there towards no turn at all, where only the left gain is left, and the last voice towards a quarter turn, where only the right is.

  And it sets the right side's oscillator ahead of the left's, by a quarter of a cycle at full Spread, so that even a voice in the centre reads a different point on each side. <Claim id="right-ahead">At a Spread of 70 % and a Chorus rate of 0.8 Hz, the first voice's read point rises through its centre 0.219 s sooner on the right side than on the left, which is 63° of the cycle</Claim>.

  <Controls ids="spread chorusMix" />

  <Trace taps="voice1Ms:ctl voice1MsRight:out voice2MsRight:alt" unit="ms">
    Voice 1's read point on the left and on the right, and voice 2's on the right. Try Spread at
    its lowest, and notice the first two lines become one. Then try it at its highest, and notice the
    right side's line pull a quarter of a cycle ahead.
  </Trace>

  With two voices and full Spread <Claim id="one-each">each ear gets one voice to itself</Claim>, the first on the left and the second on the right. The second voice is half a cycle from the first, and the right side's oscillator is a quarter of a cycle ahead, so the two do not mirror each other: the right ear's read point follows the left's <Claim id="two-sides">a quarter of a cycle behind, which is 0.31 s at a Chorus rate of 0.8 Hz</Claim>, and when one copy is furthest off pitch the other is in tune. With Spread at nothing the two sides are read at the same points with the same gains, so a sound that is the same on both sides comes out of both as <Claim id="spread-zero">the same samples</Claim>. <Claim id="spread-apart">On white noise that is the same on both sides, from a Spread of 10 % up, the delayed halves of the two sides share next to nothing: their correlation is under 0.03</Claim>.

  The mix is the Flanger's, the formula of stage 4 with this stage's delayed sound in it. It dips, and for the reason stage 8 gave: dry noise and delayed noise are different signals. With Chorus mix at 0.5, white noise comes out <Claim id="mix-dip">3.4 dB under</Claim> what went in.

  Each voice still makes a comb with the dry sound. The teeth are close, and there are several combs moving several ways, so no single one can be followed. Stop them, with Chorus depth at 0, and the voices fall on top of one another into one comb: <Claim id="chorus-teeth">twelve notches under 1 kHz, 83 Hz apart, the first found at 41 Hz, where the formula of stage 4 puts it at 42 Hz</Claim>, less than a point of that measurement away.

<Part id="wrong" title="Seven ways it goes wrong, four of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="All the way wet, and the flange is gone" set="allWet">
    Mix at its highest plays the delayed copy alone. There is nothing for it to cancel against, so there is no comb and no sweep: stage 4 measures the line flat. What is left is the copy with its few cents of wobble. If a flanger seems to be doing nothing, look at Mix first. The deepest notches are at one half.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A chorus that does not move" set="stuck">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="in chorus"
      set="stuck"
      view="spectrum"
      scale="linear"
      top={1000}
      min={-60}
      max={-20}
      caption="The pad going in, and coming out of the Chorus on this card's setting, from 0 Hz to 1 kHz. Some of its harmonics come out lower than they went in and some higher: the ones that fall in the comb's notches, and the ones on its tops."
    />

    With no depth, or with a Rate so low that nothing seems to move, the Chorus is a comb with close teeth, and stage 9 counts them. A held note that lands in a notch drops back, and one on a top comes forward. <Claim id="fault-stuck">On this card's pad the harmonic at 129 Hz, beside the comb's second notch, comes out 8 dB lower than it went in, and the one at 164 Hz, on the top next to it, 2 dB higher</Claim>. Listen for the pad turning hard and boxy. What makes it a chorus is the motion: raise Chorus depth.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Too fast and too deep" set="seasick">
    Rate and depth multiply, as stage 7 shows, and it is easy to ask for more bend than any group of players would make. <Claim id="fault-seasick">On this setting each voice goes 252.6 cents sharp and 295.8 cents flat</Claim>, more than a whole tone either way. Bring one of the two down. A fast chorus wants a shallow depth, and a deep one wants a slow Rate.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Feedback at the top" set="ringing">
    Near the end of its range the loop gives back nearly all that it is given, and every hit sets the comb's note ringing. <Claim id="fault-ring">On this setting, with Feedback at 95 %, a single sample is still coming back more than 0.4 s later at a thousandth of the size of its first return, which is 60 dB under it, wherever in the sweep it goes in</Claim>. The limiter of stage 5 caps how large the ring can grow and does not stop it. Lower Feedback until the ring is shorter than the gap between the hits.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A jump in the delay">
    A read point that jumps splices two bits of sound together, and that is a click. The Flanger keeps it from happening in both places it could. The oscillator's jumps pass through the following of stage 2, and Delay and Depth glide when they are moved. So a quick move of Delay bends the pitch for a moment and does not click. The bend is the price, and it is the lesser fault.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The copy is duller than the sound">
    Reading between two samples loses a little at the top of the band, and a moving read point is nearly always between two samples. Stage 7 measures the loss on one voice. It is also why the comb of stage 4 parts from its formula a little when Delay is not a whole number of samples. It is small, and it is the cost of a delay that can stand anywhere.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Quieter with the effect on">
    Dry sound and delayed sound that are not alike add in power, so a plain blend of the two comes out lower than either. Stage 9 measures the dip on noise through the Chorus, and stage 4 the half of the power that a comb takes out. Neither device makes it up. Expect to raise the level after it, and compare the two at matched loudness.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A flanger and a chorus are two different machines.">
    They are one drawing: a delay line, read at a moving point and mixed with the sound that went in. What differs is how long the delay is and how many times the line is read. The three pictures of part 2 are one measurement at three delay times, and the last step of part 4 turns the first towards the second by adding read points.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Feedback makes the notches deeper.">
    It fills them. With no Feedback and equal parts a notch goes to nothing. Feedback strengthens the delayed path at the comb's tops and weakens it in the notches, so the two paths no longer match there. Stage 5 has the measurement, and its picture shows the tops turn to spikes while the notches rise.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A chorus puts its copies out of tune by a fixed amount.">
    Each voice is sharp, then in tune, then flat, once in every cycle. A copy that stayed a fixed amount sharp would need a delay that shrank for ever. The trace of stage 7 shows every voice crossing the line of no bend twice a cycle, and no two crossing it together.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Depth is how far off pitch the copies go.">
    The bend is the slope of the delay, and the slope is the Rate times the swing. The formula of stage 7 has both in it and multiplies them. Double Chorus rate and leave Chorus depth alone, and the trace of that stage doubles.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The comb sweeps the way the oscillator moves.">
    The oscillator moves the delay, and a notch's frequency goes as one over the delay. A sine in delay is not a sine in frequency. The notches hurry and fan out where the delay is short, and crawl and close up where it is long. The measurement under the spectrogram of stage 4 has the lowest notch resting at the first notch of part 2, and swinging nearly three times as far above that as below it.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Three voices are louder than two.">
    The sum is scaled by one over the square root of the number of voices, so that voices which have drifted apart come out as loud as one of them, whether there are two or three. Stage 8 measures it on noise. Only where the voices coincide, with no depth, do three add up to more than two.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
