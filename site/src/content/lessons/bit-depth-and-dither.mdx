<Part id="why" title="A sample has to be written down, and there are only so many numbers to write it with.">

A guitar chord is struck and left to ring. While it is inside the engine, each sample of it is a number with a fraction, as fine as anyone could ask for. Sooner or later it has to be written into something smaller: a file, a converter, a memory that must hold a minute of sound in little room. There a sample is a whole number, and the format says how many bits that number has. Sixteen bits count to <Src path="cpp/devices/echo-memory/memory_store.h" at="static constexpr float kScale = 32767.0f;">32,767</Src> either side of zero, and no further.

So each sample is moved to the nearest level that can be written. That is rounding, and the distance a sample is moved is an error. With sixteen bits the levels lie close together and the error is small. This lesson starts with 6 bits, where the levels are far apart, so that the error can be seen and heard. Everything it shows happens at sixteen bits too, lower down.

Levels on this page are in decibels below full scale, the largest number a sample can be. <Lesson id="level-and-loudness" cap /> says what a decibel is.

Listen to the chords as played, and then written in 6 bits. Listen to the end of each ring. <Hear set="played" label="Hear the chords as played" /> <Hear set="ring" label="Hear them in 6 bits" />

The ring does not fade. It breaks up, and then it is gone. Measured on that render, <Claim id="ring-stops">72 % of the samples that come out are exactly zero, and from 1.43 s until the second chord is struck, 583 ms later, the output is exact silence</Claim>. The chord itself is still there all through that stretch: <Claim id="ring-there">the level of the input reads −44.7 dB at 1.5 s and −51.4 dB at 1.95 s</Claim>. It has only become smaller than half a step, and every sample that small is nearer to zero than to any other level.

So how can a sound be written in a few bits without the quiet part of it breaking up and stopping?

### The error is made of the music

Take the rounded output and subtract what went in. What is left is the error: everything the rounding added to the sound. The next picture is its level over the loop, beside the level of the chords.

<Still
  taps="inLevel errorLevel"
  set="ring"
  caption="The level of the chords and the level of the error. While a chord is many steps tall the error holds one height. As the ring falls under a step, the error falls with it and then lies on top of it: the output is nothing, so the error is the whole chord, turned over."
/>

Over the loop <Claim id="ring-follows">the level of the error moves through 12.4 dB</Claim>, up at each strike and down along each ring. An error that rises and falls with the notes is not a hiss behind the music. It is made out of the music, sample by sample, and it comes and goes with it. Listen for a roughness that rides on the chord and dies with it. <Hear set="ringError" label="Hear the error alone" />

### The obvious answer fails

Use more bits. Each bit more halves the step, so the levels move closer together and the ring has more of them to use on its way down.

It helps, and it does not cure anything. <Claim id="more-bits">At 8 bits, 31 % of the samples of the same chords are still exactly zero, and the level of the error still moves through 2.5 dB</Claim> with the notes. The breaking up has moved further down the ring. It moves down again with every bit, and it is still there at the bottom, because whatever the step is, a ring that dies away will pass under it. And the number of bits is seldom yours to choose. A file format has the bits it has.

Nothing at all. <Claim id="small-gone">With Level in at −30 dB the 440 Hz tone of this page stands 0.25 of a step tall at 6 bits, and what comes out is exact silence</Claim>. Now add a little noise to the tone before it is rounded, and nothing else. <Hear set="smallDithered" label="Hear it with noise added first" />

The tone is back. <Claim id="small-kept">Its peaks come out at −42 dB, less than 0.1 dB from where they went in, which is a level of −45 dB, inside a hiss whose level reads −36.1 dB</Claim>. The hiss is louder than the tone, and the tone is inside it at its own size. Listen for it under the hiss.

No single sample holds the tone, since each one is still zero or one step up or one step down. The noise lifts some samples over the half step, and where the tone is near its peak it lifts more of them. The tone is in how often the output is up and how often it is down.

### The fix is to add noise first

That noise has a name: dither. It is a small random number added to each sample just before the rounding. It looks like the wrong direction, since it makes the error larger. What it changes is what the error is made of.

<Still
  taps="inLevel errorLevel ditheredLevel"
  set="ring"
  caption="The level of the chords, of the error of plain rounding, and of the error with dither. The error with dither is a level line across the whole loop. It stands above the other error, and it does not move with the chords."
/>

On the chords at 6 bits, <Claim id="ring-dithered">the level of the error with dither stays within 0.6 dB all through the loop, at −36.1 dB</Claim>. That is higher than the error of plain rounding ever was. In exchange it has stopped following the notes: it is a hiss that is the same under a strike, under a ring and under silence. And the ring no longer stops. It fades down into the hiss, as a quiet sound fades into the noise of a room. <Hear set="ringDithered" label="Hear the chords with dither" />

The engine rounds in more places than one, and this lesson opens two of them. Its Vintage Digital device rounds on purpose and with no dither, for the sound of it. Every rounded sound on this page is that device's output. Its Echo Memory device keeps what it remembers in sixteen bits, and dithers on the way in, which nothing else in the engine does. Every sound with dither on this page comes from the function that writes that memory, called on the loop with the sound scaled so that one of its steps stands for one step of Bits. Its dither is of one kind, one random number less another, and what this page measures is true of that kind. A third idea, bending the hiss towards the frequencies where it is noticed least, is in the engine nowhere. Part 4 writes it for this page, and says so where it does.

</Part>

The diagonal is a wire, where every level comes out as it went in. Rounding is a stair laid along the diagonal. Every level that falls on one tread comes out as the same number.

Check this against the picture. Set Bits to 4 and Companding to Linear, and find 0.3 on the bottom edge. Go up to the stair and across. <Claim id="stairs-reads">It comes out as 0.25, and there are 8 risers between 0 and 1</Claim>. Each bit doubles the number of risers. At the 6 bits this page starts on there are four times as many, and at sixteen there are thousands, each too small to draw.

The first thing to keep from this picture is the tread that runs through zero. Everything within half a step of zero, either side, comes out as zero. That tread is why the tone of part 1 vanished, and why a ring that dies away comes to a stop.

The second is the gap between the stair and the diagonal. That gap is the error. It is at most half a step, and it depends on where on its tread the level happens to fall. The error is worked out from the sound, one sample at a time, with nothing random in it.

The third is what the picture leaves out, which is time. It says what one level is written as. It does not say that a sound made of thousands of samples drags its error along with it, as the pictures of part 1 showed. Dither cannot be drawn here at all: with dither, one level going in comes out as one of several, and which one changes from sample to sample.

</Part>

<StageBlock tap="dither" n={5} title="The dither: one random number less another">
  The one place where the engine adds noise to a sound before rounding it is the memory of its Echo Memory device. That device records what passes through it into a store of 16-bit frames and plays it back later, sometimes much later. A store that rounded plainly would give every quiet sound it remembered the broken tail of part 1. So the function that writes each frame adds a random number first.

  The number is drawn in a particular way: two random numbers, each spread evenly from nothing to one step, and one subtracted from the other.

    zero, and one random number less another. The first is level across one step. The second is a
    triangle two steps wide and tallest at zero.

In the listing they are the low half and the high half of one 32-bit draw. <Part id="ways" title="Five habits of working with a fixed number of bits.">

Each of these is a habit, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the stair and the errors are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Dither once, at the last step" tag="in the final rounding, and only there" set="master">
    Every rounding with dither leaves its hiss behind, and hisses add up. A sound that is rounded three times with dither carries three of them. So work at more bits than the result will have, and dither once, where the final file is written. Inside this engine there is nothing to dither along the way. Its samples are floats from one end to the other, and the one device that keeps sound in 16 bits, in the memory of stage 5, dithers by itself.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Use the stair you have" tag="level before bits" set="low">
    A sound that sits low on the stair is written with few levels, as if bits had been taken away. <Claim id="way-level">The guitar at 8 bits stands 28.9 dB over the error of plain rounding with Level in at 0 dB, and 5.9 dB over it at −24 dB</Claim>. The card plays the low one. After it, raise Level in and listen for the grit falling away. Getting a sound up the stair before it is rounded costs nothing, and no dither gives back what a low level threw away.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Give the quiet end smaller steps" tag="Companding on Mu-law" set="mulaw">
    The same low guitar on the uneven stair of stage 3. <Claim id="way-mulaw">On Mu-law it stands 33 dB over the error at −24 dB, and 38 dB over it at 0 dB</Claim>: nearly the same at both levels, where the Linear stair of the card before lost most of its margin. The trade shows at the top of the range. <Claim id="mulaw-top">A full-scale sine at 997 Hz at 8 bits stands 50 dB over the error on the Linear stair and 38 dB over it on Mu-law</Claim>. Mu-law spends its levels on the quiet end, which is why it was used to carry a spoken voice in few bits. Its error still follows the sound, so it is a different stair, and no replacement for dither.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Move the hiss to where it is noticed least" tag="noise shaping, written for this lesson" set="lifted">
    The card plays the chords at 8 bits through the shaper of stage 7. Compare it with the first card of part 3, which is the same chords at the same bits with dither alone. The hiss measures higher here and lies further up. Whether that is the better bargain is for your ear, on the speakers you have. Shaping suits a file that is finished. A sound that is worked on afterwards is rounded again at the end, and that rounding lays a level hiss of its own over the shaped one.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Use the steps as a sound" tag="few bits, a lowered Rate, no dither" set="grit">
    Take the bits down until the error is part of the sound, and lower Rate so that each value is held. The error then rises with every hit and is gone between them, which is the opposite of a hiss. After the card, raise Bits one at a time and listen for the point where the grit stops being heard as part of the drum.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Five ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="The tail turns to grit, then stops" set="ring">
    The fault this lesson opened on. A sound that dies away passes down through the last few levels, is written with two of them, then one, then none, and the error is the sound itself all the way down. More bits move it lower and leave it there. Dither is the cure, and the last button of part 1 plays these chords with it.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A quiet sound is not there at all" set="small">
    Anything smaller than half a step is written as silence, because zero is the nearest level to every sample of it. The card plays the tone of part 1, a quarter of a step tall, and what it plays is nothing. A reverb tail, the room at the end of a take and the breath before a note are all sounds of this size at some number of bits. Dither keeps them. The price is the next card.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Hiss where there was silence" set="hiss">
    Dither does not know whether there is any sound. <Claim id="fault-hiss">Rounded alone at 6 bits, the longest gap between the drums comes out as exact silence for 240 ms. With dither that gap holds a hiss at −36 dB</Claim>. The card uses 6 bits so that the hiss is easy to listen for. The answer is not to leave the dither out. It is to round only once, at as many bits as the format allows, so that the hiss is the lowest thing in the file.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Past the top of the stair">
    A stair ends. The device's stair goes a little past full scale and no further: <Claim id="over-top">handed a level of 1.25, its rounding function gives 1.125</Claim>, the <Src path="cpp/devices/vintage-digital/vintage_digital.h" at="static constexpr float kCodeLimit = 1.125f;">limit of its codes</Src>, and everything above that is cut off flat. A sound played into the running device does not get that far. The device's <Src path="cpp/devices/vintage-digital/vintage_digital.h" at="static constexpr float kKnee = 0.06f;">input stage</Src> bends a level that is over full scale before the stair gets it: <Claim id="device-top">a steady 1.25 played into the device at 6 bits comes out as 1.0625</Claim>. The store of stage 5 stops at <Src path="cpp/devices/echo-memory/memory_store.h" at="if (scaled > 32767.0f) scaled = 32767.0f;">its largest number</Src> in the same way. No dither helps with this. Keep the peaks under the top before the rounding, with the meters of <Lesson id="level-and-loudness" />.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A file written with no dither">
    The engine's own writer of sound files turns each float into a whole number with <Src path="src/core/render/encode.ts" at="return Math.round(clamped * scale)">one rounding and no dither</Src>, and it writes <Src path="src/core/render/encode.ts" at="const bitDepth = options.bitDepth ?? 16">16 bits unless it is asked for more</Src>. That is the plain rounding of this lesson, far lower down. <Claim id="file-fade">The lab's test hands the writer a tone that dies away and asks for nothing: every sample of it under half a step is written as zero, and the end of the fade is exact silence</Claim>, as the ring of part 1 ended. When a file from this engine is going to be worked on again, ask the writer for more bits, or for floats, which it also writes.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Five things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="More bits give a sound more detail.">
    Bits set the size of the error and nothing else. With the dither of this page, what comes out is the sound that went in and a hiss, and each bit puts the hiss lower by the amount stage 4 measures. Nothing above the hiss is missing at any number of bits. The tone of part 1 was written whole at 6.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Dither covers the distortion up with noise.">
    Nothing is covered. The harmonics of stage 4 are not sitting under the hiss in stage 6. The lab's test looks for the strongest of them in the output and finds no more there than at the frequencies beside it, and the second still of stage 6 shows a level line where the row of lines was. Dither changes what the error is made of, from copies of the sound to noise.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Nothing smaller than one step can be written.">
    No single sample can hold it. A run of samples can. The steady level of part 4 came out with its average right to three places while every sample of it was wrong, and the tone of part 1, a quarter of a step tall, came out at the level it went in.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Noise shaping lowers the noise.">
    It raises it. The claim under the equations of stage 7 has the number: more error over the whole band than dither alone. What shaping lowers is the part of the hiss at the low end of the picture, and it pays for that with more at the top.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Plain rounding always sounds like distortion.">
    On a sound that crosses many levels the error of plain rounding is already close to a steady hiss. <Claim id="myth-steady">On the chords at 12 bits its level holds within 0.5 dB over the whole loop, where the level of the error with dither holds within 0.6 dB</Claim>. The trouble is at the bottom of the stair: the fade, the tail, the quiet tone. That is where a file is judged, and it is what dither is for.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
