<Part id="why" title="A wave says when. A spectrum says which notes. A tune is both.">

Here is a guitar line, four seconds of picked notes. Suppose you want to know which note is sounding one second in.

The wave of the loop shows when each note is struck: a tall edge, then a slope as the string dies. It does not show what the note is. Pitch lies in how fast the wave swings, and at this scale the swings are far too close together to see.

<Still
  taps="in"
  caption="The whole loop against time. Each note begins with a tall edge and dies away. Notice that nothing here says which note is which."
/>

<Lesson id="fourier-transform" cap /> gave the other view. Take a stretch of sound, ask how much of each frequency is in it, and the answer is a spectrum. Ask it of the whole loop, and every note the guitar played stands in it as a peak with its harmonics above it.

<Still
  taps="in"
  view="spectrum"
  caption="The spectrum of the same four seconds. Every note of the line is in here somewhere. Notice that nothing says when any of them sounded, or in what order."
/>

So the question has no answer in either picture. The wave has a clock and no pitch. The spectrum has pitch and no clock.

The way out is to stop asking about the whole loop. Cut a short stretch out of it, a few hundredths of a second long, and ask the question of that stretch alone.

<Still
  taps="bins"
  view="spectrum"
  caption="The spectrum of one short stretch, taken 1.1 seconds into the loop. One note is sounding there, and the picture holds that note alone: a lowest peak and its harmonics at even steps above it."
/>

That is one stop. Slide the stretch along a little and ask again, and again, to the end of the loop. Stand each spectrum on end, set them side by side in the order they were taken, and paint each level as a shade.

<Still
  taps="picture"
  view="spectrogram"
  caption="Every stretch's spectrum, side by side: seconds across, hertz up. Each note is a ladder of lines that begins all at once and fades from the top down. Notice that this one picture answers both questions."
/>

That picture is a spectrogram, and the method is the short-time Fourier transform. A stretch is called a frame.

The method gives more than a picture. Each frame's spectrum can be turned back into samples, and because the frames overlap, adding them up where they came from returns the sound that went in. In between, a device can change the spectra: turn one narrow band down and leave the band beside it alone. That is how a device reaches one harmonic of a chord without touching its neighbours. The opening picture of this page is the loop going in and what Glimmer, a device of the engine that works on frames, makes of it.

Two prices are paid, and both are fixed the moment the length of a frame is chosen. The first is delay: a frame cannot be transformed until all of it has arrived. The second is in the picture itself.

<Part id="uses" title="Sounds that hold still take it well. Sounds that strike need help.">

The transform alone changes nothing. What it is used on depends on what is done to the frames before they are added back. On this page that is Glimmer, which turns every narrow band of the spectrum up and down on a slow clock of its own, so that a sound flickers from inside. The cards play it on four kinds of sound. Each picture is the output as a spectrogram, and the gaps in it are where a band has gone dark.

A gain on those bins moves that partial and nothing else. <Part id="build" title="Five pieces: a frame, a window, hops, overlap and add, a change.">

The transform is put together here a piece at a time, in the lab's own code, at the frame Glimmer uses: <Src path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h" at="using Stft = kit::Stft<2048, 512>;">2,048 samples, a new one every 512</Src>. Each step adds one small function. The sound of every step is real, so press "Hear it so far" as you go. Two of the steps sound wrong, and they are meant to: each is what the next piece is for.

"Frame at" is the one control until the last step. It picks which frame of the loop the pictures of a single frame show.

<Build>
  <Step title="One frame" controls="at">
    Begin with the least that could be called a transform. Wait for 2,048 samples. Cut them out as they are. Turn them into bins, and the bins back into samples. Lay the result down where it was cut from. Then do the same with the next 2,048, so that the frames lie end to end.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/short-time-fourier.cpp"
      from="// One frame: the newest 2,048 samples, cut out as they are."
      to="for (int n = 0; n < kBuildFrame; ++n) sum[static_cast<uint32_t>(i + kBuildWait + n) & kBuildMask] += re[n];"
      mark={['void cut(int i) {', 'void transform() { build_fft.forward(re, im); }', 'void back() {', 'void lay(int i) {']}
      name="frame"
    />

    The transform and its inverse undo one another, so nothing is lost. The loop is <Claim id="end-to-end">93 frames</Claim> long, and what they add up to is the sound that went in, <Claim id="built-wire">2,304 samples late, with the difference between the two more than 130 dB under the sound</Claim>. The delay is the wait for a frame to fill.

    So far this is a slow wire. The trouble is in what a frame's bins hold. Choose the sine in the row of sounds: one frequency and nothing else. Cut square, the frame begins and ends wherever the wave happens to be. The transform treats a frame as one turn of something that goes round for ever, and the jump from the frame's end back to its beginning is in no sine. It takes every frequency to draw a jump, so the one frequency leaks into all the bins: ten bins from its peak the sine still reads <Claim id="leak-square">only 32 dB under it</Claim>.

  </Step>
  <Step title="A window" controls="at">
    The leak comes from the frame's ends, so take the ends away. Multiply the frame by a curve that starts at nothing, rises to 1 in the middle and falls back to nothing. This one is the Hann window, half a turn of a cosine stood on end, which <Lesson id="fourier-transform" /> used for the same reason.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/short-time-fourier.cpp"
      from="// A window: the frame swells from nothing and dies back to nothing."
      to="for (int n = 0; n < kBuildFrame; ++n) re[n] *= hann[n];"
      mark="re[n] *= hann[n];"
      name="window"
    />

    A frame that begins and ends at nothing has no jump in it. On the sine, the leak ten bins from the peak falls to <Claim id="leak-windowed">74 dB under it</Claim>. The price is a peak three bins wide where it could have been one.

    <Spectrum taps="builtBins" linear fMax={2000} min={-100} max={0}>
      The bins of the windowed frame. Try the sine again, and notice that the skirts are gone:
      the peak stands on a clean floor.
    </Spectrum>

    The bins are now honest, and the sound is now wrong. Every frame fades in and fades out, and the frames still lie end to end, so the sound falls to nothing at every join. It comes back <Claim id="window-dip">4.3 dB quieter</Claim> than it went in, with a dip to nothing at every join. Hear it.

    </Curve>

    <OverlappingWindows slot="picture" windows="builtWindows" sum="builtWindowSum" xTitle="milliseconds" height={200}>

    </OverlappingWindows>

    <Lanes slot="picture" taps="in built">
      The sound going in and the frames laid back down. Notice the second lane pinched to
      nothing again and again along its length.
    </Lanes>

  </Step>
  <Step title="Hops" controls="at">
    The cure is not to take the window off again. It is to take more frames. Move the window on by a quarter of a frame each time, and every sample falls under four frames: where one is fading out, the next are near their tops. The distance from one frame to the next is the hop.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/short-time-fourier.cpp"
      from="// Hops: how far the window moves on before the next frame is cut."
      to="int hop() const { return pieces >= kHops ? kBuildHop : kBuildFrame; }"
      mark="int hop() const { return pieces >= kHops ? kBuildHop : kBuildFrame; }"
      name="hops"
    />

    The loop is now <Claim id="hops-frames">375 frames</Claim>, four times as many, and the picture made of their bins has four times as many columns. A frame that overlaps its neighbours shares most of its samples with them, so the extra columns add no new facts. They show the same facts moving in smaller steps.

    Four Hann windows a quarter of a frame apart add up to the same number at every sample, and the number is 2. So the flutter is gone, and the sound comes back <Claim id="hops-double">6.0 dB louder</Claim> than it went in: twice as large, and otherwise right.

    <OverlappingWindows slot="picture" windows="builtWindows" sum="builtWindowSum" max={2.2} xTitle="milliseconds" height={200}>
      Each hump is one frame's share, a quarter of a frame after the last. Notice that four of
      them lie over every moment, and that their sum is a level line at 2.
    </OverlappingWindows>

    <Lanes slot="picture" taps="in built">
      The sound going in and the frames laid back down. Notice that the second lane has the
      shape of the first again, at twice the height.
    </Lanes>

  </Step>
  <Step title="Overlap and add" controls="at">
    Halving every frame would put the level right. The engine's kit does something else, and this step does as the kit does: it multiplies each frame by the window a second time, on its way out, and then scales it.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/short-time-fourier.cpp"
      from="// Overlap and add: the window a second time on the way out, and the scale"
      to="for (int n = 0; n < kBuildFrame; ++n) re[n] *= hann[n] * scale;"
      mark={['const float scale = 8.0f / (3.0f * kBuildOverlap);', 're[n] *= hann[n] * scale;']}
      name="again"
    />

    A frame's share of the output is now the window squared: once for the way in, once for the way out. Four squared windows a hop apart add up to a constant as well, a smaller one, and the scale in the listing is what turns it into 1. With that the frames add back to the input, after the same delay as before, <Claim id="built-back">more than 130 dB under it</Claim>. It is also the output of the kit's own class on the same loop, with the difference between the two <Claim id="built-kit">more than 130 dB under the sound</Claim>.

    As long as nobody touches the bins, the second window buys nothing that a half would not. It is there for the next step. A frame whose bins have been changed does not come back shaped like the window any more: what was changed spreads round the whole frame, ends included. Laid down as it is, such a frame throws sound a long way from where it belongs. The second window pulls the ends back to nothing whatever was done in between. The lab puts one sample through changed frames both ways. With the second window, <Claim id="second-window">nothing further than 21 ms from the sample is over −79 dB; without it, −64 dB</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="frame" n={1} title="A window slides along in hops">
  A transform needs a stretch of sound with a beginning and an end. The stretch is a frame of <M t="N" /> samples, and a new one is taken every <M t="H" /> samples, the hop. Each frame is multiplied by the Hann window before anything else, for the reason step 2 of the build showed.

  <Controls ids="at size" />

  <Curve taps="frameRaw:in window:ctl frame:out" xTitle="seconds into the loop" yTitle="sample value" height={240}>
    One frame of the loop as the kit cut it, the window drawn at the frame's own height, and
    the two multiplied, which is what gets transformed. Try "Frame at", and notice the window
    slide along the loop a hop at a time, since those are the only places a frame begins.
  </Curve>

  The picture as a formula, with <M t="m" /> counting the frames:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{gathered} \sig{out}{x_m[n]} = \sig{ctl}{w[n]}\;\sig{in}{x[mH + n]} \\[6pt] \sig{ctl}{w[n]} = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos\frac{2\pi n}{N}, \quad n = 0 \ldots N-1 \end{gathered}`}
  />

  Read it against the picture. <M t="x[mH + n]" /> is the loop, read from where frame <M t="m" /> begins: the line that is cut off square at both ends. <M t="w[n]" /> is the hump, which is 0 at the frame's first sample, 1 in its middle and nearly 0 again at its last. Their product is the third line, which hugs the first in the middle of the frame and shrinks to nothing at the ends.

  In the code there is no array of frames at all. <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" at="float process(float x, OnFrame&& on_frame) {">`Stft::process`</Src> takes one sample and gives one back, keeps the last two frames' worth of input in a ring, and every `Hop` samples calls `analyse_frame`, listed here, which reads the newest <M t="N" /> samples out of the ring through the table `window_`. At Glimmer's size a frame lasts <Claim id="frame-length">42.7 ms</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="bins" n={2} title="Each frame is transformed">
  Every windowed frame goes through the transform of <Lesson id="fourier-transform" />. A frame is real, so the upper half of its spectrum mirrors the lower, and only the lower half and the two ends are kept: <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" at="static constexpr int kBins = Frame / 2 + 1;">`kBins`</Src>, which is 1,025 complex numbers from 2,048 samples. The kit hands them to the device as two arrays, `re` and `im`, to change in place.

  <Spectrum taps="bins:out" min={-120} max={0}>
    The bins of the one frame of stage 1, across the whole range of hearing. Try the sine in
    the row of sounds, and notice how little there is beside its one peak: the window at work.
  </Spectrum>

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{X_m[k]} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_m[n]\,e^{-2\pi i\,kn/N}`} />

  Read it against the picture. <M t="k" /> counts along the bottom: bin <M t="k" /> stands at <M t={String.raw`k f_s / N`} /> hertz, so at this size the bins are <Claim id="bin-spacing">23.4 Hz</Claim> apart. <M t="X_m[k]" /> is one point of the frame's line: how much of that frequency frame <M t="m" /> holds. The sum is the question of the earlier lesson, asked of this frame's samples alone.

  Two conventions matter to anything that reads those arrays. The sum is not divided by anything, so a full-scale sine exactly on a bin reads <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" at="static constexpr float kFullScale = 0.25f * Frame;">a quarter of the frame's length</Src> there: the window lets half its amplitude through, and the mirrored half of the spectrum holds half of that. A partial is three bins wide before any device has touched it. And each bin's phase is counted from the frame's first sample, which stage 7 needs.

<StageBlock tap="added" n={4} title="Adding the frames back">
  The transform loses nothing. The inverse transform of a frame's bins is the windowed frame again, so the way back to sound is to invert every frame and add each one in where it was cut from. As step 4 of the build showed, the kit multiplies the frame by the window a second time before adding, so that a frame whose bins were changed still ends at nothing.

  <Controls ids="hop size" />

  <OverlappingWindows windows="windows" sum="windowSum" xTitle="milliseconds">
    Two frames' length of time. Each hump is one frame's share of the output: its window
    squared, once for the way in and once for the way out. The other line is all of them added
    and scaled. Try Hop at each of its three settings, and notice whether that line stays level.
  </OverlappingWindows>

  <M block t={String.raw`y[n] = c \sum_m w[n - mH]\;\hat{x}_m[n - mH]`} />

  Here <M t={String.raw`\hat{x}_m`} /> is frame <M t="m" /> after the inverse transform, and <M t="c" /> is a scale still to be found. With nothing altered, <M t={String.raw`\hat{x}_m[n - mH] = w[n - mH]\,x[n]`} />, and the sum comes apart:

  <M block t={String.raw`y[n] = x[n]\cdot \sig{out}{c\sum_m \sig{alt}{w^2[n - mH]}}`} />

  Read it against the picture. Each <M t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{w^2[n - mH]}`} /> is one hump. The sum over <M t="m" /> adds the humps that lie over the moment <M t="n" />, and with the scale it is the line across the top. The sound comes back exactly when that line is level at 1: then <M t="y[n]" /> is <M t="x[n]" /> and nothing else.

  For the Hann window, <M t={String.raw`w^2[n] = \tfrac38 - \tfrac12\cos\tfrac{2\pi n}{N} + \tfrac18\cos\tfrac{4\pi n}{N}`} />. Add <M t="K = N/H" /> copies of it, each a hop further on. The constant adds up to <M t="3K/8" />. The first cosine cancels from two copies up. The second goes round twice in a frame and needs at least three. A quarter of a frame is therefore the longest hop that is a power of two and works, and there the squared windows add up to <Claim id="hann-squared-sum">1.5, and at an eighth to 3</Claim>. The kit folds all of it into one constant, <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" at="static constexpr float kOutputScale = 8.0f / (3.0f * kOverlap * Frame);">`kOutputScale`</Src>, which is <M t={String.raw`8/(3K)`} /> to undo the sum, over <M t="N" /> because its inverse transform is unscaled. At a quarter hop and at an eighth the line is <Claim id="window-sum">flat at 1</Claim>. At a half it <Claim id="half-hop-swing">swings between two thirds and four thirds</Claim>, once every hop.

  The kit will not build with a hop of half a frame: a <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" at="static_assert((Hop & (Hop - 1)) == 0 && Hop >= 2 && Hop * 4 <= Frame,">`static_assert`</Src> on its template stops it. So Hop drives the lab's own overlap-add, written by hand: window, window, scale, add. With nothing altered the two transforms undo one another and are left out. At the kit's hops the hand version is the kit's own output: the difference between them lies <Claim id="by-hand">more than 130 dB under the signal</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="gains" n={6} title="Altering the frames: a gain for every bin">
  Between stage 2 and stage 4 the bins lie in two arrays, and a device may do as it likes with them. Glimmer does the simplest thing there is to do: it multiplies each bin by a gain.

  <Controls ids="depth rate grain pattern" />

  <Spectrogram tap="gains" min={-18} max={6} top={6000}>
    Glimmer's gain for every bin of every frame, up to 6 kHz: the page's own shade is silence,
    and the strongest shade is the top of a flash. Try Pattern on Scatter, and notice every
    band keep its own time where on Upward the light climbs.
  </Spectrogram>

  <M block t={String.raw`Y_m[k] = \at{gain-L584}{g_m[k]}\,X_m[k]`} />

  Read it against the picture. <M t="g_m[k]" /> is one point of it: column <M t="m" />, row <M t="k" />. In the listing that is `re[k] *= g; im[k] *= g;`. Both parts of the bin are scaled alike, so its size changes and its phase does not.

  The craft is in <M t="g" />. Glimmer lays the spectrum out in bands, even in hertz at the bottom and even in pitch further up, and gives each band a light that rises and falls on a slow clock of its own. Depth is how dark a band goes between flashes, Rate how often it flashes, Grain how narrow the bands are, Pattern how the bands' clocks are set against one another. A band's gain is

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{gathered} g_j = L_j\,\bigl(1 - d + d\,m_j\bigr) \\[6pt] L_j = \min\Bigl(\at{lift-L517}{2},\; 1\big/\sqrt{\overline{(1 - d + d\,m_j)^2}}\Bigr) \end{gathered}`}
  />

  with <M t="m_j" /> the light, from 0 to 1, <M t="d" /> the depth, and <M t="L_j" /> a lift that pays for the dark. The bar is an average over one cycle of the flicker: the lift is chosen so that the band's power, averaged over a cycle, is what went in. At the top of its flash a band therefore stands <Claim id="flash">4.9 dB over its input</Claim>, which is what the lift costs.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h"
    from="const float d = depth * share;"
    to="swing_[j] = lift * d;"
    mark="const float lift = kit::min(kMaxLift, 1.0f / std::sqrt(power));"
    name="lift"
  />

  A bin between two bands takes a blend of their gains, half a cosine wide, so that <M t="g" /> has no edges across the spectrum. A partial is three bins wide, as stage 2 showed, and a gain that jumped from one bin to the next would cut through the middle of it. This lesson starts with three of the device's refinements set aside so that the picture is plain: every band flickers as deeply as the next (Lean at 0), every flash is as tall as the last (Vary at 0), and the two sides move as one (Apart at 0).

<StageBlock tap="turning" n={7} title="The phase between frames">
  Scaling a bin and leaving its phase alone is a deliberate restraint, and the reason lies in how neighbouring frames agree with one another. Take a steady partial at <M t="b" /> bins' worth of frequency, where <M t="b" /> is rarely a whole number. From one frame to the next, <M t="H" /> samples on, it has turned by

  <M block t={String.raw`\Delta\varphi = 2\pi\,b\,\frac{H}{N}`} />

  and the bin it shows in reports that turn in its phase. Four overlapping frames add back to one smooth sine only because each carries the phase of the one before, moved on by exactly this much. A device that scales bins keeps that agreement without thinking about it. One that writes phases of its own has to keep the account itself, bin by bin, or frames cancel where they should add. Keeping it is what a phase vocoder does.

  Read backwards, the same relation says more about a partial than its bin can. The bin <M t="k" /> only says that the partial lies within a bin or so of <M t={String.raw`k f_s/N`} />. The turn says where:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{b} = \sig{alt}{k} + \operatorname{princarg}\!\Bigl(\Delta\varphi - 2\pi k\tfrac{H}{N}\Bigr)\cdot\frac{N}{2\pi H}`}
  />

  with princarg bringing an angle into ±π. The lab calls the kit's function for this, listed below, on the loudest bin of every frame.

  <Trace taps="loudest:alt turning:out" unit="Hz">
    The centre of the loudest bin, which can only stand on a bin, and the frequency worked out
    from how far that bin's phase turned since the frame before, which can stand anywhere. Try
    the sine in the row of sounds, and notice the two separate into two level lines.
  </Trace>

  Read the formula against the picture. <M t="k" /> is the line that moves in steps. The second term is the correction: how much further, or less far, the phase turned than a partial at the bin's exact centre would have turned it. The 440 Hz sine lies between two bins. <Claim id="phase-440">Its loudest bin is centred on 445.3 Hz, and the turn of that bin's phase reads 440.0 Hz</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="wet" n={8} title="What a gain does to an attack">
  Steady partials survive a gain per bin, because a bin or three is all a steady partial is. An attack is the opposite case. One single sample, inside a frame, is every bin at the same size, with the phases set so that all those sinusoids cancel at every sample of the frame but one. Turn some bins down and leave their neighbours up, and the cancelling fails: what was hidden everywhere in the frame comes out everywhere in the frame. And since a gain turns no phase, it comes out as much before the sample as after it.

<Part id="wrong" title="Four faults, and what the engine does about each.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Sound ahead of the attack" set="smear">
    A changed frame spreads an attack over the whole frame, before it as well as after. Depth sets how much of an attack leaves its place: <Claim id="smear-depth">3 % of a single sample's energy lies ahead of it at Depth 0.5 and 21 % at full Depth</Claim>. Grain sets how far it goes: <Claim id="smear-grain">5 samples ahead on average at Grain 0 and 62 at Grain 1</Claim>. This card plays the drums through the frames alone, with nothing passed round them. Listen for a breath before each hit. What to do: lower Depth, or do as Glimmer does and send attacks round the frames. Then <Listen tap="out">hear them passed round</Listen> on the same drums.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A hop too long" set="halfhop">
    When the squared windows do not add up to a constant, the frames put a tremolo of their own on everything, at the rate of the hop. With the hop at half a frame the guitar loop comes back <Claim id="half-hop-error">wrong by an amount only 12.5 dB under the signal</Claim>, and a steady sine grows <Claim id="half-hop-sidebands">a side tone 46.9 Hz either side of it, 15.6 dB under it</Claim>. This card plays the sine that way. Listen for a buzz under the tone. What to do: keep the hop to a quarter of the frame or less. The kit's `static_assert` makes this a mistake that cannot be compiled.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A sound that lives in one band" set="lone">
    The lift holds a band's level over a whole cycle of its flicker, not from moment to moment. Where many bands sound at once, their flashes and gaps average out. A lone sine has nothing to average with: its highest sample comes out <Claim id="sine-swells">4.7 dB over the input's</Claim> while its level over the loop is unchanged. Anything that alters frames can raise peaks it never meant to. What to do: leave room above a plain sound, lower Depth, or keep such a device for sounds with many partials.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="It is always late">
    Nothing built on frames answers sooner than a frame. A part played through Glimmer by hand is heard a frame and half a hop after the key went down, and no setting shortens that. What to do: use it on tracks that are already recorded, where a host shifts every other track to meet the delay. The engine's part is to be exact about it: one fixed number, stated in the manifest, with the dry path delayed to match.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A longer frame gives a better picture.">
    It gives thinner rows and wider columns, and the two always trade one for one. The prediction in part 1 shows the same note at two sizes, and stage 3 of part 5 has the measurements: two close tones that only the longest frame tells apart, and a single sample that the longest frame smears widest.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A spectrogram shows everything that is in the sound.">
    It shows the size of every bin and throws the phase away, which is half of what the transform found. Stage 7 reads a sine's true frequency from the phase alone, to a tenth of a hertz, where the picture can only say which bin it is in.
