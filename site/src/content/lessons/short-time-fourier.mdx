<Lesson id="fourier-transform" cap />, took one stretch of sound and asked which frequencies were in it. The answer had no clock in it. A spectrum of this whole loop says which notes are in there somewhere, and not when, and music is the *when*. The short-time Fourier transform keeps both by asking that question again and again of a short stretch each time: a window slides along the sound in small steps, and every stop gives one spectrum. Side by side they make a picture of the sound, time across and frequency up.

That is the half that looks. The other half is the way back. Each of those spectra can be turned into samples again, and because the stretches overlap, adding them where they came from gives back the sound that went in. In between, a device may change the spectra: turn a band down, hold it, move it. Whatever it does to them comes out as sound.

Two prices are paid, and both are fixed the moment a frame size is chosen. A stretch cannot be transformed until all of it has arrived, so the sound comes out late by about its length. And a stretch long enough to tell two close frequencies apart is too long to say just when something happened inside it.

## Stage by stage

### 1. A window slides along in hops

A transform needs a stretch of sound with a beginning and an end. The stretch is a *frame* of <M t="N" /> samples, and a new one is taken every <M t="H" /> samples, the *hop*. The kit's hop is a quarter of the frame, so the frames overlap deeply: every sample lies in four of them. Each frame is multiplied by a Hann window before anything else, for the reason <Lesson id="fourier-transform" />, gave. A frame cut off square leaks every partial across the whole spectrum; one that swells from nothing and dies back to nothing does not.

<M block t={String.raw`\begin{gathered} x_m[n] = w[n]\,x[mH + n] \\[6pt] w[n] = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos\frac{2\pi n}{N}, \quad n = 0 \ldots N-1 \end{gathered}`} />

Here <M t="m" /> counts the frames. In the code there is no array of frames at all. <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="205-219">`Stft::process`</Src> takes one sample and gives one back, keeps the last two frames' worth of input in a ring, and every `Hop` samples calls <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="312-322">`analyse_frame`</Src>, which reads the newest <M t="N" /> samples out of the ring through the table `window_`. Glimmer's frames are <Src path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h" lines="142-143">2,048 samples, taken every 512</Src>: 42.7 ms of sound at 48 kHz.

### 2. Each frame is transformed

Every windowed frame goes through the transform of <Lesson id="fourier-transform" />:

<M block t={String.raw`X_m[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_m[n]\,e^{-2\pi i\,kn/N}`} />

The frame is real, so the upper half of its spectrum mirrors the lower, and only bins 0 to <M t="N/2" /> are kept: 1,025 complex numbers from 2,048 samples (<Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="157-164">`kBins`</Src>). Bin <M t="k" /> stands at <M t={String.raw`k f_s / N`} />, which is every 23.4 Hz at this size. `analyse_frame` gets them from <Src path="cpp/kit/fft.h" lines="54-89">`Fft::forward_real`</Src>, which does half the work of the full transform for exactly this case, and hands them to the device as two arrays, `re` and `im`, to change in place.

Two conventions matter to anything that reads those arrays. The sum is not divided by anything, so a full-scale sine exactly on a bin reads <M t="N/4" /> there (`kFullScale`): the window lets half its amplitude through, and the mirrored half of the spectrum holds half of that. The figures here are in decibels against that number, and such a sine reads <Claim id="on-a-bin">0 dB on its bin, −6.02 dB in each neighbour and nothing anywhere else</Claim>. A partial is three bins wide before any device has touched it. And each bin's phase is counted from the frame's first sample, which stage 7 needs.

### 3. The frames side by side

Stand each frame's spectrum on end, set them in the order they were taken, colour by level, and that is a *spectrogram*. At a frame of 2,048 the loop's four seconds are <Claim id="frames-count">375 frames</Claim>, a column every 10.7 ms. It is the picture at the top of the page, drawn here for its lowest 1.5 kHz so that the rows can be told apart.

The frame's length sets both grains of the picture at once. Bins are <M t={String.raw`f_s/N`} /> apart, and a frame lasts <M t={String.raw`N/f_s`} /> seconds, so

<M block t={String.raw`\Delta f \cdot T = \frac{f_s}{N}\cdot\frac{N}{f_s} = 1`} />

whatever <M t="N" /> is. Halve the spacing of the bins and every frame takes twice as long. No setting escapes that: choosing a frame size is choosing which blur to live with.

### 4. Adding the frames back

The transform loses nothing. The inverse transform of a frame's bins is the windowed frame again, so the way back to sound is to invert every frame and add each one in where it was cut from. The kit does one thing more before adding: it multiplies the frame by the Hann window a second time (<Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="391-397">the last loop of `synthesise_frame`</Src>).

<M block t={String.raw`y[n] = c \sum_m w[n - mH]\;\hat{x}_m[n - mH]`} />

Here <M t={String.raw`\hat{x}_m`} /> is frame <M t="m" /> after the inverse transform and <M t="c" /> is a scale still to be found. The second window is there for frames that *have* been altered. A change made to the bins does not stay under the frame's taper. In time it spreads round the whole frame, ends included, and a frame that no longer ends at zero would click against its neighbours at every hop. The second window brings the ends back to zero whatever was done in between.

With nothing altered, <M t={String.raw`\hat{x}_m[n - mH] = w[n - mH]\,x[n]`} />, and the sum comes apart:

<M block t={String.raw`y[n] = x[n]\cdot c\sum_m w^2[n - mH]`} />

So the sound comes back exactly when the *squared* windows of all the frames over a sample add up to the same number at every sample. For the Hann window, <M t={String.raw`w^2[n] = \tfrac38 - \tfrac12\cos\tfrac{2\pi n}{N} + \tfrac18\cos\tfrac{4\pi n}{N}`} />. Add <M t="K = N/H" /> copies of it, each a hop further on. The constant adds up to <M t="3K/8" />. The first cosine cancels from two copies up. The second goes round twice in a frame and needs at least three. A quarter of a frame is therefore the longest hop that is a power of two and works, and there the squared windows add up to <Claim id="hann-squared-sum">1.5, and at an eighth to 3</Claim>. The kit folds all of it into one constant, <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="305-307">`kOutputScale`</Src>, which is <M t={String.raw`8/(3K)`} /> to undo the sum, over <M t="N" /> because its inverse transform is unscaled.

### 5. What it costs: latency

The delay is not a weakness of the code. A frame cannot be transformed before its last sample has arrived, and by then its first sample is a whole frame old. The kit begins writing a frame out half a hop after it is transformed:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{latency} = N + \frac{H}{2}`} />

At a frame of 2,048 a unit sample comes out <Claim id="latency">2,304 samples later</Claim>, which is 48 ms. At the other sizes on the switch it is <Claim id="latency-sizes">576, 1,152 and 4,608 samples</Claim> (<Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="157-164">`kLatency`</Src>).

The half hop is there to even out the load. A stereo device has two of these objects, and left alone both would transform on the same sample: four transforms in one block of audio, none in the next few. <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="166-167">`init(stagger)`</Src> lets the second channel do its work half a hop after the first, on the very same frames. The extra half hop of latency is the room for that, and both channels have it so that they stay in step. Glimmer's right side runs this way.

A device that mixes its altered sound with the untouched sound has to delay the untouched sound by the same amount, or the two no longer line up. <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="223">`Stft::dry()`</Src> returns the input of `kLatency` samples ago straight from the ring the frames are cut from, so the delay costs no memory, and the device's manifest states the number (<Src path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h" lines="142-143">`latencySamples`, 2304</Src>) so that a host can line other tracks up with it.

### 6. Altering the frames

So far this has been a careful way of doing nothing. The point of it is the gap between stage 2 and stage 4, where the bins lie in two arrays and a device may do as it likes with them. Glimmer does the simplest thing there is to do: it multiplies each bin by a gain.

<M block t={String.raw`Y_m[k] = g_m[k]\,X_m[k]`} />

In <Src path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h" lines="577-587">`Glimmer::frame`</Src> that is `re[k] *= g; im[k] *= g;`. Both parts are scaled alike, so the size of the bin changes and its phase does not.

The craft is in <M t="g" />. Glimmer <Src path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h" lines="18-30">lays the spectrum out in bands</Src>, even in hertz at the bottom and even in pitch further up, and gives each band a light that rises and falls on a slow clock of its own. Depth is how dark a band goes between flashes, Rate how often it flashes, Grain how narrow the bands are, Pattern <Src path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h" lines="59-64">how the bands' clocks are set against one another</Src>. A band's gain is

<M block t={String.raw`\begin{gathered} g_j = L_j\,\bigl(1 - d + d\,m_j\bigr) \\[6pt] L_j = \min\Bigl(2,\; 1\big/\sqrt{\overline{(1 - d + d\,m_j)^2}}\Bigr) \end{gathered}`} />

with <M t="m_j" /> the light, from 0 to 1, <M t="d" /> the depth, and <M t="L_j" /> a lift that pays for the dark: it is chosen so that the band's power, averaged over one cycle of its flicker (the bar), is what went in. A bin between two bands takes a blend of their gains, half a cosine wide, so that <M t="g" /> has no edges across the spectrum. A partial is three bins wide (stage 2), and a gain that jumped from one bin to the next would cut through the middle of it. The gains are worked out once a frame, in <Src path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h" lines="481-574">`prepare`</Src>. This lesson sets three of the device's refinements aside so that the picture is plain: every band flickers as deeply as the next (Lean 0), every flash is as tall as the last (Vary 0), and the two sides move as one (Apart 0).

### 7. The phase between frames, and what a gain does to an attack

Scaling a bin and leaving its phase alone is a deliberate restraint, and the reason lies in how neighbouring frames agree with one another. Take a steady partial at <M t="b" /> bins' worth of frequency, where <M t="b" /> is rarely a whole number. From one frame to the next, <M t="H" /> samples on, it has turned by

<M block t={String.raw`\Delta\varphi = 2\pi\,b\,\frac{H}{N}`} />

and the bin it shows in reports that turn in its phase. Four overlapping frames add back to one smooth sine only because each carries the phase of the one before, moved on by exactly this much. A device that scales bins keeps that agreement without thinking about it. One that writes phases of its own has to keep the account itself, bin by bin, or frames cancel where they should add. Keeping it is what a *phase vocoder* does.

Read backwards, the same relation says more about a partial than its bin can. The bin <M t="k" /> only says that the partial lies within a bin or so of <M t={String.raw`k f_s/N`} />. The turn says where:

<M block t={String.raw`b = k + \operatorname{princarg}\!\Bigl(\Delta\varphi - 2\pi k\tfrac{H}{N}\Bigr)\cdot\frac{N}{2\pi H}`} />

with princarg bringing an angle into ±π. This is <Src path="cpp/kit/stft.h" lines="270-290">`Stft::advance_to_bin`</Src>, and the lab calls it on the loudest bin of every frame.

  The centre of the loudest bin (green), which can only stand on multiples of 23.4 Hz, and the frequency worked out from how far that bin's phase turned since the frame before (red), which can stand anywhere. On the guitar both follow the tune, a few hertz apart. Choose the sine as the source and they separate into two level lines.

The 440 Hz sine lies between bins 18 and 19. <Claim id="phase-440">Its loudest bin is centred on 445.3 Hz, and the turn of that bin's phase reads 440.0 Hz</Claim>.

Steady partials survive a gain per bin, because a bin or three is all a steady partial is. An attack is the opposite case. One unit sample, inside a frame, is every bin at the same size, with the phases set so that all those sinusoids cancel at every sample of the frame but one. Turn some bins down and leave their neighbours up, and the cancelling fails: what was hidden everywhere in the frame comes out everywhere in the frame. And since a gain turns no phase, it comes out as much before the sample as after it.

In the frames' sum the sample comes out <Claim id="click-smear">0.77 high, with a fifth of its energy ahead of it and as much behind</Claim>, and the largest sample ahead is <Claim id="click-ahead">14.5 dB under the one that went in</Claim>. Sound ahead of its own attack is the artefact that work on frames is known for.

Glimmer does not try to mend the frames. It goes round them. It watches its input for attacks (<Src path="cpp/devices/glimmer/glimmer.h" lines="359-385">`next_segment`</Src>: the squared step from sample to sample, summed 64 samples at a time, jumping to three times the largest sum of the 2,048 samples before), and it has the latency in hand: an attack it has just seen will not be heard for another 2,304 samples. So ahead of each one it fades its output across to the delayed dry sound, and back again afterwards, `out = dry + Mix · (1 − pass) · (wet − dry)`. The red curve above is the result: the unit sample comes out <Claim id="click-passed">whole, with nothing ahead of it over −80 dB</Claim>. From <Claim id="attack-dry">21 ms ahead of an attack to 6 ms after it</Claim> the output is the input, sample for sample, 2,304 late: for that long there is no flicker at all, on the attack or on whatever else was sounding under it.

## What goes wrong

**Latency.** Nothing built on frames answers sooner than a frame. Glimmer's 48 ms has to be made up somewhere, and a host shifts every other track to meet it. The engine's part is to be exact about it: a fixed number, stated in the manifest, with the dry path delayed to match.

**Sound ahead of the attack.** Depth sets how much of an attack leaves its place: <Claim id="smear-depth">3 % of the unit sample's energy lies ahead of it at Depth 0.5 and 21 % at full Depth</Claim>. Grain sets how far it goes: <Claim id="smear-grain">5 samples ahead on average at Grain 0 and 62 at Grain 1</Claim>. The engine's answer here is to pass attacks round the frames altogether, and its cost is the flicker missing around every attack.

**A hop too long.** When the windows do not add up to a constant, the frames put a tremolo of their own on everything, at the rate of the hop. At a hop of half a frame the output is <Claim id="half-hop-error">wrong by an amount only 12.5 dB under the signal</Claim>, and a steady sine at a frame of 2,048 grows <Claim id="half-hop-sidebands">a side tone 46.9 Hz either side of it, 15.6 dB under it</Claim>. The kit's `static_assert` makes this a mistake that cannot be compiled.

**A sound that lives in one band.** The lift holds a band's level over a whole cycle of its flicker, not from moment to moment. Where many bands sound at once their flashes and gaps average out. A lone 440 Hz sine has nothing to average with, and its highest sample comes out <Claim id="sine-swells">4.7 dB over the input's</Claim> while its level over the loop is unchanged. Anything that alters frames can raise peaks it never meant to.
