<Part id="why" title="Two speakers, and a sound that has to stand somewhere between them.">

A guitar is recorded with one microphone. That is one channel: one stream of samples. The room it will be heard in has two speakers, or a pair of headphones.

Send the guitar to the left speaker alone and the left speaker is where it comes from. Send the same samples to both speakers and listen for where it seems to be. A listener sitting between the speakers gets the same sound in both ears, at the same moment and the same loudness, and most report one source straight ahead, in the middle, where there is no speaker at all.

Listen for it there. <Hear set="middle" label="Hear the guitar in the middle" />

So the place of a sound between two speakers is not a place. It is two gains, one for each speaker. That is the first half of this lesson: which two gains put the guitar a little to the left, and why the obvious pair is wrong.

The second half is about sounds that already have two channels. A pad recorded in stereo has two channels that differ. Can they be made to differ more, and what does that cost?

### The obvious answer fails

To move the guitar from the left speaker to the right one, bring the left gain down from 1 to 0 while the right gain goes up from 0 to 1, in a straight line. Halfway across, each speaker gets half of the guitar. The two halves add up to one whole guitar, so it seems fair.

It is not, because what two speakers add up to in a room is power, and power is the square of the amplitude. Half the amplitude is a quarter of the power. Two speakers with a quarter each make half the power that one speaker made with all of it. Measured on a 1 kHz tone, the two channels together carry <Claim id="line-dip">3.01 dB</Claim> less power in the middle than at either end. A sound that walks across the room loses power on the way and gets it back as it arrives.

### Share the power, not the amplitude

The cure is to pick the two gains so that their squares add up to 1 wherever the sound stands. In the middle that gives each speaker not a half but <Claim id="law-centre">0.707</Claim> of the sound, which is the square root of a half. This is called an equal-power pan law, and it is the one the engine's kit carries. The control that moves a sound along it is called Pan.

### A picture for two channels

A wave drawn against time shows one channel. Stereo is about how two channels differ, and that needs a picture of its own.

Take one sample of a sound that has two channels. Its left value says how far across to go, and its right value says how far up. Put a dot there. Do the same for a thousand samples spread through the loop. The picture below is the pad, as it comes.

<StereoCloud
  taps="going"
  set="as-it-is"
  caption="The pad, a dot a sample. The dashed diagonal is where a dot lies when left and right are the same. The pad's dots lie round the diagonal and stray from it on both sides: its two channels are alike and not the same."
/>

A sound that is the same in both speakers has every dot on the dashed diagonal: as far up as it is across. The guitar in the middle is a straight line there. A sound that is in the left speaker alone lies flat along the line marked "left channel". The pad is a cloud. How far the cloud strays from the diagonal is how much the pad's two channels differ, and that difference is what this lesson calls width.

One number sums the cloud up. Multiply left by right at every sample, add the products up, and divide by the sizes of the two channels. The answer is 1 when the channels are the same, 0 when they have nothing to do with each other, and −1 when one is the other turned upside down. Its usual name is the correlation of the two channels, and this page calls it how alike the channels are. For the pad it reads <Claim id="pad-alike-in">0.48 going in</Claim>.

<Predict
  question="The widened pad is played on a telephone, which has one speaker: left and right are added together. Beside the pad before it was widened, on the same telephone, is it louder, quieter or the same?"
  choices={['Louder', 'Quieter', 'The same']}
>

Quieter. On one speaker the widened pad measures <Claim id="pad-one">1.6 dB</Claim> under the pad that went in.

Adding the two channels keeps what they share and throws away where they differ: a dot on the diagonal comes through whole, and a dot on the line that crosses it adds up to nothing. The widener made the pad wider by turning up the difference between the channels, by <Claim id="pad-side">2.9 dB</Claim>, and turning down what they share. One speaker gets only the part that was turned down.

It gets worse with a sound that had no width to begin with. A strum recorded in mono has nothing between its channels to turn up, so at high settings the widener makes a difference out of delayed copies. Added back together, those copies cancel at some frequencies and not at others.

That is the whole subject in three moves. Two gains place a sound. Two more gains widen a pair of channels. And adding the channels back together shows what the width was paid for with.

</Part>

Tilt your head an eighth of a turn to the left and the picture has two new axes. Along the dashed diagonal is what the two channels share. That is called **mid**. Across it is where they differ, which is called **side**. Left and right, mid and side are two ways of reading the same dots. Width does nothing along the left and right axes. It works along the other pair: it stretches or squeezes the cloud across the diagonal.

Now check one thing against the picture. Set Going in to "In mono, placed by Pan". The cloud going in collapses to a straight line through the middle, because one sound times two fixed gains can only ever move along one direction. Pan turns that line: all the way down it lies flat along "left channel", at 0 it lies on the diagonal, all the way up it stands along "right channel". That is all a pan control does.

Leave Pan where the page starts it, halfway to the left, and look at what comes out. It is not a line any more. The widener treats the low part of a sound and the rest of it differently, as part 4 builds, so the dots open into a cloud. The part above the bass has been moved further out than Pan put it. Measured on a 1 kHz tone at these settings, the right speaker <Claim id="tone-edge">goes in 7.7 dB under the left and comes out 19.1 dB under it</Claim>: the tone, placed halfway left, has ended up nearly all in the left speaker.

Notice what the picture leaves out. It says nothing about which frequencies are where, and nothing about time: a dot does not say when it happened. A widener that works on the bass and the treble differently, or that delays one side, looks here like any other cloud. Part 5 has the pictures for those.

</Part>

    Two gains can only turn up a side that is there. A sound in the middle has none, and 2.5 times nothing is nothing. So above <Src path="site/labs/stereo.cpp" at="if (pieces >= kScatter && w > 0.75f)">a Width of 0.75</Src> the widener begins to make a difference of its own.

<StageBlock tap="placed" n={1} title="The pan law: two gains from one angle">
  This is the kit's function for placing one sound between two speakers. Many of the engine's instruments call it to spread their voices or their grains across the speakers, as the kit's own grain player <Src path="cpp/kit/grains.h" at="pan_gains(pan, &grain.gain_left, &grain.gain_right);">does</Src>. It takes Pan and gives back two gains.

  <Controls ids="pan" />

  <Curve taps="lawLeft lawRight" uprights="pan" xTitle="Pan" yTitle="gain" min={0} max={1.05} height={240}>
    The gain of each speaker against Pan, worked out by the kit's function, with Pan's own setting
    as the dashed line. Try Pan at 0, and notice the dashed line pass through the point where the
    two gains cross.
  </Curve>

  As a formula, with <M t="p" /> for Pan and <M t="x" /> for the one sound going in:

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{pan-angle}{\theta = (p + 1)\,\frac{\pi}{4}}`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{g_L} = \at{pan-left}{\cos\theta}`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{alt2}{g_R} = \at{pan-right}{\sin\theta}`} />

  Read it against the picture. Pan runs from −1 to 1, so <M t={String.raw`\theta`} /> runs from nothing to a quarter of a turn. At the left end the cosine is 1 and the sine is 0: all of the sound goes left. At the right end it is the other way round. In the middle the angle is an eighth of a turn, where cosine and sine are equal, and that is the crossing in the picture. The left speaker then gets <M t={String.raw`g_L\,x`} /> and the right <M t={String.raw`g_R\,x`} />.

  The reason for a cosine and a sine is the circle of step 2 of part 4, in one line:

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{g_L}^2 + \sig{alt2}{g_R}^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1`} />

  Measured on a 1 kHz tone at five places from all the way left to all the way right, the power of the two channels together stays within <Claim id="law-power">less than 0.001 dB</Claim>.

  There is a price, and it is paid on one speaker. Add the two gains instead of their squares and the sum is not level at all.

  <Still
    taps="lawSum lineSum"
    view="curve"
    xTitle="Pan"
    caption="The two gains added, in decibels, against Pan: what one speaker gets when left and right are summed. The straight line of part 1 is level here. The equal-power law swells in the middle."
  />

  A sound in the middle comes out of one speaker <Claim id="law-sum">3.01 dB</Claim> louder than the same sound placed all the way left. So each law keeps one thing level and lets the other move. The straight line is right for one speaker and sags in two. Equal power is right for two and swells in one. No pair of gains does both, which is why a mixer may offer more than one law.

  A pan control moved by a slow wave, back and forth, is the side-to-side tremolo of <Lesson id="tremolo-and-ring-modulation" />. The engine's tremolo uses the same cosine and sine for it, <Src path="cpp/devices/tremolo/tremolo.h" at="pan[0] = 1.41421356f * kit::SineTable::cos_lookup(angle);">scaled up</Src> so that the middle is at full level.

  <M
    block

  <M block t={String.raw`a = e^{-2\pi f_c / f_s}`} />

  <M t="L" /> is the left channel going in and <M t="b" /> its bass. <StageBlock tap="widened" n={4} title="Back to left and right">
  Mid and side are of no use to a speaker, so they are turned back into left and right at once:

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{L'} = \at{ms-left}{\sig{alt}{g_M M} + \sig{alt2}{g_S S}}`} />

<StageBlock tap="secondOut" n={9} title="The same job, built with one speaker in mind">
  The engine has a second widener, a later one, and the lab runs it beside the first on the same sound. It begins the same way, with half the sum and half the difference. What it does next is different in the one respect this lesson has been measuring: it never changes the level of the mid. The mid is turned in time as the side's filters turn the side, so the two stay in step, and that is all that is done to it.

One speaker gets the mid, and the mid is the input at its own level. The listing's last two lines also multiply by the device's Output, which is left at its default here, a gain of 1.

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Gaps on one speaker" set="made-one">
    The scatter gives left and right different combs. Added together, the two combs do not fill each other in. They make a third. At Width 0.9, <Claim id="fault-gaps">one speaker's share of the sound has a gap over 25 dB deep between 1 kHz and 4 kHz</Claim>, and that is on top of what the strum loses overall.   <Fault title="Every hit becomes several" set="smear">
    <Claim id="smear">At Width 1 the groups of stage 5 are 10.67 ms apart, and on the left the last of them starts 42.67 ms after the sample that made it. <Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="Pan moves a sound from one place to another.">
    Pan changes two gains, and nothing else. The same sound comes out of both speakers at the same moment, at two levels, and the listener's hearing does the placing. A sound recorded with two microphones differs between its sides in time as well as in level, and no pan control makes that.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A widener makes any sound wide.">
    Up to 0.75 it can only turn up a side that is already there. <Claim id="myth-mono">The strum, the same on both sides going in, comes out at Width 0.75 with its two sides still the same and 1.9 dB quieter</Claim>. The width the strum got in part 3 was all made by the scatter and the delay, at the cost that part 7 lists.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Mid is the middle and side is the edges.">
    Mid is what the two channels have in common and side is what they do not. Neither is a place. A sound all the way left is not "all side": it is mid and side in equal parts, as stage 4 worked out. That is why turning up the side turned that sound over on the right, and did not just move it further left.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Width at 0 is mono.">
    In this device it is not, because the bass keeps its whole side there. <Claim id="myth-zero">At Width 0 the pad's left and right come out 0.85 alike</Claim>, where a mono sound is 1. For mono, add the channels: that is the last step of the build, and it is one line.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="If it is good in stereo it will be good on one speaker.">
    The two are different sounds, and the difference is exactly the side, which one speaker never gets. A widener works by adding side. Whatever it added is what goes missing, and what it took from the mid to pay for it stays missing too.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Equal power is the right pan law.">
    It is right for two speakers in a room, where powers add. It is wrong for a sum to one speaker, where amplitudes add, by the amount stage 1 measured. Each law keeps one of the two level. Choosing between them is a matter of where the music will be heard.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
