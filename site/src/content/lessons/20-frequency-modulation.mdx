Take a sine wave, the plainest sound there is (lesson 1). Now let a second sine push its phase forwards and pull it back, hundreds of times a second. The first sine is the **carrier**: it is the one that is heard. The second is the **modulator**: it is never heard, it only bends the carrier. Done slowly this is a wobble in pitch. Done hundreds of times a second it is too fast to be a wobble, and what comes out is something else: a set of new, steady frequencies standing either side of the carrier. That is all frequency modulation is, and it costs two table reads and a multiplication for every sample.

Two numbers decide the result. The **ratio** of the modulator's frequency to the carrier's decides *where* the new frequencies stand, and so whether the tone is a note with harmonics or the clang of a bell. The **index**, how far the phase is pushed, decides *how many* of them there are. Put the index under an envelope, large at the strike and falling away, and the tone is bright when it is struck and pure as it rings, which is how a struck bar or a glass behaves.

The lab strikes the engine's Glass once every two seconds and runs it one sample at a time. Glass has four operators, wired as two carrier and modulator pairs, tuned apart and panned apart. The lab sets Detune to 0, Spread to 1, Velocity to 0 and Feedback to 0, so the left channel is one pair alone, at the note's own pitch, struck the same every time. ## Stage by stage

### 1. Two sines from one table

An operator is a counter and a table. The counter is the phase, a number from 0 to 1 that says how far through its cycle the sine is. Every sample it steps on by the frequency over the sample rate, and wraps:

<M block t={String.raw`\varphi[n+1] = \bigl(\varphi[n] + f / f_s\bigr) \bmod 1, \qquad y[n] = \sin\bigl(2\pi\,\varphi[n]\bigr)`} />

The step is `increment` and the wrap is `advance` in <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="234-237">the device</Src>. The sine is not computed: <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" lines="106-140">`SineTable::lookup`</Src> reads it from a table of 4,096 steps round the circle and draws a straight line between the two nearest. All four operators of every voice read the same table.

The carrier steps at the note's frequency. The modulator steps at the note's frequency times the Ratio, so the device counts its ratio as modulator over carrier: a Ratio of 2 is a modulator an octave above the note. Ratio is not a slider. It is a list of twelve, <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="52-54">`kRatios`</Src>: 0.5, 1, 1.41, 2, 2.76, 3, 3.5, 4, 5, 7, 9 and 14. The lab offers eight of them. Stage 3 shows why a list is the honest control.

### 2. The modulator is added to the carrier's phase

This is the whole technique. Before the carrier reads the table, the modulator's output, scaled by the index, is added to its phase:

<M block t={String.raw`y(t) = \sin\bigl(2\pi f_c\,t + I\,\sin(2\pi f_m\,t)\bigr)`} />

<M t="f_c" /> is the carrier's frequency, <M t="f_m" /> the modulator's, and <M t="I" /> the **index**: the furthest the phase is pushed, in radians. At an index of <M t={String.raw`\pi`} /> the carrier is thrown half a cycle forwards and half a cycle back. In the code it is one line of <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="274-279">`render`</Src>, `lookup(phase[0] + index_a * e1 * mod_a) * e0`: `mod_a` is the modulator's sine, `e1` its envelope, `e0` the carrier's. The table takes its phase in cycles, so the code holds the index in cycles too, <M t={String.raw`I / 2\pi`} />. The kit's own <Src path="cpp/kit/osc.h" lines="83-95">`SineOsc`</Src> has the same input and the same unit.

  What is added to the carrier's phase over the same twelve milliseconds as the first picture: the modulator's sine times the index, in radians. Where it is above zero the red wave of that picture is ahead of the grey one.

Why is this called *frequency* modulation when it is the phase that is moved? Frequency is the rate at which phase turns. A phase that is pushed forwards is, for that moment, turning faster. Take the slope of the phase above and the carrier's frequency at each instant is

<M block t={String.raw`f(t) = f_c + I\,f_m\cos(2\pi f_m\,t)`} />

so the frequency swings <M t={String.raw`I\,f_m`} /> hertz either side of the carrier. A modulator at 770 Hz with an index of 2 swings a 220 Hz carrier by 1,540 Hz each way, far below zero and back. Frequency modulation proper adds the modulator to the frequency and lets the phase be the running sum of it. The two differ by that one sum, an integral: modulating the phase by a sine is modulating the frequency by a cosine, <M t={String.raw`I\,f_m`} /> deep.

The device modulates the phase, and so do most engines that use the name. Nothing has to be summed, so nothing can drift: the moment the modulator is silent the carrier is exactly back on its own phase. A modulator with a steady offset in it would put a true frequency-modulated carrier out of tune, and here only shifts it by a fixed angle. The lab builds the held tone both ways, in double precision, and compares:

### 3. Side bands, and where the ratio puts them

A sine inside a sine can be taken apart into plain sines:

<M block t={String.raw`\sin\bigl(\theta_c + I\sin\theta_m\bigr) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} J_k(I)\,\sin\bigl(\theta_c + k\,\theta_m\bigr)`} />

Read the right-hand side as a spectrum. There is a line at the carrier (<M t="k = 0" />) and a line at <M t={String.raw`f_c + k\,f_m`} /> for every whole <M t="k" />, above and below: the **side bands**. Multiplying by a sine (lesson 16) makes one pair of them. Modulating the phase makes the whole ladder, a modulator's width apart. The numbers <M t={String.raw`J_k(I)`} /> are the lines' levels, and stage 4 is about them.

With the note on 468.75 Hz, the middle of one of the transform's bins, and a ratio of 2, every line of the held tone stands <Claim id="sidebands-where">within one bin, 11.7 Hz</Claim>, of the carrier plus or minus a whole number of modulators.

Count down the ladder in the picture and it runs out of room. The upper side bands are at 990, 1,760 and 2,530 Hz. The first lower one should be at 220 − 770 = −550 Hz. A negative frequency is nothing strange: it is a sine running backwards, and <M t={String.raw`\sin(-x) = -\sin x`} />, so it is the same sine at +550 Hz turned upside down. The lower side bands **reflect** off zero and come back up: 550, 1,320, 2,090 Hz. At an index of 1 the reflected first side band reads <Claim id="reflected-level">−7.1 dB, the same as the upper one</Claim>.

Where the reflected lines land is what the ratio is for.

- **A whole ratio** puts every line on a multiple of the note. At 2 the ladder is 1, 3, 5, 7 times the note, and the reflected lines land on those same places: a tone with odd harmonics only. At 1 it is every harmonic. A line that lands on another adds to it or takes from it, by its sign. At a ratio of 2 and an index of 1 the reflected first side band lands on the carrier itself, and the line at the note reads <Claim id="ratio-two">+1.6 dB</Claim>: more than the bare carrier had.
- **3.5**, where the device starts, puts lines at 2.5, 4.5, 6 and 8 times the note. No two land together, and half of them fall half-way between the note's harmonics. Strictly it is a harmonic series on a note an octave lower with most of its members missing.
- **1.41 and 2.76** share no simple fraction with the note at all. Nothing lines up, the spectrum has no common spacing, and the result is the uneven set of overtones that a bell or a struck glass has.

That is why Ratio is a list. Between two entries there is no half-way timbre, only a modulator gliding in pitch through tones that are in tune with nothing. The device reads Ratio when a note starts, and a note that is already sounding keeps the one it was struck with.

### 4. How strong each line is

The levels <M t={String.raw`J_k(I)`} /> are **Bessel functions**, and the name is harder than the idea. Each is a curve of one line's level against the index. The lab measures them from a pair of the kit's table sines, by asking how much of a sine at each line's frequency the tone holds:

The dashed upright is the index slider. While the note plays, the dot rides the carrier's curve at the index the note has at that instant, and slides back to the left as the note rings.
</Curve>

Read the picture from the left. At an index of 0 the carrier is at 1 and every side band at 0: the held tone is <Claim id="one-sine">one sine, with every other line more than 130 dB under it</Claim>. As the index grows the carrier *falls*, because the energy that fills the side bands comes out of it: the wave never gets taller than 1, so what the side bands gain something else must lose. The first pair rises, then the second, then the third, each starting later than the one before. At an index of 1 the carrier's line reads <Claim id="bessel-one">−2.3 dB and the first pair −7.1 dB</Claim>, the 0.765 and 0.440 that tables of the functions give.

Then the curves do what makes this sound unlike a filter opening. Each line rises, falls to nothing, and comes back upside down. The measured curves cross zero where the tables say: <Claim id="line-zeros">the carrier at 2.40 and 5.52, the first pair at 3.83 and 7.02, the second at 5.14</Claim>. At an index of 2.405 the carrier's line in the held tone is <Claim id="carrier-null">81 dB down</Claim>: a note with nothing at all at its own pitch.

<Spectrum taps="steady" linear fMax={6000} min={-80} max={0}>
  The held tone's spectrum again, to watch against the curves. Take the index slowly from 0 to 8: energy moves outward from the carrier into more and more lines, and any one line swells, vanishes and returns.
</Spectrum>

How many lines matter? The rule of thumb is that the lines worth counting reach about <M t={String.raw`I + 1`} /> modulators either side of the carrier, a width of <M t={String.raw`f_m\,(I + 1)`} /> hertz each way. Measured from the curves above, at an index of 2 the carrier and three pairs hold <Claim id="carson">99.8 % of the power</Claim>. The lines beyond are small but they never stop, which matters in "What goes wrong".

### 5. The index under an envelope

A held index gives a held tone. The instrument comes alive when the index moves, and in this device it always moves: the modulator has an envelope of its own.

The device's knob is not the index but **Brightness**, from 0 to 1. At the strike the index is

<M block t={String.raw`I_{\text{strike}} = 8\;B^{1.5}\;\sqrt{261.63 / f}`} />

radians: <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="56-63">`kMaxIndex`</Src> is 8, Brightness goes through a power of 1.5 so the low end of the knob has room, and the square root is the key scaling of <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="80-87">`key_index_scale`</Src>, which gives notes below middle C more index and notes above it less (never more than 1.4 times, never less than 0.3). The lab's Index slider works that sum backwards and sets the Brightness that gives the index asked for. At the starting settings the device grants <Claim id="index-strike">2.00 rad for the 2 asked</Claim>.

From there the index is that number times the modulator's envelope, <Src path="cpp/kit/env.h" lines="12-112">the kit's `Adsr`</Src>. Its decay is one multiplication a sample towards the level it settles on, with the coefficient chosen so that 60 dB of the fall takes the set time:

<M block t={String.raw`e[n] = s + \bigl(e[n-1] - s\bigr)\,c, \qquad c = e^{-6.908 / (T f_s)}`} />

Here <M t="s" /> is the level it settles on (0 while Sustain is 0) and 6.908 is <M t={String.raw`\ln 1000`} />, 60 dB. <M t="T" /> is where the device's voicing is. For the modulator it is Decay times <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="80-83">`key_time_scale`</Src>, which stretches low notes and shortens high ones. For the carrier it is four times that (`kAmpDecayRatio`). So the brightness of a note is gone long before its loudness. At 220 Hz and a Decay of 0.4 s the modulator's envelope falls 60 dB in <Claim id="mod-decay">0.43 s</Claim> and the carrier's in <Claim id="amp-decay">1.72 s</Claim>.

### 6. The rest of the device around the pair

What goes out is the pair times a gain, then two guards. One voice at full level is scaled by 0.29 (`kVoiceGain`) and the Volume. A <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h">DC blocker</Src> at 12 Hz follows, because a side band can land on 0 Hz exactly: the first lower one does at a ratio of 1. With two plain sines it arrives there at a level of nothing, but once Feedback is up it does not, and a constant offset is not sound. At a ratio of 1, an index of 2 and full Feedback the held tone sits <Claim id="dc-offset">0.013 below centre</Claim>. Last is a soft clip that is exactly a wire below half of full scale and rounds off only when many notes pile up.

<Check id="rebuilt" />

That check is the lab's proof that the stages above are the device's. It takes the phases, the envelopes, the modulator's output and the index out of every sounding voice, runs them through the line of stage 2, the gain, a DC blocker and the clip, and compares with the left channel. At the starting settings the two are <Claim id="rebuilt-exact">the same, sample for sample</Claim>.

The other three operators are more of the same thing. In the **Bell** algorithm, used here, and in **Pad**, they make a second pair. <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="65-78">`kShapes`</Src> voices it apart from the first: in Bell its index is 0.7 times the first pair's and its modulator dies in 0.6 of the time, measured here as <Claim id="pair-b">0.70 times the index and 0.26 s against 0.43 s</Claim>. Detune tunes the pairs half its amount flat and sharp, so they beat, and Spread pans them apart, so the beat moves between the speakers. In **Glass** three operators are stacked, a modulator on a modulator on a carrier, beside one bare carrier. In **Mallet** one modulator bends two carriers and the fourth operator is a short partial at four times the note.

  The index of each pair across the loop, in radians: the first pair (blue), heard on the left, and the second (orange), on the right. The dashed line is the slider. When the first pair's trace starts below it, the device has granted less than was asked: see "What goes wrong".
</Trace>

**Feedback** is the last wire. An operator can modulate itself: its own output, scaled, is added to its own phase. A sine fed back leans over towards a sawtooth and grows harmonics of its own, so one operator does the work of a small ladder. The device feeds back the mean of the operator's last two outputs, not the last one, which stops the sample-to-sample flutter that plain feedback falls into, and it stops at 0.75 rad (<Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="222-246">`kMaxFeedback`</Src>). The header gives the reason: past 1 rad the harmonics stop falling away fast enough for any cap to keep them under half the sample rate. At full Feedback the modulator of the starting note has a second harmonic <Claim id="feedback-saw">10 dB under its first</Claim>.

## What goes wrong

**Aliasing.** The ladder of side bands has no top rung. A line that passes half the sample rate folds back down (lesson 1) to a frequency that belongs to no ladder, and a high ratio at a large index sends many there. The usual cure is to run the oscillators at several times the sample rate. This device does not. It knows from the Bessel functions how far the lines above −60 dB reach, <M t={String.raw`I + 1.6\sqrt{I} + 1.8`} /> modulators from the carrier, and when a note starts it caps the index at the largest value whose reach stays under 0.47 of the sample rate (<Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" lines="287-307">`index_for_room`</Src>). At 220 Hz and a ratio of 14 the device grants <Claim id="cap-granted">2.78 rad of the 8 asked</Claim>. Above 0.47 of the sample rate the capped tone's strongest line is <Claim id="cap-clean">71 dB down, where the uncapped tone's is 13 dB down</Claim>. The price is that high notes are duller than the knob says, and that is audible as the design: at 880 Hz and a ratio of 3.5 the most the device grants is <Claim id="key-scale">2.64 rad</Claim>. A modulator that would itself sit above the limit is not run at all. At 1,760 Hz and a ratio of 14 it would be at 24.6 kHz, and the note is <Claim id="modulator-silent">one sine</Claim>.

**A note with no fundamental.** Near an index of 2.4 the carrier's line is gone, and at a ratio that is not whole nothing else stands at the note's pitch. Whether the note still seems to sit at its pitch is then up to the ear, and worth listening for. More index is not more of the same tone.

**Clicks at the strike.** Every fresh voice starts its four operators at phase 0, so every strike has the same attack. A voice taken over while it still sounds is not reset, because the jump in phase would click, and a key struck again while held fades its old voice out over 30 ms beside the new one.

**Zipper noise in the index.** Brightness is smoothed over 5 ms before it reaches the operators, so turning the knob under a held note slides the index and does not step it.
