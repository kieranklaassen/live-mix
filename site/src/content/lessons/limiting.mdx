<Part id="why" title="Sound has a top, and loud music wants to sit right under it.">

A drum loop is hits and the spaces between them. The hits are tall and short, and the spaces are nearly empty. Over the four seconds of the loop on this page the highest sample stands <Claim id="crest">18.7 dB</Claim> above the loop's level, which is its mean power. For most of the loop the sound is nowhere near its own top.

Listen to it first. <Hear set="played" label="Hear the drums as played" />

Digital sound has a top that nothing passes. A sample is a number, and the largest number a converter or a file can carry is called full scale. Levels are counted down from it in decibels, so full scale is 0 dB and everything else is below. How loud a sound seems follows its level, not its highest sample: <Lesson id="level-and-loudness" /> measures both. So this loop, whose level lies so far under its hits, sounds quiet beside music whose level sits closer to the top.

The question of this lesson is how to turn a sound up by more than the room there is above its highest peak.

### The obvious answers fail

Turn it up anyway. A gain multiplies every sample by one number, so the spaces come up and the hits come up by exactly as much. On this page that gain is called Push. With Push where the page starts it, the highest sample of the drums stands <Claim id="over-full">8.2 dB over full scale</Claim>.

A converter has no number for a sample over full scale. Whatever passes the top is cut off flat, which is called clipping. This page plays every stage <Src path="site/src/lab/player.ts" at="With the volume at its starting place the stages play 12 dB under full scale.">with room above full scale</Src>, 12 dB of it where the page's volume starts, so Pushed can be heard here unharmed. That room is not endless. <Src path="site/src/lab/player.ts" at="export const volumeGain = (volume: number) => 0.5 * volume * volume">With the volume all the way up the page plays at half height</Src>, and a sound past twice full scale, as the tallest hits of Pushed are, is then cut off on its way to your ears. So is Pushed with Push near the top of its range, where the volume starts. The pictures show neither. Nothing outside this page is so forgiving. The stage called "Pushed, and cut off at the ceiling" is what would come out. <Hear set="clipped" label="Hear it turned up and cut off" />

A wave with its top cut off is a different wave. The flat part has corners, and corners are made of frequencies that were never played. A drum hit is a burst of noise a few thousandths of a second long, and whether the cut can be told on it is for your ear: the button above plays it. Try a note that holds as well. Step 2 of part 4 measures the damage on a low tone, and this button plays the bass line turned up and cut off. <Hear set="bassClipped" label="Hear the bass turned up and cut off" />

/>

The second answer is the compressor of <Lesson id="compression" />, a device that turns loud parts down by itself. It fails here for a reason that lesson gives a whole fault to. A compressor measures a level over a stretch of time and then moves its gain, so the front of every hit has gone through before the gain arrives. That suits a device whose job is to shape a sound. A top that must not be passed needs three things a compressor does not have: the height of the wave itself and not its average, a gain that takes off exactly what stands over, and that gain already down when the peak arrives.

### A gain that is there first

A device with those three things is a limiter. It makes the sound wait a moment, and works out its gain from the copy that has not waited. So the gain is always a little ahead of the sound it is put on. When a peak is on its way, the gain slides down to meet it, holds while it passes, and climbs back afterwards. The level it will not let anything pass is called the ceiling.

The spaces between the hits got all of the Push. The hits got only what room there was under the ceiling, and the limiter took the rest back. So the loop is louder, and its hits stand less far above it than they did. That trade is what a limiter is for, and it is also what it costs.

<Part id="uses" title="Four kinds of sound, and what a ceiling does to each.">

A limiter does one thing, so its controls are few. Push decides how much of the sound meets the ceiling. Ceiling decides where the top is. Release decides how long the gain takes to come back after a peak. What changes from sound to sound is how often the gain is asked to move, and how far.

Each card below shows a sound and what a sensible setting does to it, lists that setting, and plays it. A card's button moves the whole page to its setting, so every figure further down shows it too, and the play bar offers the way back to where you were.

The quickest way to understand a limiter is to make one. This part begins with a gain and adds six pieces to it, one at a time.   <Step title="Work out the gain that would do it" controls="push ceiling">
    A clip changes samples one by one. A limiter changes a gain, and the first thing it needs is the right gain for a peak. For a peak over the ceiling that is the ceiling divided by the peak: the number that puts it on the ceiling exactly. For a peak under the ceiling it is 1.

    One gain goes on both channels, decided by whichever is higher, so the sound does not lean to one side when one side is limited. <Claim id="built-gain">For the tone of the step before, the gain now dips 6.0 dB</Claim> at every crest, where the step before had no gain to move.

    And nothing sounds better for it. A gain worked out afresh at every sample, from that sample alone, takes each sample that is over down to the ceiling and leaves every other as it was. On a sound that is the same in both channels, <Claim id="gain-is-clip">it makes the wave the clip made, and what is not the tone is still 12.7 dB under it</Claim>. The gain is the right idea with no memory.

    Now the gain gets a memory, of one kind. It may fall as fast as a peak asks. Around them it has what a device needs and the build left out: controls that glide when they are moved, a faster way to find the lowest of a window, and a rest after silence.

<StageBlock tap="peak" n={2} title="How high is the wave? The peaks between the samples">
  The last step of the build said why samples are not enough: the wave a converter makes of them can stand higher between two samples than either. That wave is the one smooth curve through all of them that holds nothing above half the sample rate, and <Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" /> shows why the samples settle it. Its height is called the true peak.

  The plainest case is a sine at a quarter of the sample rate, which gets four samples to a cycle. Sampled on its crests, the samples reach its full height. Sampled half way between a crest and a zero crossing, every sample lands at the same lower height, and all the crests fall in the gaps: the wave stands <Claim id="quarter-rate">3.01 dB</Claim> over every sample of it.

    minimum: [-24, 0],
    gain: [-24, 0],
  }}
  lines={{ peak: 'ceiling' }}
>
  Every stage on one time axis. Try any control, and notice which lanes change and which do not.
  Push changes every lane but the first. Ceiling and Release leave the sound after the wait as it
  was: they change the gains, from the fourth lane down or the fifth, and what comes out.
</Lanes>

To judge what a limiter did, compare three things: <Listen tap="delayed">what went in, pushed and delayed</Listen>, <Listen tap="out">what came out</Listen>, and <Listen tap="removed">what was taken away</Listen>, which is the first less the second.

### The same limiter, with a slow gain in front

The library does not ship this limiter by itself. It ships it as the last stage of the <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-limiter/ambient_limiter.h">Ambient Limiter</Src>, the device at the end of this page, which runs here beside the limiter on the same loop, at the same Push, and at the same Ceiling as far down as its own goes.

In front of the limiter that device puts a second gain, a slow one, called the ride. It watches the highest sample of the last <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-limiter/ambient_limiter.h" at="static constexpr float kHoldSeconds = 0.04f;">40 ms</Src>. When that stands over a line <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-limiter/ambient_limiter.h" at="static constexpr float kMarginDb = 0.5f;">0.5 dB</Src> under the ceiling, the ride turns the sound down towards the line with a time constant of <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-limiter/ambient_limiter.h" at="static constexpr float kDownSeconds = 0.25f;">a quarter of a second</Src>, and lets it back over <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-limiter/device.json" at={'"default": 1.5,'}>a second and a half</Src>. A sound that stays over the ceiling is ridden down and stays down. The limiter behind it, with its release fixed at <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-limiter/ambient_limiter.h" at="static constexpr float kWallReleaseMs = 80.0f;">80 ms</Src>, is left with the moments the ride was too slow for.

<Part id="ways" title="Six things people do with a ceiling.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the gain is doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Put it last, and hope it does nothing" tag="no Push at all" set="safety">
    At the end of a chain, with nothing pushed into it, a limiter is a guard against the peak nobody planned. On the mix <Claim id="way-safety">the highest sample stands 4.2 dB under full scale. With Push at 0 dB, and still at 3 dB, the gain is exactly 0 dB all through the loop. At 4 dB the deepest dip is 0.8 dB</Claim>. After the card, raise Push until the Gain lane of part 5 first moves. That is how much room the mix had.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Make it louder, a little at a time" tag="Push" set="mix">
    Every decibel of Push past the first peak is taken off the peaks and nowhere else. The first few are nearly free, because the tallest peaks are few and short. Each one after costs more, because the gain is down for more of the time. After the card, raise Push a decibel at a time and listen for the hits to stop standing out. Then go back two.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Leave room above the ceiling" tag="Ceiling, a little lower" set="room">
    The ceiling is held on the device's own ruler, and part 7 shows a finer ruler reading a little more. Whatever comes after the limiter can add to that: the Ambient Limiter's own notes on Ceiling say to <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-limiter/device.json" at="Lower it to leave headroom">lower it to leave headroom for whatever comes after</Src>. <Claim id="way-room">With Ceiling at −2 dB the engine's meter reads the mix within 0.01 dB of it, and a finer ruler finds the highest point of the wave 1.8 dB under full scale</Claim>.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Give low sounds a longer release" tag="Release" set="bass">
    The gain should stay down for longer than one cycle of the lowest note, or it moves inside the note and bends it. On a bass line, or on a mix with a bass in it, start with Release several times longer than for drums alone. After the card, shorten Release and listen to the notes for a buzz that nobody played.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Listen to what is taken away" tag="the difference" set="taken">
    The card plays only the part the limiter removed: the delayed sound less the output. <Claim id="way-taken">On the drums with Push at 6 dB, that part is 11.9 dB quieter than what is let through. At 12 dB the two are as loud as each other, within 0.3 dB</Claim>. A few ticks at the top of each hit are the price of a little loudness. When the taken part starts to sound like the music, a good deal of the music is being taken.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Let a slow gain take what stays loud" tag="Ride" set="ridden">
    A held sound pushed far over the ceiling keeps a limiter at work the whole time, and its gain is forever falling and climbing. A slow gain in front can sit still instead. <Claim id="way-ridden">On this pad the limiter alone moves its gain through 7.7 dB. With the slow gain in front, the slow gain stays between 7.74 dB and 9.2 dB down, and the limiter behind it goes no deeper than 1.4 dB</Claim>. The card plays the pad through the Ambient Limiter. For the same pad through the limiter alone: <Hear set="alone" label="Hear the limiter alone" />
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Five ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="A short release bends low notes" set="bent">
    The window of stage 5 holds the gain down for a millisecond and a half, and one cycle of a low note lasts far longer. With a short release the gain climbs back between one crest and the next and is knocked down again, once or twice in every cycle. A gain that moves inside a cycle changes the shape of the wave, and the note grows harmonics. <Claim id="fault-bent">On the saw wave the gain swings 4.2 dB in every cycle at a Release of 10 ms, and 0.5 dB at 400 ms</Claim>. On a plain tone, <Claim id="low-10">55 Hz and 7 dB over the ceiling, it swings 3.0 dB inside each half cycle at 10 ms</Claim> and <Claim id="low-100">0.57 dB at 100 ms</Claim>. Lengthen Release until the gain lies still across a note, or let a slow gain take the note, as the last card of part 6 does.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A long release ducks" set="ducked">
    The other way round, a long release leaves the gain down long after the peak that asked for it, and everything quiet that follows a loud hit is turned down with it. <Claim id="fault-ducked">With Release at 2 s the gain on these drums is at least 7.7 dB down all through the loop, and the loop comes out 4.6 dB louder than it went in. At 100 ms it comes out 8.5 dB louder</Claim>. The limiter has become a fader that somebody pulled down. Shorten Release until the gain gets home between hits. When a sound is pushed hard there is no release that avoids both this fault and the one before, and the cure is less Push.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Pushed too far" set="flat">
    Every decibel of Push past the ceiling comes off the peaks, so the hits lose their height against everything around them. <Claim id="fault-flat">With Push at 24 dB the highest sample of the drums comes out 11.0 dB over their level, where it went in 18.7 dB over it. The gain is at least 0.4 dB down all through the loop, and what is taken away is more than 11 dB louder than what is let through</Claim>. It is louder, and there is nothing left for it to be loud against. Turn your own volume down to match the level of the loop as played before deciding the Push helped.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The ruler is an estimate">
    Four points to a gap can miss a crest that falls between them, and a sum of twelve samples cannot follow the wave all the way to half the sample rate. So a finer ruler than the device's own can read the output a little over the ceiling. The section under these cards measures how much. The cure is room: the device starts its ceiling at <Src path="cpp/devices/true-peak-limiter/true_peak_limiter.h" at="static constexpr float kDefaultCeilingDb = -1.0f;">−1 dB</Src> and not at full scale, and the last decibel is there for what the estimate misses.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="It is late, and how late depends on the sample rate">
    Everything comes out of a limiter later than it went in, by the delay of stage 7. Played beside a sound that did not go through it, the two are out of step unless the other is held back by as much, which is why the device reports its delay. The look-ahead is a time, so the delay in samples is not one number: the limiter's notes give it as <Src path="cpp/devices/true-peak-limiter/true_peak_limiter.h" at="// the latency follows the sample rate: 71, 77 and 149 samples at 44.1, 48">71, 77 and 149 samples at 44.1, 48 and 96 kHz</Src>. The Ambient Limiter <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-limiter/ambient_limiter.h" at="static constexpr int kLookahead = 72;">fixes the look-ahead in samples</Src> instead, so its delay is the same at every rate.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

### The ruler, close to

The detector's four points are each a small filter, and a filter does not treat every frequency alike. This picture is how much of a steady tone each pair of points keeps, against the tone's frequency.

The two points in the middle of the gap <Claim id="inner-22k">keep a tone within 0.3 dB up to 20 kHz and have lost 3.0 dB of it at 22 kHz</Claim>. The two nearest a sample <Claim id="outer-22k">have lost 0.5 dB</Claim> there. Music has little up there, and noise has as much there as anywhere.

The gain is also moving while a peak goes by. So even the device's own ruler, laid on the output, reads the limited drums <Claim id="own-ruler-over">0.001 dB</Claim> over the ceiling: by then each of its twelve samples has been turned down by a slightly different gain.

A finer ruler finds more. The lab's test has one that owes the device nothing: a sinc that reaches <Src path="site/tests/labs/limiting.test.ts" at="const half = 32">thirty-two samples either side</Src>, read at as many points to the sample as asked. On the limited drums it reads <Claim id="sharper-ruler-4">0.14 dB over the ceiling</Claim> at four points to the sample and <Claim id="sharper-ruler-16">0.166 dB</Claim> at sixteen. White noise is the hard case: <Claim id="noise-over">pushed 12 dB it reads 1.8 dB over the ceiling on that ruler, while the engine's meter reads it within 0.01 dB of the ceiling</Claim>.

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A limiter is a compressor with its ratio at infinity.">
    Of the curve that is true: the line of stage 3 is flat above the ceiling, as such a compressor's would be above its threshold. The curve is the small part. The gain of the compressor of <Lesson id="compression" /> arrives after the front of a hit, as part 1 said. A limiter reads the height of the wave at one instant and has its gain down first. The picture of part 2 is the difference.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="If no sample is over the ceiling, nothing is over.">
    Stage 2 has a tone whose every sample is well under the ceiling and whose wave is over it, and the limiter turns it down. A device that only watched samples would let it through, and the converter after it would be asked for a height it does not have.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A true-peak limiter means no meter will ever read over.">
    It means the device's own ruler will not, and that ruler is four points to a gap. The section at the end of part 7 lays finer rulers on the same output and reads more, most of all on noise. That is what the room above the ceiling is for.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A limiter makes things louder.">
    It only turns down: <Claim id="only-down">the gain in the answer to part 1's question has no point above 0 dB</Claim>. The loudness comes from Push, and the limiter's part is to take back whatever of the Push would have gone through the ceiling. On the drums of part 1 it took back about half.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Look-ahead means it reacts before the sound happens.">
    Nothing sees the future. The sound is made to wait, and the gain is worked out from the copy that did not. The gain is early only against a sound that is late, and stage 7 measures how late. Everything else that plays alongside has to wait as long.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="It only touches the peaks.">
    There is one gain, and it is on everything. While it is down for a peak, whatever else is sounding at that moment is down with it, on both channels, and it stays down for as long as the release takes. <Claim id="mostly-down">On the drums as the page starts them the gain is more than 0.1 dB down for 90 % of the loop</Claim>. The peaks decide the gain. They are not the only thing it is put on.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
