A compressor is a hand on a fader that never tires. It listens to how loud the sound is, and when that passes a level you chose, it pulls the fader down by an amount you also chose. When the sound drops again it lets the fader back up. Everything else about a compressor is detail on three questions: **how loud is "loud"**, **how far down**, and **how fast**.

The signal splits in two. One copy is the sound itself, which only ever gets multiplied by a gain. The other copy, the *side chain*, is never heard: it exists to work out what that gain should be. These are its stages, in the order the signal meets them. Press Play, then Listen on any stage that is sound.

## Stage by stage

### 1. The side chain ignores the lows

Low frequencies carry most of the energy in music. A kick drum or a sub drone would otherwise decide the gain for everything else, and the whole mix would duck on every beat. So the first thing the side chain does is high-pass itself: a state-variable filter at 12 dB an octave (<Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h">the kit's `Svf`</Src>, with <M t={String.raw`Q = 1/\sqrt 2`} />, the flattest setting with no bump at the corner).

This filter is in the detector's path only. The lows are still in the sound you hear; they just don't get a vote.

### 2. How loud is it? Mean power, smoothed

A sample is not loud or quiet; it's a number that swings through zero thousands of times a second. Loudness is a property of a stretch of signal. The usual measure is the *root mean square*: square each sample (so negative and positive both count), average, and take the root. The compressor skips the root, because the next stage takes a logarithm anyway and a root is just a factor of two in decibels.

The two channels are linked: the device takes the mean of their squares, <M t={String.raw`\tfrac12(l^2 + r^2)`} />, so one gain is applied to both sides and the stereo image never moves.

For "average" it uses the cheapest averager there is, a one-pole low-pass on the power:

<M block t={String.raw`p[n] = x^2[n] + a\,\bigl(p[n-1] - x^2[n]\bigr), \qquad a = e^{-1/(\tau f_s)}`} />

Here <M t={String.raw`\tau`} /> is 30 ms and <M t={String.raw`f_s`} /> is the sample rate. Each new sample of power moves the average a small step towards itself; <M t="a" /> says how much of the old average survives. In the code this is <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h">`OnePole::lowpass`</Src>, one line: `state = x + (state - x) * a`.

One pole is not enough, and the reason is worth seeing. The square of a sine at frequency <M t="f" /> is a constant plus a cosine at <M t="2f" />: the power *wobbles* at twice the frequency of the sound. A single 30 ms pole lets a little of that wobble through, and a gain that wobbles at 110 Hz is no longer a fader, it's a modulator: it bends the wave and adds harmonics. So the device runs **two** 30 ms poles in a row. Choose the saw as the source to see it: it is the lowest note here, at 110 Hz, so its power wobbles at 220 Hz.

<Trace taps="levelOnePole:alt level:ctl" from={3} span={0.06} unit="dB">
  Sixty milliseconds of the level, in decibels: after the first pole (green) and after the second (blue). With the saw as the source the green line ripples at twice the note's frequency and the blue one is nearly flat.
</Trace>

For a 55 Hz sine, the lowest A of a bass, the wobble after one pole is <Claim id="ripple-one-pole">0.42 dB from trough to crest</Claim>; after the second it is <Claim id="ripple-two-poles">0.02 dB</Claim>, which is 52 dB under the level it rides on. That is why even the fastest attack cannot bend a bass note.

Then the power becomes a level in decibels, <M t={String.raw`L = 10\log_{10} p`} />:

<Trace taps="level" lines="threshold" min={-60} max={0} unit="dB">
  The level the detector reads across the whole loop, with the threshold as a dashed line. Whatever rises above the line is what the compressor will act on.
</Trace>

Decibels are the right unit from here on because hearing works in ratios: the threshold, the ratio and the knee are all statements about ratios of level. One thing to know when you set a threshold against this detector: a full-scale sine on both channels reads <Claim id="sine-reads">−3 dB</Claim>, not 0, because the mean of <M t={String.raw`\sin^2`} /> is one half.

### 3. How far down? The curve

Now the compressor knows the level <M t="L" />. The curve turns it into a gain change <M t="G" />, in decibels, zero or below. With <M t={String.raw`x = L - T`} /> the distance over the threshold <M t="T" />, ratio <M t="R" /> and knee width <M t="W" />:

<M block t={String.raw`s = \frac{1}{R} - 1 \qquad\qquad G(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x \le -W/2 \\[4pt] s\,\dfrac{(x + W/2)^2}{2W} & -W/2 < x < W/2 \\[8pt] s\,x & x \ge W/2 \end{cases}`} />

The slope <M t="s" /> is the heart of it. A ratio of 4:1 means that for every 4 dB the input rises over the threshold, the output may rise 1 dB; the other 3 dB are taken away, so <M t={String.raw`s = \tfrac14 - 1 = -\tfrac34`} /> dB of gain per dB over. At 1:1 the slope is 0 and nothing happens. As the ratio grows the slope approaches −1 and the output stops rising at all, which is a limiter (lesson 8).

A hard corner at the threshold is audible: a sound hovering around it flips between two behaviours. The *knee* replaces the corner with a parabola that starts half a knee below the threshold and ends half a knee above, chosen so that its value and its slope meet the straight lines at both ends. In the code this is the `if` ladder at the top of <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h">`step_gain`</Src>, term for term.

### 4. How fast? Attack and release

If the gain jumped to whatever the curve asked for, every tick, it would follow the level's every twitch and the sound would be crushed flat and distorted. A compressor is musical because it is *slow*, and slow in two different ways: how quickly it clamps down when the level rises (attack) and how quickly it lets go when the level falls (release).

Both are one more one-pole smoother, this time on the gain change in decibels, with a different time constant depending on direction:

<M block t={String.raw`G_k = G^{\text{target}}_k + c\,\bigl(G_{k-1} - G^{\text{target}}_k\bigr), \qquad c = \begin{cases} e^{-1/(\tau_{\text{attack}} f_c)} & \text{target below } G_{k-1} \\ e^{-1/(\tau_{\text{release}} f_c)} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}`} />

A time constant here means *63 % of the way in that time*: after one attack time the gain has covered 63 % of the distance to where the curve wants it, after two, 86 %, after three, 95 %. It never arrives; it gets closer by the same fraction every tick. <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h">`time_to_coeff`</Src> is that exponential.

Note the rate <M t="f_c" />. This smoother does not run on every sample. From the decibel conversion onward the device works on a *control clock*, once every 16 samples, 3,000 times a second at 48 kHz. Logarithms and powers are expensive and the gain of a compressor has no business changing faster than that.

Smoothing in decibels, not on the linear gain, is a choice. It makes a release from −12 dB take as long as one from −3 dB, which is how a hand on a fader with a decibel scale behaves, and it is why the reduction traces below are clean exponentials.

There is a catch with attack times that the device's header is careful about. Attack is the time constant of *this* smoother, but the detector in stage 2 needs time of its own to notice that the level has risen. With the attack at its shortest, 10 ms, a tone that starts from silence takes about <Claim id="attack-floor">39 ms</Claim> to bring down 63 % of its final reduction; at an attack of 100 ms, about <Claim id="attack-100">134 ms</Claim>. The detector adds roughly 30 ms to whatever you set. You can see it at the start of each drum hit:

<Wave taps="in out" from={0} span={0.12}>
  The first 120 ms of the loop. The front of the hit comes out as loud as it went in: the gain has not moved yet. Compressors on drums are often set slow on purpose for exactly this, to keep the crack and tame the body.
</Wave>

**Tails** is this device's own addition. The Ambient Compressor was built for sounds that swell and die away over seconds, and a classic release pulls a dying tail back *up* as it fades, flattening the shape of the decay. In its Hold setting the device pauses the release whenever the level is falling by more than 1.5 dB a second, so a tail keeps its own shape; it tells by comparing the level with a one-second average of itself. This lesson starts on Release, the textbook behaviour, so the traces above are the classic ones. Switch to Hold with the guitar chords playing and watch the red line stop rising while each chord rings out.

### 5. Back to a gain, without zipper noise

The reduction in decibels becomes a number to multiply by, <M t={String.raw`g = 10^{G/20}`} />. But it only changes once every 16 samples, and a gain that steps is a staircase multiplied onto the sound: at 3,000 steps a second that is a faint buzz, called *zipper noise*. So between ticks the device ramps the linear gain in a straight line: each tick it lands exactly on the last target and sets a per-sample step towards the new one. The price is that the gain is 16 samples, a third of a millisecond, behind. The sound itself is not delayed at all.

### 6. Make-up and mix

Compression only ever turns things down, so the result is quieter than the input. *Make-up* gain turns the compressed sound back up by a fixed amount. This is where compression earns its reputation for making things louder: pull the peaks down 9 dB, add 9 dB of make-up, and the peaks are where they were while everything quiet is 9 dB closer to them.

*Mix* blends the compressed sound with the untouched input:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{out} = \text{in}\cdot\bigl(1 - m + m\,g\,g_{\text{makeup}}\bigr)`} />

Dry and wet are the same signal at different gains, so this is a plain linear crossfade; an equal-power one would add 3 dB half way. With the mix at 0 the output is <Claim id="mix-zero">the input, sample for sample</Claim>. Settings in between are *parallel compression*: the dry signal keeps its sharp peaks on top while a heavily squeezed copy fills in underneath.

## What goes wrong

**Pumping.** With a short release and a loud, regular sound (the drums here, release at 0.1 s, threshold at −40 dB), the gain dives on each hit and rushes back up in the gap, dragging the quiet sound between hits up with it. You hear the room, the noise and the tails *breathe* in time with the beat. Sometimes that's the effect you want. When it isn't: lengthen the release, raise the threshold, or raise "Ignore below" so the kick stops deciding.

**Distortion from being too fast.** If attack and release were shorter than one cycle of the wave, the gain would change within the cycle and reshape it. This device makes that impossible on purpose: its attack stops at 10 ms and its detector has those two 30 ms poles in front. Faster designs need a look-ahead delay to stay clean.

**The first hit after silence.** A compressor that remembers its gain meets the next sound with whatever the last one left. This device forgets instead: after half a second of exact silence it rests, the reduction returns to 0 dB, and the detector is cleared. On silence its gain is <Claim id="silence">exactly 0 dB</Claim> and its output is exact silence.

**Louder is not better.** Make-up gain makes anything sound more exciting for a moment. Match the level by ear before deciding whether the compression helped.

## What to try

- Drums, threshold −30 dB, ratio 6. Sweep **attack** from 10 ms to 300 ms and listen to the front of each hit come back.
- Same settings, sweep **release** from 0.1 s to 3 s. Short pumps; long turns the compressor into a slow level control that barely moves.
- Guitar chords, **tails** on Hold and then Release. Watch the reduction trace while a chord dies away.
- The saw, and the level figure in stage 2: this is the ripple the second pole is there to remove.
- Noise, any settings: the level is constant, so the gain settles and stays. A compressor only does something interesting to a sound whose level changes.
- Set **ratio** to 1. Every figure goes flat and the red wave covers the grey. A ratio of 1:1 is a wire.
