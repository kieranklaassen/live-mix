<Part id="why" title="Music is louder and quieter than the place it has to fit.">

A guitar chord is struck and left to ring. The strike is the loudest thing in the bar. Two seconds later the same chord is still sounding, far quieter, and that last part of the ring is often the part worth hearing.

This page has a meter for how loud a sound is. It reads in decibels below full scale, the largest number a sample can be, and it measures the way the compressor's own detector will; part 5 says how. On the two chords of the loop below it reads <Claim id="chords-span">−17.7 dB at the strike and −51.7 dB at the end of the ring, which is 34 dB apart</Claim>.

<Still
  taps="meterIn"
  set="played"
  caption="Four seconds, two chords. Each strike stands at the top of the picture, and the ring falls away from it for two seconds."
/>

Listen to them first. <Hear set="played" label="Hear the chords as played" />

Then the recording leaves the room it was made in. It is played in a car, or in a kitchen with the tap running. Each of those places has a floor and a ceiling. Under the floor, a quiet sound is lost in the noise of the place. Over the ceiling, the speaker distorts or the converter runs out of numbers. The distance between the quietest and the loudest moment of a piece of music is its dynamic range, and a performance often has more of it than the listening place has room for.

So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you bring the end of the ring up without the strike going through the ceiling?

### The obvious answer fails

Turn it all up. A fader multiplies every sample by one number, so the end of the ring comes up, and the strike comes up by exactly as much. Here the chords are turned up until the loop measures <Claim id="fader-over">10 dB louder. The loudest sample of the strike now stands more than 4 dB over full scale, and the strike and the end of the ring are still 34 dB apart</Claim>. A converter has no number larger than full scale. Whatever passes it is cut off flat, which is distortion.

<Still
  taps="in out"
  set="fader"
  caption="The chords as played, and turned up. The numbers at the side count in full scales: the strikes of the output pass 1, the most a converter can carry."
/>

Turn your own volume down first: it is loud, and the strikes pass full scale. <Hear set="fader" label="Hear it turned up" />

Nothing was gained. The ring is louder and the strike is over the ceiling. The distance between them is what it was.

### First there was a hand

The first answer was a person. An engineer sat with a hand on the fader and the song in their head. Just before a loud phrase the hand came down a little, and in the quiet ones it went back up. This is called riding the gain. Done well, nobody hears it: they hear a player who is always there.

A hand has limits. It can follow a phrase but not a syllable, and it cannot follow a drum hit at all. It tires, and it does not do the same thing twice.

### Then the hand was built

So the hand was built as a machine. It listens to the level, and when the level is high it turns the sound down by itself. When the level falls it lets the sound back up. That is all a compressor is.

<Still
  taps="meterIn gain"
  set="evened"
  caption="The level of the chords, and the gain the engine's compressor puts on them. At each strike the level jumps up and the gain drops. As the ring dies away the gain climbs back."
/>

Each control of a compressor answers one of three questions that the engineer answered without thinking.

- **How loud is loud?** Below some level the hand stays still. Threshold sets that level. What "level" means for a sound that swings both ways thousands of times a second is a question of its own, and stage 3 of part 5 answers it.
- **How far down?** Once the sound is over the line, some of the excess is taken away. Ratio says how much, and Knee says whether that begins suddenly or gently.
- **How fast?** Attack is how quickly the hand moves when the sound gets loud. Release is how quickly it lets go again.

### And then everything could come up

There is a second half to the idea, and it is the reason compression ended up on nearly everything. Once the loud part is lower, the whole sound can be turned up without the loud part reaching the ceiling. Here are the same chords through the engine's compressor, followed by the same 10 dB that put them over full scale before.

  caption="The level going in and the level coming out. The end of each ring has come up a long way, and the strike has hardly moved. In the first second after a strike the ring comes out lower than it went in: the gain is still on its way back."

Measured on that render, <Claim id="evened-lift">the end of the ring comes out 9.2 dB higher than it went in and the strike 1.5 dB higher. The two are now 26.3 dB apart, and the loudest sample is 1 dB under full scale</Claim>. The fader alone gave the same lift to both, and put the strike over full scale doing it.

Compare it with the two before. <Hear set="evened" label="Hear it compressed, then turned up" />

Quieter. A compressor only turns down. With Make-up at 0 dB and the rest of that setting as it was, the chords come out <Claim id="quieter">11.7 dB quieter</Claim> than they went in, measured over the whole loop, and no sample is larger than it was.

Everything that comes out of a compressor louder was made louder by a plain gain that follows it. That gain is called make-up, because it makes up for what was taken. The compressor's part is to change how far the sound can come up before its loud moments reach the ceiling.

<Part id="picture" title="Level in along the bottom, level out up the side.">

One picture holds most of what a compressor does. Pick a level on the bottom edge, go straight up to the line, then across to the left edge: that is how loud it comes out. The diagonal is a wire, where a sound comes out as loud as it went in. A compressor follows the diagonal up to the threshold and then leans over. How far it leans is the ratio.

Check this against the picture. Leave the two controls where the lesson starts them, and find −12 dB on the bottom edge. Go up to the line and across. It comes out at <Claim id="curve-reads">−21 dB</Claim>. That tone went in 12 dB over the threshold and comes out <Claim id="ratio-4">3 dB over</Claim> it, and that is what a ratio of 4 means: of every 4 dB over the line, 1 dB is let through.

Turn Ratio to 1 and nothing happens at all. The higher the ratio, the flatter the line. A line that is completely flat lets nothing get louder than the threshold, which is a limiter, the subject of <Lesson id="limiting" />.

Notice what the picture leaves out: time. It says where the gain ends up for a level that holds still. It does not say how the gain gets there, and a played sound does not hold still. Most of what a compressor does to a played sound is in the part this picture leaves out, and that is where the rest of the lesson goes.

</Part>

    A tone exactly at the threshold was left alone by the corner. <Claim id="build-knee">With a knee of 6 dB at a ratio of 4 it now loses 0.56 dB</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="in" n={1} title="The sound splits in two">

<StageBlock tap="sidechain" n={2} title="The detector ignores the lows">
  Low frequencies carry most of the energy in music. Left alone, the kick drum and the bass would decide the gain for everything else, and the whole sound would dip on every beat. So the side chain filters its own copy first, with a high-pass at 12 dB an octave whose corner is set by Ignore below.

  The lows are still in the sound you hear. They just do not get a vote.

    with the dashed line while everything above it stays at 0 dB.
  </Spectrum>

    t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{|H(f)|^2} = \frac{(f/\at{ignore-cutoff}{f_c})^4}{1 + (f/\at{ignore-cutoff}{f_c})^4}`}
  />

  Read it against the picture. Far above the corner the top and the bottom of the fraction are nearly the same size, so it is close to 1 and nothing is lost: the flat part of the line. At the corner the fraction is one half, and half the power is 3 dB down. An octave under the corner the top is a sixteenth, and the line is falling by 12 dB for each further octave. Measured with steady tones through the running device, the side chain is <Claim id="corner-3db">3.0 dB down at the corner</Claim> and <Claim id="octave-under">12.3 dB down an octave under it</Claim>.

  The filter is the kit's state-variable filter, the subject of <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" />. The device sets it with the one resonance that gives this shape, with no bump at the corner.

<StageBlock tap="level" n={3} title="How loud is it? The mean of the squares">
  A single sample is not loud or quiet. A full-scale sine wave passes through zero twice in every cycle, and it does not go silent twice a cycle. Loudness belongs to a stretch of sound, so the detector needs an average, and the plain average of a wave that swings both ways is zero. Squaring fixes that. Every sample becomes positive, and the square of a signal is its power, which is what loudness follows.

  The two channels are squared and averaged together, so one gain goes on both sides and the stereo picture cannot wander. Then the power is smoothed, and read off in decibels.

  <Trace taps="level" lines="threshold" min={-60} max={0} unit="dB">
    The level the detector reads across the loop, with Threshold across it. Try Threshold lower, and
    notice how much more of the line stands above it: that part is all the compressor acts on.
  </Trace>

  The smoothing is a one-pole filter, the smallest filter there is and the subject of <Lesson id="one-pole" />. Each new value is the old value moved a little towards the input:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{ctl}{p[n]} = \sig{in}{x[n]} + \at{pole-step}{a}\,\bigl(\sig{ctl}{p[n-1]} - \sig{in}{x[n]}\bigr)`}
  />

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{pole-coeff}{a = e^{-1/(\tau f_s)}}`} />

  Here <M t="x" /> is the power going in, half the sum of the two channels' squares, and <M t="p" /> is the smoothed power. <M t="a" /> is a number just under 1 that says how much of the old value is kept. <M t={String.raw`\tau`} /> is the filter's time constant, which the device sets to <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h" at="static constexpr float kRmsSeconds = 0.03f;">30 ms</Src>, and <M t="f_s" /> is the sample rate. The filter is set by a corner frequency, so the device <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h" at="stage.set_cutoff(1.0f / (kit::kTwoPi * kRmsSeconds), sr);">asks for the corner</Src> that goes with that time, one over <M t={String.raw`2\pi\tau`} />, and in the filter's own line the <M t={String.raw`2\pi`} /> cancels.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/kit/filters.h"
    from="// One-pole lowpass, y += (1 - a)(x - y)"
    to="float highpass(float x) { return x - lowpass(x); }"
    mark="state = flush_denormal(x + (state - x) * a);"
    name="pole"
    at={{ coeff: 'a = std::exp(-kTwoPi * clamp(hz', step: 'state = flush_denormal(x + (state - x) * a);' }}
  />

  One pole is not enough. The square of a sine wave is a steady part plus a wobble at twice the sine's frequency, and one pole lets some of that wobble through. A gain that follows a wobble bends the wave it is put on. So the device runs a second pole of the same kind after the first.

  <Still
    taps="levelOnePole:alt level:ctl"
    source="saw"
    values={{ ratio: 1, scLowCut: 20 }}
    from={3}
    span={0.06}
    caption="Sixty milliseconds of a steady saw wave. The level after one pole ripples once in every cycle of the wave: the square of a saw wave repeats once a cycle, where the square of a sine repeats twice. After the second pole the ripple is nearly gone."
  />

  For a sine at 55 Hz, the lowest A of a bass, the level after one pole wobbles by <Claim id="ripple-one-pole">0.42 dB from trough to crest</Claim>, and after the second by <Claim id="ripple-two-poles">0.02 dB</Claim>. The device's own notes put that second wobble <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h" at="52 dB down after the two poles">52 dB under the level it rides on</Src>.

  Last, the power becomes a level in decibels, because hearing judges loudness in ratios and Threshold is marked in dB:

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{ctl}{L} = \at{curve-level}{10 \log_{10} p}`} />

  Ten, not twenty, because <M t="p" /> is already a power. That has a consequence worth remembering when setting a threshold. A full-scale sine wave on both channels has a mean power of one half, so it reads <Claim id="sine-reads">−3 dB</Claim>, not 0.

  The device works this level out on each reading and does not keep it. The level in this stage's pictures is the lab's, worked out in the same way from the smoothed power the device does keep, which is why this stage and the next say they were written for the lesson.
</StageBlock>

<StageBlock tap="target" n={4} title="How far down? The curve">
  Now there is a level, and the picture of part 2 becomes arithmetic. Call the distance of the level over the threshold <M t="x" />. The curve turns <M t="x" /> into a change of gain in decibels, and that change is zero or less: this is the stage that decides, and it can only decide to turn down.

  <Controls ids="threshold ratio knee" />

  <Curve
    taps="curve:out curveFormula:alt"
    diagonal
    follow="level"
    uprights="threshold"
    xTitle="level in, dB"
    yTitle="level out, dB"
    min={-60}
    max={0}
    height={320}
  >
    The curve measured on the device, and the curve the formula under this picture draws. Try Knee
    from 0 dB to its widest, and notice the corner at the threshold round off while both ends of the
    line stay where they were.
  </Curve>

  <Check id="curveError" />

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{G(x)} = \begin{cases} 0 & x \le -W/2 \\[4pt] \at{curve-knee}{s\,\dfrac{(x + W/2)^2}{2W}} & -W/2 < x < W/2 \\[8pt] \at{curve-over}{s\,x} & x \ge W/2 \end{cases}`}
  />

  <M block t={String.raw`s = \frac{1}{R} - 1`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{L_{\text{out}}} = \sig{ctl}{L} + \sig{alt}{G(L - T)}`} />

  <M t="R" /> is Ratio, <M t="T" /> is Threshold and <M t="W" /> is Knee, the width of the rounded part in decibels. The slope <M t="s" /> says how many decibels of gain are taken for each decibel over the line. At a ratio of 4 it is three quarters of one, taken away. At a ratio of 1 it is zero, and the curve is the diagonal.

  The three rows are the three parts of the line in the picture. The first is the diagonal: under the knee nothing is taken. The last is the leaning part, a straight line of slope <M t="s" />. The middle row is the knee, a parabola. It is chosen so that both its height and its slope meet the straight lines at each end, which is why the measured curve shows no kink where the knee begins or ends.

  Below the bottom of the knee the reduction is <Claim id="below-knee">exactly 0 dB</Claim>. The compressor is not almost off there. It is off.

  Read the listing against the formula: `slope_` is <M t="s" />, `knee_` is <M t="W" />, `over` is <M t="x" />, and the two comparisons are written with twice <M t="x" /> so that nothing has to be divided.

  <Listing
    slot="listing"
    path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h"
    from="const float level = power > kFloorPower"
    to="target = slope_ * into * into / (2.0f * knee_); }"
    name="curve"
    at={{ level: 'const float level = power > kFloorPower', over: 'target = slope_ * over;', knee: 'target = slope_ * into * into / (2.0f * knee_);' }}
  />
</StageBlock>

<StageBlock tap="reduction" n={5} title="How fast? Attack and release">
  If the gain jumped to whatever the curve asked for, the result would be step 3 of the build: a gain that copies every twitch of the level. So one more one-pole filter runs, this time on the reduction itself, in decibels. It has two time constants and picks between them on every reading: Attack when the curve asks for more reduction than there is now, Release when it asks for less.

  <Controls ids="attack release tails" />

  <Trace taps="target:alt reduction:out" min={-24} max={0} unit="dB">
    What the curve asks for, and the reduction in force. Try Attack longer, and notice the reduction
    arrive later and reach less deep on each hit. Try Release longer, and notice it stay down across
    the gaps.
  </Trace>

  <Check id="rebuilt" />

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{D[k]} = \sig{alt}{G[k]} + c\,\bigl(\sig{out}{D[k-1]} - \sig{alt}{G[k]}\bigr)`}
  />

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`c = \begin{cases} \at{ballistics-attack}{e^{-1/(\tau_{\text{attack}} f_r)}} & G[k] < D[k-1] \\[4pt] \at{ballistics-release}{e^{-1/(\tau_{\text{release}} f_r)}} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}`}
  />

  It is the filter of stage 3 again. <M t="G" /> is what the curve of stage 4 asks for on reading <M t="k" />, and <M t="D" /> is the reduction in force, how far down the gain is. On each reading <M t="D" /> closes part of the gap between them, and <M t="c" /> is the share of the gap that is left. <M t="f_r" /> is the rate of the readings, <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h" at="float tick_rate_ = 3000.0f;">3,000 a second</Src> at the page's sample rate. Each share is worked out when its control is set, by the kit's function for it:

  <Listing
    path="cpp/kit/math.h"
    from="// A time constant as a one-pole coefficient"
    to="return std::exp(-1.0f / (seconds * sample_rate)); }"
    name="coeff"
  />

  The check above is the lab's proof that it has read the device rightly. On every reading it takes the device's own detector power, works out the level, the curve and this filter again, and compares the result with the reduction the device stored. On the drums at the starting settings the two differ by <Claim id="rebuilt-zero">less than 0.001 dB</Claim>.

  A time constant is not the time a change takes. After one, the share of the gap that is left is <M t={String.raw`e^{-1}`} />, so the reduction has covered 63 % of the distance; after two, <M t={String.raw`1 - e^{-2}`} />, which is 86 %; after three, 95 %. And the detector in front of it has two poles of its own to get through first, which take longer the less far the sound stands over the threshold. Measured on the running device with a tone 18 dB over the threshold that starts from silence, the reduction takes <Claim id="attack-floor">39 ms</Claim> to cover 63 % of the way with Attack as short as it goes, and <Claim id="attack-100">134 ms</Claim> with Attack at a tenth of a second.

  This device adds one thing a plain compressor does not have. With Tails on Hold, the release is paused while the sound is dying away, which the device takes to mean that the level stands more than <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h" at="static constexpr float kDyingDb = 1.5f;">1.5 dB</Src> under its own average over the last <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h" at="static constexpr float kSlowSeconds = 1.0f;">second</Src>. A ring that is fading is left to fade at its own speed, and the gain does not creep up underneath it. This lesson starts on Release, which is how a textbook compressor behaves.

    set="evened"
    values={{ tails: 0 }}
    caption="The chords of part 1 again, with Tails on Hold. The gain line is nearly flat: it moves a little at each strike and then stays where it is while the ring dies away. In part 1, on Release, it climbed back."
  />

  On those chords, <Claim id="tails-hold">the gain on Hold stays between 19 dB and 20 dB down all through the loop. Just before the second strike it is still 20.0 dB down, where on Release it has come back to 0.9 dB down</Claim>.

<StageBlock tap="gain" n={6} title="From decibels to a number to multiply by">
  The reduction is in decibels and the sound is in samples, so the last step of the side chain turns one into the other.

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{ctl}{g} = \at{ramp-target}{10^{\,\sig{out}{D}/20}}`} />

  <M t="D" /> is the reduction of stage 5. Twenty this time, because <M t="g" /> multiplies an amplitude and not a power. A reduction of 6 dB is a gain of about one half.

  There is a catch. The reduction only changes once every 16 samples. A gain that moves in steps puts a small step into the sound at every reading, thousands of them a second, which is known as zipper noise. So between readings the device does not hold the gain still. It walks it in a straight line from the last value to the new one.

  <Curve taps="gainHeld:alt gainRamped:ctl" xTitle="samples" yTitle="gain">
    128 samples of the loop, taken where the gain moves most: the value the device aims at, which
    holds still between readings, and the gain it puts on the sound. Notice the second line reach
    each step of the first exactly one reading late.
  </Curve>

  The price is that the gain arrives 16 samples late, a third of a millisecond at the page's sample rate. The sound itself is not delayed at all.

  <Listing
    slot="listing"

<Part id="ways" title="Six things people do with these controls.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the curve and the gain are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Even out a performance" tag="threshold just under the loud phrases" set="line">
    The original job. A low ratio and a soft knee, with Threshold set so that the loud phrases lose a few decibels and the quiet ones lose less. After the card, raise Threshold until the trace of stage 5 only moves on the loudest phrases, and stop there.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Let the hit through" tag="a slower attack" set="hit">
    With a slower attack, more of each hit has passed before the gain arrives. On the drums <Claim id="way-hit">the gain dips 9.3 dB with Attack at 60 ms and 11.3 dB with it at 10 ms</Claim>, so the slower setting leaves more of each hit standing. After the card, shorten Attack and listen for the body of each hit shrinking.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Bring up the tail" tag="a short release, then make-up" set="evened">
    Let the gain come back while the sound rings, and turn the result up. The ring rises towards the strike: this is the setting of part 1. A long release does the opposite. It holds the gain down across the ring, and the ring keeps its distance from the strike. <Claim id="way-tail">With Release at 20 s the gain is still 19.1 dB down just before the second strike, and the strike and the end of the ring come out 34.4 dB apart, no closer than the 34 dB they went in</Claim>. Tails on Hold does the same on purpose, as stage 5 shows.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Hold a mix together" tag="a low ratio, a wide knee, slowly" set="mix">
    A decibel or two of reduction across everything at once. When one part gets loud the rest gives way a little, and separate parts begin to move as one. After the card, lower Threshold a little at a time. Listen for the point where the mix starts to sound held, and then for the point where it starts to sound small.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Compress beside the sound" tag="heavy settings, mix near a third" set="beside">
    Squash a copy hard, turn it up, and blend it under the untouched sound. <Claim id="way-beside">On the drums the compressed copy alone moves through 19.7 dB of gain. Blended, the gain on the whole moves through 5.7 dB, and is less than 3 dB up or down at any moment</Claim>. The hits keep their shape, because they are mostly the sound as it went in.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Keep the lows out of it" tag="Ignore below, raised" set="lows">
    If the whole sound dips with every bass note, the detector is listening to the bass. Raise Ignore below until the gain follows the part of the sound you care about. On the mix, with Threshold and Ratio left alone, <Claim id="way-lows">the gain dips 6.2 dB with Ignore below at 300 Hz and 11.1 dB with it at 20 Hz</Claim>.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Six ways it goes wrong, four of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Pumping" set="pump">
    With a short release and a loud, regular sound, the gain dives on each hit and rushes back in the gap. Everything quiet swells in time with the beat. <Claim id="fault-pump">On the drums the gain swings between 0.6 dB and 20.6 dB down on this setting. With Release at 1 s it stays between 10.1 dB and 20.6 dB down</Claim>, and the swelling stops. Some music wants the swell. When it is an accident, lengthen the release.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The front of the hit gets through" set="front">
    This compressor measures before it acts, and measuring takes time. By the time the gain moves, the first few hundredths of a second of a hit have passed at the gain that was there before it. <Claim id="fault-front">On the drums the gain goes as far as 11.3 dB down, and the loudest sample that comes out stands only 0.5 dB under the loudest that went in. They are not the same hit. The one that gets through follows a gap. The loudest sample going in follows close on another hit, meets the gain that hit left, and comes out 6.4 dB down</Claim>. If you like the hit, that is a gift, and part 6 uses it. If the top of the hit is the trouble, a compressor is the wrong tool. The tool that holds a ceiling is the limiter of <Lesson id="limiting" />.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Too much" set="flat">
    A threshold under everything, a high ratio and a lot of make-up. The compressor is at work the whole time, <Claim id="fault-flat">with the gain at least 9.0 dB down all through the loop. Going in, the loudest sample stood 18.4 dB over the level of the loop. Coming out, it stands 26.8 dB over it</Claim>. What stands up is the front of the one hit that met the least reduction, and everything else is pressed flat under it. Raise Threshold until the gain comes back to nothing between phrases, so that the sound has something to be loud against.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The noise comes up too" set="evened">
    Make-up raises everything that was quiet, and that includes the hiss and the room. In part 1 the end of each ring came up by nearly all of the make-up, which was the point. Anything else that was down there came up with it. Listen to the last half second of each chord. The cure is a quieter recording, or less make-up.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A gain that is too fast">
    A gain that changes within one cycle of a wave reshapes the wave, and that is distortion. This device is built to keep away from it. Its attack stops at <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/device.json" at={'"min": 10,'}>10 ms</Src>, and the two poles of stage 3 stand in front of it. A faster design has to read the sound ahead of time to stay clean.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The first hit after a silence">
    A compressor that remembers its gain meets the next sound with whatever the last one left behind. This one rests after <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-comp/ambient_comp.h" at="static constexpr float kRestSeconds = 0.5f;">half a second</Src> of silence: the reduction goes back to nothing and the detector is cleared. Fed silence, its gain is <Claim id="silence">exactly 0 dB</Claim> and its output is exact silence.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A compressor makes things louder.">
    It only turns down. The loudness comes from the make-up after it: with Make-up at 0 dB the chords of part 1 came out quieter, and the answer to that part's question has the number. What the compressor changes is how far the sound can come up before its loud moments reach the ceiling.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A ratio of 4 means four times quieter.">
    The ratio works only on the part above the threshold, and in decibels. The sentence under the curve of part 2 is the case to remember: a tone goes in 12 dB over and comes out 3 dB over. A sound below the knee is not touched at all, as stage 4 shows.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="An attack of 10 ms means it has reacted in 10 ms.">
    After one time constant the gain is 63 % of the way there, and the detector adds its own time in front. Stage 5 measures it on the running device: at the shortest setting, for a tone 18 dB over the threshold, it is nearly four times what the control says.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The threshold is a peak level.">
    The detector measures the mean of the squares, smoothed. A full-scale sine wave reads 3 dB under its own peak, as stage 3 shows, and a single full-scale sample reads <Claim id="click-reads">−36 dB</Claim>, far under its own height. So a threshold lets peaks well above itself through. That is the fault of the front in part 7.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="It only changes the volume, so it cannot change the tone.">
    A slow gain is a change of volume. A gain that moves within one cycle of the wave multiplies the wave by a changing number, and that makes frequencies that were not there. It is why the level is smoothed twice: the ripple in the picture of stage 3 is what such a gain would follow.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Mix at one half is half the compression.">
    Mix blends amplitudes, not decibels. <Claim id="mix-half">A tone that the compressor turns down 12 dB comes out 4.1 dB down at a mix of one half</Claim>, not 6. The second line in the picture of stage 7 shows the same thing for every level at once.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
