<Part id="why" title="One echo is a rattle. A room is thousands of them, and more every moment.">

Clap once in a tiled stairwell. What comes back is not a second clap. It is a wash that starts at once and thins away over a second or two. <Lesson id="plate-reverb" cap /> says what that wash is made of: reflections off every surface, a few at first and then too many to count.

A computer has no walls. What it has is memory, and the cheapest thing memory can do with a sound is say it again later. So here is the question of this lesson. How do you get from a machine that repeats a sound to one that surrounds it, without a thousand separate repeats to look after?

The answer has a long name, the _feedback delay network_, and a short recipe: a few delay lines, and a rule for sharing what comes out of them.

### First attempt: one line, fed back

<Lesson id="delay-and-echo" cap /> builds the simplest thing that keeps a sound alive. A delay line hands back what it was given some time ago. Send what it hands back into its own input, a little quieter, and the sound goes round and round.

The picture is what that does to a click. A click is one sample standing up from silence, the shortest sound there is, so whatever comes out is the machine's answer to a single instant.

The line here is the shortest of the eight this lesson's device has. With Decay at 1.5 s, where the page starts, each echo is <Claim id="comb-step">5.78 dB</Claim> under the one before, and the echoes never get closer together. Seen as a spectrum, a row of even echoes is a row of even peaks, which is why this is called a _comb_. This comb has a tooth every <Claim id="comb-teeth">9.19 Hz</Claim>. Nine repeats a second is a rhythm, not a note and not a room.

Listen to the drums as they were played, then through the one line. <Hear set="dry" label="Hear the drums" /> <Hear set="one-line" label="Hear them through one line" /> Listen for every hit said again, and again, at one fixed distance.

### Second attempt: more lines

One line gives one row of echoes. Eight lines of eight different lengths, side by side, give eight rows. If the lengths have nothing to do with each other the rows do not fall into step.

  taps="bare"
  values={{ matrix: 0 }}

  from={0}
  span={1}
  caption="The same click through eight lines of unlike lengths, each fed back to itself. Half of the lines are heard on this side, so four rows of echoes are laid over each other. There are more echoes, and each row still keeps its own even step."
/>

It is busier, and it has the same fault. Count the echoes that reach one side after a click, an echo being any sample that is not exactly zero: <Claim id="echo-apart">12 in the first half second and 15 in the next</Claim>. The count goes on at that rate for as long as anything can be heard. In a room the number arriving each moment keeps growing, and that growth is what turns separate echoes into a tail. <Hear set="eight-apart" label="Hear the drums through eight lines" /> Listen for a flutter behind each hit: more repeats, none of them a wash.

Adding lines does not cure it. A hundred lines would give a hundred rows, each as regular as the first, and a hundred times the memory.

### The rule that works

Keep the eight lines. Change one thing: stop sending each line's output back into its own input. Take all eight outputs, make eight different sums and differences of them, and send one of those into each line. Nothing is added to the sound by this. The sums are scaled so that the eight numbers that go back in hold the energy of the eight that came out, which stage 5 of part 5 measures.

<Predict
  question="Each fed back to itself, the eight lines send 15 echoes to one side in the second half second after a click. Share every output over all eight inputs instead, adding no energy. How many echoes arrive in that same half second?"
  choices={[
    '15 still: sharing moves sound about and makes none',
    'About 120: eight times as many',
    'Nearly 2,000',
  ]}
>

Nearly 2,000. Measured on a network with nothing in it but the eight lines and the sharing, <Claim id="echo-density">117 echoes arrive in the first half second and 1,979 in the second, where the unshared lines gave 12 and 15</Claim>.

The reason is a chain. An echo that comes out of one line now goes into all eight. Each of those eight comes out later, at eight different moments, and each goes into all eight again. One echo becomes eight, then sixty-four, then five hundred and twelve. The energy is the same as before. It has been cut into more and more pieces, which arrive closer and closer together.

The sharing is done by a matrix, a square table of numbers. The lines with their matrix are the network, and the engine's device puts four more things round it, which part 4 adds one at a time. <Hear set="network" label="Hear the drums through the whole device" />

Two things the device does by default are switched off where this lesson starts, so that the network shows by itself: the _breath_, which opens and closes the input, and the slow sway of the line lengths. Both come back as controls, and part 9 sets the lesson's starting settings beside the device's own.

The network in these two pictures is not the device. It is a bare one written for this lesson out of the engine's kit, its delay line and its two matrices, with the device's eight lengths and nothing else round them. Mixing, and the Gain of part 5, are the lab's own controls and act on this bare network only. The device has neither.

<Lesson id="plate-reverb" cap /> counts another way: a sample is an echo there when it is larger than both its neighbours and within 60 dB of the largest of the same 10 ms. That count is for a finished reverb, whose answer soon has hardly a zero left in it. This one is for a bare network, where the zeros between the echoes are what the sharing fills. By that count, <Claim id="density-peaks">the device's own answer to a click holds 160 echoes in every 10 ms on average over the 400 ms from 200 ms on, which is what white noise counted the same way holds</Claim>.

Mixing has three positions. None sends each line back into itself. Hadamard and Householder are two matrices that share the lines out, and part 5 writes both down. Size makes every line longer or shorter. Longer lines mean fewer trips a second, and each trip is one more round of sharing, so the count climbs more slowly.

The second picture is the sound those counts belong to: the answer of the same bare network to the same click, now with the gain that Decay sets.

These two pictures are answers to a click, and a click holds very little energy. Put one through the device itself at the starting settings and the tail, at its loudest, is <Claim id="tail-quiet">25 dB</Claim> under the click. That is why the buttons on this page play drums and the pictures draw clicks.

<Aside title="What this page's loop leaves out">

The page plays a loop of two seconds, and a tail can be longer than that. So the lab plays the loop through the device twice and keeps the second pass, which begins with the tail of the first already sounding. On the drums at the starting settings that tail is <Claim id="second-pass">23.5 dB</Claim> under the output over the first half second.

A loop left running would have every earlier pass in it, not one. <Claim id="older-passes">With Decay at 1.5 s the passes before that one would add a part more than 100 dB under the output. With Decay at 20 s they would add a part 19 dB under it</Claim>, measured by giving the lab the loop twice over. At long decays the page plays a little less tail than a loop left running would gather.

<Part id="uses" title="Three kinds of sound, and the room each is given.">

A feedback delay network is a room with no walls to recognise. Once its matrix is in, its echoes no longer come in even rows, which were the flutter of part 1. What it has is a length, a size and a tone, which here are Decay, Size and Damping. That makes it the reverb to reach for when a sound only needs somewhere to be.

It has one habit to know before using it. Its lines are long, so nothing comes out until the shortest has been travelled once, and the tail that follows is thin for its first moments. The diffusers of part 4 are there to thicken it. How much of it is left to hear depends on how much front the sound has.

Each card shows a sound with the device's output drawn behind it, lists a setting to start from, and plays it. A card's button moves the whole page to its setting, and the play bar offers the way back.

The quickest way to understand the network is to make one. This part starts with the comb of part 1 and adds six pieces to it. After each piece the pictures are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

The build is fed what the device's network is fed, which is the loop as one channel. Two pictures follow each step. <Build>
  <Step title="One comb" controls="decay size">
    A delay line is memory that is written at one place and read some places behind it, where the sound of that long ago is. <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" cap /> builds one from nothing.

    The first piece is one line and the two functions every later piece will use, `read` and `write`. The line is the kit's <Src path="cpp/kit/delay.h" at="class DelayLine {">`DelayLine`</Src>. The device keeps <Src path="cpp/devices/fdn-reverb/fdn_reverb.h" at="struct DelayLine {">a line of its own</Src> in its header, which stores and reads in the same way. What is written is the new sound plus what the line has just given back, times a gain under 1. That is a comb.

    <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[n]} = \at{comb-read}{w[n-M]}`} />

    <M block t={String.raw`\at{order-feed}{w[n]} = \sig{in}{x[n]} + g\,\sig{out}{y[n]}`} />

    Read the two lines against the picture. <M t="x[n]" /> is the sound going in at sample <M t="n" />, and <M t="y[n]" /> is what the line gives back. <M t="w[n]" /> is what is written into the line, and the first equation says the line gives it back <M t="M" /> samples later: <M t="M" /> is the line's length. The second says what is written: the new sound, and the echo times the gain <M t="g" />. In the curve each spike is one trip, <M t="M" /> samples after the last and <M t="g" /> times its height.

    The gain is not chosen by hand. It comes from Decay, by a formula that stage 6 of part 5 takes apart. Until then it is enough that a longer Decay puts it nearer 1. Size multiplies the length.

  <Step title="The mixing matrix" controls="decay size">
    The third piece is one line of the build and one function of the engine's kit. Before the eight outputs go back in, they are replaced by eight sums and differences of all eight.

    The function works in three passes. The first takes the eight numbers in neighbouring pairs and replaces each pair by its sum and its difference. The second does the same to results two places apart, and the third to results four places apart. After three passes every result holds all eight inputs, each one added or subtracted, and no two results have the same pattern of signs. The last line scales everything down so that the eight results hold the energy of the eight inputs and no more. Stage 5 of part 5 writes it as a matrix, and lists the device's own function, which is the same three passes written out by hand.

  <Step title="Damping" controls="decay size damping">
    In a room the high frequencies die first. Air and soft surfaces take a little more from them at every reflection. The fourth piece copies that with the smallest filter there is, the one-pole low-pass of <Lesson id="one-pole" />, on what goes back into each line.

    <M block t={String.raw`\at{damp-pole}{p_i[n]} = (1-a)\,v_i[n] + a\,p_i[n-1]`} />

    Here <M t="v_i[n]" /> is what the last step wrote into line <M t="i" />: the new sound, and the line's share from the matrix times the gain. <M t="p_i[n]" /> is what is written in its place: mostly the last thing written, <M t="p_i[n-1]" />, and a little of the new. The nearer the coefficient <M t="a" /> is to 1, the slower <M t="p_i" /> follows, and fast movement, which is high frequency, is what it loses. Damping sets <M t="a" />.

    One trip through this filter costs the highs very little. A sound that lasts makes many trips and pays on each. Damping starts at 0, where the filter stands at the top of hearing and takes almost nothing, so raise it. <Claim id="build-damping">With Damping at 0.4 the build's answer to a click falls 22 dB a second faster in the band around 8 kHz than in the band around 500 Hz</Claim>, read between a quarter of a second and one second after the click.

  <Step title="Diffusion" controls="decay size damping">
    Even with the matrix, a tail begins as a few separate echoes, because one trip is all the sharing the first of them have had. The fifth piece does not touch the loop. It stands ahead of it and turns every sample into a short burst before the lines hear it. It cannot fill the wait before the shortest line delivers, which stays as long as that line. It thickens what comes after.

    It is four all-pass filters in a row. An all-pass lets every frequency through at full level and changes only when each one arrives. <Lesson id="plate-reverb" cap /> explains this filter and why a reverb puts a few of them at its door. Each of the four holds a short delay line of its own, and their lengths and coefficients are the device's.

<StageBlock tap="shared" n={5} title="The matrix: every line into every line">
  This is the rule of part 1 and the third piece of part 4, written as a matrix. The eight line outputs are multiplied by it, and the eight results go back towards the eight inputs.

  Not any matrix will do. The lines store what is written into them and give it back later. A matrix that could make its output larger than its input, round after round, would make the network grow without limit. The safe choice is a matrix that keeps energy: the sum of the squares of the eight numbers going in equals the sum of the squares coming out, whatever the numbers. Such a matrix only turns the eight numbers about. The loss round the loop is then set by the gain of stage 6 alone, and that gain can be chosen for the decay time without a thought for the matrix.

  The device's is built by doubling, starting from two by two:

  <M

  />

  <M

Two doublings from <M t="H_2" /> give the eight by eight the device uses, which has the standard name of a Hadamard matrix. ### When nothing is going in

One more thing the device does is in none of these stages. A tail that is left alone never reaches zero by arithmetic: each trip multiplies it by a number under 1. So wherever the network stores a number the engine <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h" at="inline float flush_denormal(float x) {" to="return (x > -kDenormalThreshold && x < kDenormalThreshold) ? 0.0f : x;">writes anything too small to matter as an exact zero</Src>, as step 1 of part 4 said. Given silence, the lines empty themselves, and then <Claim id="silence">silence in is exact silence out, every sample a zero</Claim>.

When nothing has gone in and both sides of the wet signal have been exact zeros for <Src path="cpp/devices/fdn-reverb/fdn_reverb_device.h" at="static constexpr float kIdleHoldSeconds = 2.0f;">2 s</Src>, the device goes to sleep. It stops running the network and puts out silence until sound arrives again, and while it sleeps it keeps <Src path="cpp/devices/fdn-reverb/fdn_reverb.h" at="inline void FdnReverb::advance_modulators(int frames) {">turning the breath and the eight sines</Src>, so that they stand where they would have stood. The kit's <Src path="cpp/kit/idle.h" at="class IdleGate {">`IdleGate`</Src> keeps the count.

The lab found a case where the lines never empty. It is reached only through the lab's own Wander, so the device as shipped does not meet it. With the lines still, a length that is a whole number of samples leaves the read of stage 4 a fraction of nothing, and <M t={String.raw`\eta`} /> is exactly 1. Put that into the read's equation with nothing left in the line: what it returns is its own last answer with the sign turned, as large as it was. So the small errors of rounding that the read's sums have left in that answer are never lost. They go on, sign turned on every sample, and they are far larger than anything the engine writes as zero.

<Claim id="still-residue">After a click with Decay at 0.1 s, line 1 is left holding 0.00000005, which is under −140 dB, and the output still carries it 7.9 s after the click. After the drums what is left is under 0.000001</Claim>. What it changes is that the device stays awake: the output is never the exact zeros the device waits for. The test finds the same with Size at 0.5, 1.5 and 2, each of which leaves some length whole. With Size at 1.01, or with the lines moving, every line empties to exact zeros.

  <Way title="Near walls" tag="Size down, Decay as it was" set="near">

<Part id="wrong" title="Five ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="A ring behind every hit" set="ring">
    This is the network without its matrix: eight lines, each fed back to itself. Every hit is followed by eight even rows of echoes. Listen for each row as a flutter or a low buzz. The device cannot be set like this, because its matrix is always in, but a network whose lines fall into step goes the same way. Put Mixing back on Hadamard and listen for the rows dissolving.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A late, thin start" set="sparse">
    With Size at its largest the shortest line is over two tenths of a second long, as the first card of part 6 measured. A sharp hit gets silence, then a burst, then another, before the matrix has had the trips it needs to fill the gaps. On the drums, listen for a slap of echo ahead of the reverb. Lower Size until the start closes up, and get the length back from Decay.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A held note that swims" set="swim">
    A long steady note shows what the movement of the lines costs. Its level drifts up and down by a few decibels as the resonances slide past it, as stage 4 of part 5 measured, and its pitch bends a little with every line. A sound that is itself moving hides this. A held sine does not, so listen for it there. The device has no control to stop it, so the cure is less of the wet signal: lower Mix.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A long Decay that falls short">
    Decay is a promise the device keeps only roughly, and less well the longer it is. At a long setting the gain takes very little on each trip, so the little that the filters of stage 7 take at the two ends of the spectrum is a large part of the whole. <Claim id="decay-long">With 20 s set and Damping at 0, the level of a click's tail, every frequency together, falls between 1 s and 7 s after the click at the rate of 60 dB in 16 s</Claim>. <Claim id="decay-long-bands">Around 1 kHz the same tail falls at the rate of 60 dB in 19 s, and around 8 kHz in 11 s</Claim>. If a tail has to last a given time, measure it and do not trust the number on the control.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A loud sound into a long tail">
    With a long Decay the lines hold a great deal of what has gone in, and each new loud sound is added to it. When what is written into a line comes near full scale, the soft limit of stage 8 bends it, on that trip and on every trip after, until the tail has died down. Listen for a tail that turns rough. Lower the level going in, or shorten Decay, before reaching for anything else.
  </Fault>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="More delay lines make a longer reverb.">
    The length of the tail is set by how much one trip keeps and how long a trip takes, and Decay works the first out from the second. Lines add echoes, not time: in stage 6 of part 5 one line with a gain from its own length dies in 1.50 s, and so do eight. What more lines buy is how soon the echoes run together, and how even the tail is across frequency.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Feeding eight lines into each other must make it louder.">
    It would, with a careless matrix. The two matrices here only turn the eight numbers about: stage 5 of part 5 holds both to it. Sharing cuts the same energy into more pieces. The count of echoes in part 2 climbs steeply, and no energy is added to make them.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Decay is how long the tail lasts.">
    Decay is the time the middle of the spectrum takes to fall to a thousandth, and stage 6 of part 5 measures how nearly the device keeps to it. The highs go sooner by as much as Damping asks, and the lowest bass goes sooner whatever Damping says, as stage 7 measures.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="With Damping at 0 there is no filter in the loop.">
    At 0 the low-pass stands at the top of hearing and still takes a little from every trip, and the filter that guards against a steady offset is there whatever Damping says. Stage 7 of part 5 measures both.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The line lengths have to be prime numbers.">
    Primes are a convenient way to be sure that no two lengths share a factor. At this page's rate the device's lengths are not primes, and stage 3 of part 5 found that it costs nothing a tail of ordinary length can show. What has to be avoided is two lines in a simple ratio, whose echoes would land together from the start.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A reverb is one answer to a click, so one recording of that answer holds all of it.">
    That is true of a room, and of this network with its lines held still. With the lines moving, as the device always has them, two clicks a second apart get two different answers: stage 4 measures how different. <Lesson id="convolution-reverb" cap /> is the reverb made from one recorded answer, and it is the next lesson. It can copy a room exactly, and it cannot copy this.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
