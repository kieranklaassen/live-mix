An equaliser turns some frequencies up or down and leaves the rest alone. One knob marked "tone" does that, and so does a desk with forty bands. Underneath, nearly all of them are the same small filter used several times over: a filter that remembers two samples, called a *biquad*. Give it five numbers and it is a bell that lifts one band, a shelf that tilts one end of the spectrum, or a cut that removes everything past a frequency.

The five numbers are the interesting part. They are not a frequency, a gain and a width. They are weights in a sum of recent samples, and a *recipe* turns the one into the other. This lesson takes one biquad apart, reads the recipes the kit uses, and then puts six filters in a row, which is all the tone half of the Ambient EQ is.

The device does more than a textbook equaliser. It also has *Clear*, a listener that turns down narrow bands that ring out above the sound around them, and Clear is on by default. Here it starts at 0, so the plain bands show first, and it comes back as a control in stage 7. These are the device's stages in the order the sound meets them. ## Stage by stage

### 1. One biquad: five numbers and two memories

A biquad makes each output sample from five things: the input now, the two inputs before it, and its own two previous outputs.

<M block t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} y[n] = {} & b_0\,x[n] + b_1\,x[n-1] + b_2\,x[n-2] \\ & - a_1\,y[n-1] - a_2\,y[n-2] \end{aligned}`} />

The three <M t="b" /> terms are a weighted sum of recent input: they can only mix what came in. The two <M t="a" /> terms feed the output back into itself, and that is what lets a filter with two samples of memory act on a wave whose cycle lasts hundreds of samples: each output carries a little of every output before it.

Written that way the filter remembers four samples. <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="114-128">The kit's `Biquad`</Src> remembers two, <M t="z_1" /> and <M t="z_2" />, in an arrangement called *transposed direct form II*:

<M block t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} y &= b_0\,x + z_1 \\ z_1 &\leftarrow b_1\,x - a_1\,y + z_2 \\ z_2 &\leftarrow b_2\,x - a_2\,y \end{aligned}`} />

Each memory holds the part of the coming outputs that is already known. Follow one input through: it reaches the output at once through <M t="b_0" />, one sample later through <M t="z_1" />, and two samples later through <M t="z_2" /> by way of <M t="z_1" />. Those are the three <M t="b" /> terms, and the output travels the same road through <M t="a_1" /> and <M t="a_2" />. It is the same equation with half the memory. The lab runs both on the left channel of the loop, the kit's form and the equation as written above, and they differ by <Claim id="forms-agree">about 110 dB under the sound</Claim>, which is the two ways of rounding and nothing else.

### 2. Poles and zeros: why a bell boosts

Feed a biquad a steady sine and a sine of the same frequency comes out, scaled and shifted. How much it is scaled is the filter's *response* at that frequency. For a sine, a delay of one sample is a turn by a fixed angle, so write <M t={String.raw`z^{-1}`} /> for "one sample ago" and the equation of stage 1 becomes a ratio of two quadratics, which is where the name biquad comes from:

<M block t={String.raw`H(z) = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}}`} />

A frequency <M t="f" /> is the point <M t={String.raw`z = e^{\,j 2\pi f / f_s}`} /> on a circle of radius 1: 0 Hz at one side, half the sample rate <M t="f_s" /> opposite. Each quadratic has two roots. The roots of the top are the filter's *zeros*, the places where nothing would come out. The roots of the bottom are its *poles*, where the output would be without limit. In a bell or a shelf neither lies on the circle, but as the frequency passes near a zero the response dips, and as it passes near a pole it swells. A pole also has a plain meaning in time: its angle is the frequency the filter rings at when left alone, and its distance from the centre is how much of that ring is left after each sample.

A bell has a pair of zeros and a pair of poles at the same angle, the angle of its centre frequency, at different distances from the centre. Far from that angle the two pairs are equally far away and cancel. Close to it the nearer pair wins. In a boost the poles are nearer the circle; in a cut the zeros are. For a bell of +6 dB at 1 kHz with a Q of 2, the poles stand <Claim id="pole-radius">0.977 from the centre and the zeros 0.955</Claim>.

### 3. The bell's recipe

A recipe goes from what you want (a centre <M t="f_0" />, a gain <M t="G" /> in decibels, a width <M t="Q" />) to the five numbers. The kit's bell is <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="149-155">`set_peak`</Src>:

<M block t={String.raw`w = 2\pi\frac{f_0}{f_s} \qquad \alpha = \frac{\sin w}{2Q} \qquad A = 10^{G/40}`} />

<M block t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} b_0 &= (1 + \alpha A)/a_0 & a_0 &= 1 + \alpha / A \\ b_1 &= -2\cos w\,/a_0 & a_1 &= -2\cos w\,/a_0 \\ b_2 &= (1 - \alpha A)/a_0 & a_2 &= (1 - \alpha / A)/a_0 \end{aligned}`} />

Term by term. <M t="w" /> is the angle of the centre frequency on the circle, and the <M t={String.raw`-2\cos w`} /> that the top and the bottom share puts both pairs of roots at that angle. <M t={String.raw`\alpha`} /> is the width: it says how far inside the circle the roots stand, so a small <M t="Q" /> pulls them inwards and broadens the bell. <M t="A" /> carries the gain, and it goes in twice: the zeros get <M t={String.raw`\alpha A`} /> and the poles <M t={String.raw`\alpha / A`} />, which moves one pair out and the other in by the same factor. At the centre everything else cancels and the response is <M t={String.raw`\alpha A`} /> over <M t={String.raw`\alpha / A`} />, exactly <M t="A^2" />, which is why <M t="A" /> is <M t={String.raw`10^{G/40}`} /> and not <M t={String.raw`10^{G/20}`} />. At 0 Hz and at half the sample rate the top and the bottom come to the same number, so a bell is exactly 0 dB at both ends whatever its settings. Dividing by <M t="a_0" /> makes the bottom start with 1, as the equation of stage 1 wants; the kit's `normalise` does that. Before any of it, <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="184-188">`prepare`</Src> holds the frequency between 5 Hz and 0.49 of the sample rate and <M t="Q" /> at 0.05 or more.

  The bell's curve, measured: one sample through the kit's filter, and the level of each frequency in what came out. Gain moves the top, Q squeezes the sides, and the dashed line follows the frequency.

A +6 dB bell at 1 kHz measures <Claim id="bell-centre">+6.00 dB at 1 kHz</Claim>. <M t="Q" /> is the centre frequency over the width, where the width is taken between the two frequencies at which the bell is half its decibels up: at a Q of 2 those are <Claim id="bell-width">781 Hz and 1,280 Hz, 499 Hz apart</Claim>. Two octaves either side, at 250 Hz and 4 kHz, <Claim id="bell-two-octaves">0.11 dB</Claim> is left at a Q of 2. At 0.7, the Q the device gives its bells, <Claim id="bell-two-octaves-broad">0.8 dB</Claim> is left: a broad bell reaches a long way. A cut is the same recipe with the sign of <M t="G" /> turned, which swaps the zeros and the poles, so a cut undoes its boost: the two curves add to 0 dB <Claim id="bell-mirror">within 0.001 dB everywhere</Claim>.

### 4. Shelves and cuts

A shelf lifts or lowers everything on one side of a frequency and leaves the other side alone. It is the same biquad with another recipe, <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="157-168">`set_low_shelf`</Src>. With <M t="w" /> and <M t="A" /> as before, <M t={String.raw`\alpha`} /> taken at a fixed <M t={String.raw`Q = 1/\sqrt 2`} />, and <M t={String.raw`\beta = 2\sqrt{A}\,\alpha`} />, all six still to be divided by <M t="a_0" />:

<M block t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} b_0 &= A\,[(A+1) - (A-1)\cos w + \beta] \\ b_1 &= 2A\,[(A-1) - (A+1)\cos w] \\ b_2 &= A\,[(A+1) - (A-1)\cos w - \beta] \\ a_0 &= (A+1) + (A-1)\cos w + \beta \\ a_1 &= -2\,[(A-1) + (A+1)\cos w] \\ a_2 &= (A+1) + (A-1)\cos w - \beta \end{aligned}`} />

It is longer and reads the same way. Add the three <M t="b" /> and the three <M t="a" /> (that is the response at 0 Hz, where <M t="z = 1" />) and their ratio is <M t="A^2" />, the whole gain. Turn the sign of the middle one in each (half the sample rate, <M t="z = -1" />) and the ratio is 1. Between the two the curve passes half its decibels at the corner. <M t={String.raw`\beta`} /> is the width again, and the fixed <M t="Q" /> is the steepest slope that does not overshoot. <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="170-181">The high shelf</Src> is the same recipe with the sign in front of every <M t={String.raw`\cos w`} /> turned, which swaps the two ends.

The device's Low is that shelf at 120 Hz and its Air the high shelf at 9 kHz; <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" lines="226-231">the frequencies are fixed</Src>. Low at +6 reads <Claim id="shelf-low">+3.00 dB at 120 Hz, +5.63 dB an octave under and +0.37 dB an octave over</Claim>. Air at +6 reads <Claim id="shelf-air">+3.00 dB at 9 kHz and +5.99 dB at 20 kHz</Claim>. At +12 neither curve <Claim id="shelf-flat-top">rises above 12 dB or dips under 0</Claim>.

A cut removes one end altogether. The kit's biquad recipe for it, <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="142-147">`set_highpass`</Src>, keeps the bell's bottom half without <M t="A" /> and makes the top <M t={String.raw`\tfrac12(1+\cos w)\,(1 - 2z^{-1} + z^{-2})`} />: both zeros on the circle at 0 Hz. So 0 Hz is removed entirely and the response climbs from there at 12 dB an octave until the poles level it off. The low-pass puts both zeros at half the sample rate.

The Ambient EQ does not use those two recipes. Its cuts are the state-variable filter of lesson 5, which draws the same curves and can be retuned on any sample. <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" lines="386-400">The Low cut</Src> is two of them in a row with Qs of 0.5412 and 1.3066: one sags at the corner, one peaks, and together they are the flattest fourth-order curve there is (Butterworth), 24 dB an octave. The High cut is one, at <M t={String.raw`Q = 1/\sqrt 2`} />, 12 dB an octave. Set to 100 Hz, the Low cut is <Claim id="low-cut">3.01 dB down at 100 Hz and 24.1 dB down at 50 Hz</Claim>. The High cut is <Claim id="high-cut">3.01 dB down where it is set; an octave above, it is 12.4 dB down when set to 1 kHz and 14.3 dB down when set to 5 kHz</Claim>, because a digital low-pass has to reach silence at half the sample rate and steepens as it nears it.

That check is a second reason for the device's choice. Set to 30 Hz, the biquad pair is <Claim id="cut-forms">0.11 dB off at 20 Hz; set to 100 Hz it is 1.1 dB off there, 56 dB down</Claim>, while the state-variable pair lies on the textbook curve. A biquad's five numbers are kept to about seven digits. For a filter this low <M t="a_1" /> is nearly −2 and <M t="a_2" /> nearly 1, and where the corner lies depends on how far <M t={String.raw`1 + a_1 + a_2`} /> is from zero, which is about <M t="w^2" />, a tiny number for a filter this low: most of the seven digits are spent on the 2 and the 1. The state-variable filter keeps the small number itself.

### 5. Six filters in a row

Filters in a row multiply their responses, and decibels turn multiplying into adding. So the curve of a whole equaliser is the curves of its bands added, point by point. <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" lines="151-183">The device's chain</Src> is the Low cut, Low, Body, Presence, Air and the High cut. Body and Presence are the bell of stage 3 at 320 Hz and 3 kHz with a Q of 0.7: Body at −5 reads <Claim id="body-centre">−5.00 dB at 320 Hz and −2.38 dB an octave either side</Claim>.

### 6. Phase comes with level

Every filter above is *minimum phase*: of all the filters that could draw its level curve it is the one that answers soonest, with no delay at all. The price is that it cannot change the level of a band without shifting its timing. The shift is fixed by the level curve. Either side of a boost the phase turns: for the +6 dB bell, <Claim id="bell-phase">19.4° forward at 774 Hz and 19.4° back at 1,262 Hz, nothing at the centre, and 36.7° for +12 dB</Claim>. Far from the bell, where the level is untouched, so is the phase.

<Curve taps="bellPhase" uprights="bellHz" xLog xTitle="hertz" yTitle="degrees" min={-60} max={60}>
  How far the bell of stage 3 turns each frequency, in degrees. Below the centre of a boost the output runs ahead of the input and above it behind; a cut is the same curve upside down. More gain turns it further; more Q keeps the turning closer to the centre.
</Curve>

In time, that phase curve is a ring. The poles are a resonance: a sudden sound sets it going and it dies away at the rate their distance from the circle allows. A broad bell is over within a cycle. A narrow one is not: at a Q of 10, a +12 dB bell at 1 kHz rings on with a time constant of <Claim id="ring-boost">6.4 ms</Claim>. The cut of the same width and depth rings for <Claim id="ring-cut">1.6 ms</Claim>, because its poles are the boost's zeros, which stand further in.

### 7. Clear: bells that set themselves

After the six filters come <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" lines="311-346">23 more biquads</Src>, every one a bell that only cuts: four half an octave wide from 45 Hz to 180 Hz, then thirds of an octave up to 14.5 kHz, each with the Q that makes it one band wide (2.87 and 4.32). Nobody sets them. A Fourier transform of the last 4,096 samples (lesson 3) <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" lines="500-571">measures the power in each band</Src> and compares it with the mean of the bands two to four away on either side. Only what stands more than 6 dB above that mean is excess. The band is turned down by Clear times its excess, of which no more than 12 dB counts. The transform only listens: the sound passes through the bells and nothing else, so Clear adds no delay.

## What goes wrong

**Cramping.** Stage 3 showed that a bell is exactly 0 dB at half the sample rate. A bell whose centre is far below that never notices. One near it is squeezed against the ceiling, and its lower side narrows with it. Half an octave above its centre, a +6 dB bell with a Q of 2 holds <Claim id="cramp">2.02 dB when it stands at 1 kHz and 0.62 dB when it stands at 12 kHz; an octave above, 0.63 dB and exactly nothing</Claim>. The kit uses the plain recipe and does nothing about this. The device keeps its bells where it barely shows: Presence, the higher of the two, is <Claim id="presence-lopsided">lopsided by 0.14 dB, 2.81 dB at 1.5 kHz against 2.67 dB at 6 kHz</Claim>. A shelf has no such trouble, because its recipe reaches the whole gain exactly at half the sample rate.

<Controls ids="bellGain bellQ" />

**Rounding.** Each of a biquad's two memories is rounded to about seven digits on every sample, and the feedback treats the error like any other signal. A filter with poles close to the circle near 0 Hz, such as the Low shelf, amplifies it there. With the Low cut at 500 Hz the device measures <Claim id="rounding-floor">−70 dB at 44 Hz where its bands' own curves add to −81 dB</Claim>. Seventy decibels under the sound will not be heard. It is why the check in stage 5 looks no further down than −20 dB, and why a filter that must be very low or very deep is a job for the state-variable form.

**Ringing.** Stage 6 measured it. A narrow boost is a resonator, and on drums it adds a short note at its own frequency to every hit.

**Moving a filter.** A biquad's memories were filled under its old coefficients. Swap the coefficients in one step and the output jumps. <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" lines="14-23">The device never does</Src>: gains glide with a time constant of 5 ms and are redesigned every 16 samples, a cut coming off its stop starts from rest and is crossfaded in over 5 ms, and <Src path="cpp/devices/ambient-eq/ambient_eq.h" lines="446-476">Clear ramps each cut</Src> across the 1,024 samples between two of its measurements, designing the bell again every 64.

**A boost is gain.** Twelve decibels on a band that was already near full scale goes past it, and nothing in the device holds it back. Turning down what is too much leaves more room than turning up what is missing.

## What to try

- Choose **Noise** and look at the first picture: the red line is the curve. Move each of the six controls of stage 5 and find its band.
- Drums, and Listen on "Through one bell". Set **Q** to 10 and the gain to +12 dB, then sweep **Frequency**: the ring finds each drum in turn. Then take the gain to −12 dB.
- **Clicks**, with the same narrow boost: each click comes back as a short note at the bell's frequency.
- Chords. Take **Body** from −12 dB to +12 dB and listen to the weight of the strum come and go around 320 Hz.
- Drums, **Low cut** up to 150 Hz. Listen on "After the low cut", then on the output.
- Set all four bands to 0 dB and both cuts to the ends of their ranges. The red spectrum covers the grey: an equaliser doing nothing is a wire.
- Guitar, **Clear** at 1. Watch the two pictures of stage 7, and compare the output with "After the high cut", which is the sound before Clear's cuts.
