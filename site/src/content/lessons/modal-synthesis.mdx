<Part id="why" title="A bar rings at frequencies that no string has.">

A metal bar is struck once with a mallet and left alone. The mallet touches it for less than a thousandth of a second. The note that follows lasts for seconds, and in all that time nobody does anything to the bar. The picture above shows both, as the engine's Bells makes them: the mallet's push is the sliver at the left of its own picture, and everything in the picture under it is the bar by itself.

Nothing goes into this lesson's lab. It presses one key <Src path="site/labs/modal-synthesis.cpp" at="constexpr int kKeyDown = 480;">10 ms into the loop</Src>, holds it for the time that Key held for sets, and every sound and picture on this page is read out of the engine while that one note rings.

Listen to the note for what is in it. <Hear set="bar" label="Hear the bar" /> Listen for a knock at the start, for a clear low pitch under it, and for higher tones over that pitch which do not sound like part of it and which are gone before the low one is.

Those tones have an exact description. A steady sound is a sum of sine waves, each at one frequency, and each of them is called a partial. <Lesson id="fourier-transform" cap /> is about taking a sound apart into them. In the picture below time runs across and frequency runs up, and each level line is one partial of the bar.

So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you make a note whose partials stand at awkward ratios to one another, and die each at a speed of its own?

### The obvious answer fails

<Lesson id="plucked-string" cap /> made a string from a loop of memory one period long. Whatever is in such a loop comes past again once a period. That is its strength, and here it is the trouble. A wave that repeats exactly every period can only hold partials that fit a whole number of times into that period: the note, twice the note, three times the note. A partial at 2.757 times the note does not fit, so no loop of one length can hold it.

To hear what that costs, the lab keeps a second bar beside the real one. It has the same number of partials, struck as hard and dying as fast, with only their places changed: they are moved onto the ladder of whole multiples in order, the second to twice the note, the third to three times it.

A sound whose shape never comes round cannot be kept in a loop, and it cannot be one wave read over and over from a table either (<Lesson id="wavetables" />). It needs another kind of machine.

### What a bar does instead

A string is a path, and a wave runs along it and back. A bar is a stiff body, and it does not work that way. It can bend in several patterns, each of which it holds for as long as it is left alone: one long bow from end to end, an S with a still point in the middle, a shape with three bends, and so on. Each pattern is called a mode.

Every mode swings at a frequency of its own, set by how stiff and how heavy the bar is over the length of one bend. Short bends are much stiffer than long ones, which is why the frequencies climb faster than whole multiples do. Every mode also loses its energy at a rate of its own, the short bends soonest. And each has places along the bar that it keeps still.

Strike the bar and it moves in all of its modes at once. From then on each one swings and dies as if the others were not there, and the sound is all of them added together.

That is a recipe, and the recipe is the technique. Keep one resonator for each mode, tuned to that mode's frequency and given that mode's decay. Push all of them at once with something shaped like a mallet. Add them up. It is called modal synthesis, and the rest of this lesson is those three sentences made exact.

A resonator is nothing new. It is a filter that rings: the band-pass of <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" /> and the bell-shaped band of <Lesson id="equalising" />, with the peak made so narrow that a single click sets it ringing for seconds at the peak's own frequency.

Here is a sentence to check against that picture. Leave the three controls where the lesson starts them and read the lines half a second after the strike: <Claim id="half-second">Mode 1 stands at −25 dB and Mode 6 at −72 dB</Claim>.

Decay is the time the lowest mode takes to <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="static constexpr float kSixtyDb = 6.907755279f;">fall 60 dB</Src>, which is to a thousandth of its size. The lesson starts it at <Src path="site/labs/modal-synthesis.cpp" at='lab::Control decay{"decay", "Decay"'>3 s</Src>, and that gives the rate part 1 measured for the lowest mode. Damping sets how much sooner the upper modes die than the lowest. It is the difference between metal and wood. A steel bar loses a little energy on every swing whatever the swing's length, so its high modes outlast their welcome. Wood soaks up fast swings, and a wooden bar is a knock with a short hum after it. Measured over the same half second, <Claim id="damping-ends">Mode 2 falls at 20.7 dB a second with Damping at 0 and at 90.3 dB a second with Damping at 1, while Mode 1 falls at 20.0 dB a second at both</Claim>.

Notice what the pictures leave out. They do not say where the six frequencies come from, or what starts the modes, or why some start larger than others. They show one side of a sound that has two. Those are the pieces the rest of the lesson adds.

</Part>

<Part id="uses" title="Anything that is struck once and then rings.">

Modal synthesis suits whatever is hit, plucked or rubbed and then left to itself: bars, bells, bowls, glasses, gongs, the tines of a thumb piano. All of them are stiff bodies with a few strong modes at ratios that belong to their shape. It does not suit a string, which has dozens of modes at whole multiples and is made far more cheaply by the loop of <Lesson id="plucked-string" />, or a voice or a horn, where air is blown through for as long as the note lasts.

The engine's Bells has <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="enum Material : int { kBell = 0, kBowl, kVibraphone, kBar, kKalimba, kGlass, kGong, kNumMaterials };">seven materials</Src>, and a material is only a table: for each mode its ratio to the note, how strongly a strike starts it, and a number or two for its shape. Each card below plays one note of one material, lists the settings that make it, and shows what to look for. A card's button moves the whole page to its settings, so every figure further down shows that note too, and the play bar offers the way back to where you were.

<Part id="build" title="Start with one number that turns. Add six pieces.">

The quickest way to understand a bank of modes is to make one. This part starts with a single resonator and adds six pieces to it, one at a time. After each piece the picture is drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

Up to the sixth piece the build works everything out for itself, from the device's table of modes and its constants. The seventh takes three numbers for each mode from the device as it runs, and does the arithmetic with them in its own lines.

Some of the steps are wrong on purpose. Hearing what is missing is how the next piece earns its place.

    A mode needs to remember two things: how far along its swing it is, and how large the swing is. One number with two parts holds both. Think of it as a point on a sheet of paper, with the centre of the sheet as nothing. Its distance from the centre is the size of the swing, and its direction from the centre is the place in the swing.

The share sets the decay. The sound is how far the point stands above or below the centre, so a point going round at a steady rate draws a sine wave.

<StageBlock tap="sum" n={6} title="Two gains for each mode, and the sum">
  The modes are added twice, once for the left side and once for the right, and each mode has a gain for each. That is all the stereo there is: no mode is delayed or filtered on its way out.

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y_L[n]} = \sum_k \at{mode-left}{a_k\,v_k[n]}`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`y_R[n] = \sum_k b_k\,v_k[n]`} />

  <M t={String.raw`a_k`} /> and <M t={String.raw`b_k`} /> are the two gains of mode <M t="k" />, and <M t={String.raw`y_L`} /> and <M t={String.raw`y_R`} /> are the two sums.

  What the gains are depends on whether the mode is single or one of a pair. Round things ring in pairs, as the bowl of part 3 did, and the table marks the pairs. One half of each pair <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="float left = kit::kSqrtHalf, right = kit::kSqrtHalf;">stays in the middle</Src>, equal on both sides.   <M block t={String.raw`\at{gains-turn}{\psi = 0.8 \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{2} \cdot W}`} />

<Part id="ways" title="Six things players do with these controls.">

Each of these is a habit of playing, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up to the engine and see what the mallet, the decays and the two sides are doing.

  <Way title="Take a soft mallet" tag="Hardness at nothing" set="soft">

<Part id="wrong" title="Seven ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Struck where the note stands still" set="node">
    Every mode has places on the bar that do not move, and a mode struck there is not struck. For most modes that is useful, and it is what Position is for. For Mode 1 it takes the note itself away. <Claim id="fault-node">Struck on Mode 1's still point, Mode 1 starts 59 dB lower than at the end of the bar</Claim>. Listen for a sound that has a clang and no pitch under it: the upper modes are all there, and the one the key was named for is not. Move Position a little either way and the note comes back.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A wobble" set="wobble">
    A little Detune is the slow life of a bowl. A lot of it is two notes heard to be out of tune. <Claim id="fault-wobble">With Detune at 6 Hz the level of the note dips 0.17 s apart</Claim>, and the upper pairs, which are spread wider, beat faster still. On one note that is a flutter. On a chord every note flutters at once, at rates that have nothing to do with the music. Bring Detune down until the movement is felt more than heard.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="More than the output can hold" set="hot">
    Stage 9's clip does nothing to an ordinary note, and that makes it easy to forget. A hard mallet with Brightness up puts a tall spike at the front of the note, and Volume at its upper end doubles it. <Claim id="clipped">With Volume at +6 dB the modes add up to a peak 6.0 dB over full scale, and the output's largest sample is 0.0 dB</Claim>: the clip has bent the front of the note to fit. Listen for a knock that is duller than the mallet should make it. Once the ring has fallen under the knee it passes untouched, so the note sounds as if it had been hit with something softer. Bring Volume down, and make the part louder later in the chain.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A mode above the band">
    A mode's frequency is the note times a ratio, and the bar's highest ratio is nearly 25. On a high key that product passes the top of what the sample rate can hold, where a resonator would ring at some other, wrong frequency. The device therefore <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="if (hz >= kTopOfBand * sr || hz < 5.0f) {">leaves out any mode</Src> above <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="static constexpr float kTopOfBand = 0.45f;">0.45 of the sample rate</Src>. Nothing wrong is heard, but a high note has fewer modes than a low one and sounds plainer. That is true of small real bars as well.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The same key, struck again">
    A bar that is struck while it still rings does not start again from rest. The new push adds to what is there, and whether a mode grows or shrinks depends on where it was in its swing when the mallet arrived. The device does the same: <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="// The same key while its note still rings: strike those modes again.">it strikes the modes that are already ringing</Src>, and clears nothing. Two quick strikes of one key are therefore never quite alike, which is right for a bar and surprising to anyone who expects the same sound twice.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A thirteenth note">
    Long notes pile up. The device keeps <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="static constexpr int kMaxVoices = 12;">twelve notes</Src> going at once, and a bell with a long Decay can still be ringing many bars after its key. When a thirteenth is asked for, one of the twelve is taken. It is faded out over <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="steal_step_ = 1.0f / (0.002f * sr);">2 ms</Src> first, so there is no click, but a note that was ringing is gone. If long tails are being cut off, shorten Decay or raise Release.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Numbers too small to hold">
    A mode that shrinks for long enough reaches numbers so small that a processor handles them very slowly, and a resonator keeps multiplying them by itself. When a mode's size falls <Src path="cpp/devices/modal-bells/modal_bells.h" at="} else if (power < 1.0e-24f) {">under that floor</Src> the device sets both of its parts to nothing. Nothing is heard of it. Without it, a quiet tail would cost more to compute than a loud note.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A bell's partials are harmonics.">
    Harmonics are whole multiples of the note, and they belong to things that are stretched or blown: strings and pipes. A bar, a bell, a bowl and a plate are stiff, and their modes stand at ratios that their shape decides. Part 1 measured the bar's beside the whole multiples. The ear hears the difference at once, and it is most of what "struck metal" means.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="It plays back a recording of a bar.">
    Nothing is recorded. Each mode is two numbers, turned and shrunk on every sample, and the note is their sum. That is why the mallet, the place of the strike and the length of the ring can change from one note to the next, and why step 7 of the build could arrive at the device's own signal from a few dozen lines.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Decay is a fade on the output.">
    A fade turns every partial down together. Decay sets how long the lowest mode rings, and Damping sets how much sooner each higher mode dies, so the sound changes as it falls: bright at the strike, and one low tone at the end. The picture of part 2 shows the six lines parting. A fade would keep them level with each other.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A harder mallet only makes the note louder.">
