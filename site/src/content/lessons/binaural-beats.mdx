<Part id="why" title="Two tones a few hertz apart beat. Where they meet decides where the beat is.">

One string is tuned against another by ear. While the two are a little apart, the sound of both together swells and fades, slowly when they are close and quickly when they are not. That swelling is a beat, and it is one of the oldest measuring tools in music.

The lab on this page makes a beat with two sine tones around one held key. It starts the key at <Src path="site/labs/binaural-beats.cpp" at={'lab::Control key{"key", "Key", "Hz"'}>220 Hz</Src> and Beat, the distance between the two tones, at <Src path="site/labs/binaural-beats.cpp" at={'lab::Control beat{"beat", "Beat", "Hz"'}>6 Hz</Src>. Half of Beat goes above the key and half below. Nothing goes into this lab. It presses its own key before the loop begins and holds it, so every sound on the page is a steady one.

That picture is the whole cause of a beat. Where the two tones move together they add to twice one tone. Where they move against each other they add to nothing. They drift from the one state to the other and back, over and over, because one of them is a little faster.

### In the air, the two tones add

Play both tones from one loudspeaker, or put both into both sides of a pair of headphones, and each ear is given their sum. With Ears at 0 the lab does that. Measured on that render, <Claim id="air-beat">each ear's signal holds both tones, 223 Hz and 217 Hz, at −21.2 dB each, and its level falls more than 25 dB and comes back 6 times a second</Claim>. The two ears' signals are <Claim id="air-alike">the same, sample for sample</Claim>.

Listen to it on anything: speakers will do. <Hear set="air" label="Hear two tones in the air" />

The reason is one line of trigonometry. Write <M t="f" /> for the key, <M t="b" /> for Beat, both in hertz, and <M t="t" /> for time in seconds:

<M
  block
  t={String.raw`\begin{gathered} \sig{alt}{\sin 2\pi\bigl(f + \tfrac{b}{2}\bigr)t} + \sig{alt2}{\sin 2\pi\bigl(f - \tfrac{b}{2}\bigr)t} \\[6pt] = \sig{out}{2\cos(\pi b t)\,\sin(2\pi f t)} \end{gathered}`}
/>

Read it against the pictures. Before the equals sign are the two tones of the first picture. After it is one tone at the key, multiplied by a slow cosine, and the cosine is the outline of the second picture. The cosine turns at half of <M t="b" /> and passes through zero twice in each turn, so the outline closes <M t="b" /> times a second. This is the multiplication of <Lesson id="tremolo-and-ring-modulation" /> read backwards. There a tone times a slow wave came out as two tones, a sum and a difference. Here two tones go in, and what comes out is a tone times a slow wave.

This beat is in the sound. A microphone in the room would record it and a meter on the wire shows it. It is there for one ear as much as for two.

### One tone to each ear

Now keep the two tones apart. The higher one goes into the left side of a pair of headphones and the lower one into the right, and nothing of either goes into the other side. With Ears at 1, where this lesson starts, the lab does that. They are the same two tones as before. They no longer meet in any wire.

### What this page can measure, and what it cannot

A meter reads a signal. This page can say what each channel holds to a fraction of a decibel, and every number on it about a signal has a test behind it. It cannot say what a listener hears. No test on this site listens.

What is long established about hearing is this. Given two steady tones a few hertz apart, one to each ear, listeners report a slow beat, or a slow movement of the tone inside the head, at the rate of the difference between the tones. It was described in the nineteenth century, and it is in the textbooks of hearing under the name binaural beat. The usual account is that it comes from the part of hearing that compares the two ears' signals to tell where a sound is, which is here given two tones that slip steadily past each other. That is a statement about people, taken from the literature of hearing. This page repeats it and has not measured it.

The same literature reports the beat only within limits: for tones that are low enough in pitch, and close enough to each other. This page gives no figure for either limit, having measured neither. Where the engine's device gives one, in its notes or by what it does to a key, the lesson says that the figure is the device's.

A good deal more is said about these sounds: that listening to them does something to the listener beyond being heard. The site has measured nothing of that kind and has no instrument that could, so this lesson says nothing about it.

So the question this lesson answers is a narrow one, and it can be answered exactly. When a device says it makes a beat between the ears, what is in each channel, and how is it built so that the beat stays out of both?

</Part>

<Part id="picture" title="Three levels: the left ear, the right ear, and the two added.">

One picture holds the idea. It draws the page's meter on three signals at once: the left ear's, the right ear's, and half of their sum.

The meter is the lab's own. It squares each sample, takes the mean of the squares over <Src path="site/labs/binaural-beats.cpp" at="const int window = static_cast<int>(lab::kSampleRate / key_hz(0) + 0.5f)">one cycle of the key</Src>, takes the mean of those means over one more cycle, and reads the result in decibels. Write <M t="s[n]" /> for sample <M t="n" /> of a signal, <M t="N" /> for the number of samples in one cycle of the key, <M t="p" /> for the first mean and <M t="m" /> for the meter's reading:

<M
  block
  t={String.raw`\begin{gathered} p[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} s[n-j]^2 \\[6pt] m[n] = \at{meter-db}{10\log_{10}}\Bigl(\at{meter-read}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} p[n-j]}\Bigr) \end{gathered}`}
/>

One cycle is long enough to average the wave's own swing away, and short enough to follow a beat of a few hertz. So a level that moves in this picture is a level that moves.

What the meter cannot do is reach the bottom of a dip. Where two tones cancel, it is still averaging the cycles on either side, so it reads a deep dip and not silence. That is why this page says of such a dip that the level falls by more than so many decibels. Where it gives a figure, as for the chord of part 3, the figure is what the meter reads.

One thing that can be heard here belongs to the page's loop and not to the device. Move Beat or Key off a whole number of hertz and an ear's wave may dip where the loop's end is joined to its start. The last card of part 7 says why, and why the levels in this picture do not show it.

<Faults>
  <Fault title="The two channels get added" set="folded">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="mid"
      set="folded"
      from={1}
      span={0.5}
      caption="Half a second of the two ears added. The outline closes and opens: the beat is in this signal."

    A phone's one speaker, a bus set to mono, a reverb fed from both sides: each adds the two channels, and the sum of the two tones beats at full depth whatever Ears says, as parts 1 and 2 measured. Nothing after the sum can take it out again. If the beat is to stay out of the signal, the two channels have to stay apart all the way to the headphones. If the sound will be added anyway, choose that on purpose: Ears at 0 does it outright.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A note on the edge of the band" set="edge">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="levelShifted"
      set="edge"
      from={1}
      span={1}
      min={-70}
      max={-10}

    The Ear Shift moves a band and leaves the rest. A note on the band's edge is half in and half out, so each ear gets it both moved and unmoved, and those two beat inside the one ear. <Claim id="fault-edge">With High at 440 Hz and a tone at 440 Hz, the level of an ear falls more than 30 dB, 3 times a second, which is half of Beat. With High an octave above the tone it moves less than 1.5 dB</Claim>. Keep the notes that matter well inside the band. Low starts at <Src path="cpp/devices/ear-shift/device.json" at={'"default": 60,'}>60 Hz</Src>, as low as it goes, for that reason.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The bed reaches the guard" set="loud-bed">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="left"
      set="loud-bed"
      from={1}
      span={0.5}

    The device is scaled for its tones, and the bed is added on top. <Claim id="fault-bed">With Bed at 1 the loudest sample of the loop stands at −3.2 dB, and less than 0.2 % of the samples in either ear are over half of full scale</Claim>, where the soft clip of stage 7 begins to bend them. It is a small share. The tones alone stay under the guard, a chord of three included, so it is the bed that reaches it. Lower Bed, or lower Volume on the device.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Turns that cancel" set="cancel">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="levelPulsed levelPulsedMid"
      set="cancel"
      from={1}
      span={1}
      min={-140}
      max={-10}

    The way card that made the ears take turns has a price. Add the two ears and one is always fading in exactly as the other fades out. <Claim id="fault-turns">With Offset at 180° the two ears added hold at −24.1 dB and move by less than 0.01 dB</Claim>: on one speaker the pulse is gone, and what is left is a steady tone at half strength. Keep Offset lower, or move Duty away from a half, for a sound that may be added.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A loop that does not come round">
    This one is the page's, not the device's. The lab plays four seconds and joins the end to the start with a short crossfade. Where the two tones do not come round in that time, the crossfade passes through a moment where the end and the start disagree, and each ear's wave dips there, once in every pass of the loop. Listen for a short dip in a tone that should be steady. The meters on the ears do not show it: they read the signal before the loop is cut. A held key on the device itself has no loop and no such dip. Every card on this page is set where the tones come round. Move Beat or Key by hand and the dip may come, and a whole number of hertz on both takes it away again.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what the signals show.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="The beat is in the recording.">
    With one tone to each ear, neither channel beats. The meter on each ear holds still to a hundredth of a decibel, as the answer in part 1 shows, and each channel holds one tone. The beat is in the recording only once the two channels are added.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="There is a low tone at the rate of the beat in there.">
    No signal on this page holds a tone at the rate of the beat. <Claim id="no-six">At 6 Hz, and at 3 Hz, the lab finds under −120 dB in the left ear, in the right ear and in the two added</Claim>. Even the sum, which beats, is two tones near the key and nothing else: <Claim id="sum-rest">with those two taken away, what is left of it is more than 130 dB under it</Claim>. A beat is a level that moves, not a low tone.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="It works the same on speakers.">
    On speakers both tones reach both ears, so each ear is given the sum, and the sum beats in the air. That is the first case of part 1 and step two of the build. It is a beat, and a different one: it is in each ear's signal, where the other is in neither.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A pulsed tone at the same rate is the same thing.">
    A pulse is one tone whose level is switched, and a meter on one ear shows all of it, as step six of the build measured. A pair of tones, one to an ear, moves no meter. The two are built differently and their signals are different. Which of them a listener prefers, the page cannot say.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Any two pitches will do.">
    The engine's device does not treat them alike. It folds every key into a range of its own, and it stops splitting a partial above the top of that range, as stages 1 and 2 show. Its notes give hearing as the reason. This page has measured the folding and the splitting, and has not measured the hearing.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="It is known what these sounds do to the person who listens.">
    Not from anything on this site. What the site can show is what each channel holds: two steady tones, a few hertz apart, kept apart. That a listener given them reports a beat is long established in the study of hearing, and part 1 repeats it without having measured it. What listening does to a listener beyond that is a question for people who measure listeners. No test here does, and this lesson says nothing about it.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
