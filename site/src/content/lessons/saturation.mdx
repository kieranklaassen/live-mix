<Part id="why" title="A played note starts tall and dies away. A curve changes that, and a computer can get the curve wrong.">

A guitar line is picked one note at a time. Each note is at its tallest the moment the pick lets go of the string. From there it rings and dies away, and the next note arrives before it has gone.

<Still
  taps="in"
  set="played"
  caption="Two seconds of a picked guitar line, eight notes. Each starts at its full height and has fallen to a fraction of that by the time the next one starts."
/>

Listen to it first. <Hear set="played" label="Hear the line as played" />

Two numbers describe that shape. One is the loudest sample, the top of the tallest note. The other is the level of the whole loop: the root of the mean of its squared samples, in decibels below full scale, the largest number a sample can be. <Lesson id="level-and-loudness" /> is about both. On this line the first stands a long way over the second. The starts of the notes set how far the line can be turned up before something clips. Most of each note is the ring, and the ring sits far below.

How do you bring the body of each note up without its start going through the ceiling, and without waiting for anything to react? That is the question this lesson starts from.

### Bend it

A compressor, the subject of <Lesson id="compression" />, measures how loud a sound is and turns it down for a while. Measuring takes time, and the start of a note has passed before the gain has moved. A saturator does not measure. It has a fixed curve, and it puts every sample over that curve by itself: a small sample comes out as it went in, and a large one comes out less large than it went in. There is no attack and no release, because nothing is remembered from one sample to the next.

The first saturators were not designed. A valve, a transformer and magnetic tape each pass a small signal as it is and give way gradually as the signal grows. Engineers found that a part recorded a little too hot came back sounding fuller to them, and began to do it on purpose.

Here is the guitar line through the engine's saturator, at the settings this page opens on: gain before the curve, the curve, and a smaller gain after it.

### The bend adds something

Holding the tops in is half of what the curve does. The other half is the reason people reach for it by ear. A wave that has been bent is a wave of another shape, and a wave of another shape has other frequencies in it.

The plainest sound there is shows it. The page's sine is one frequency, <Src path="site/labs/bench.cpp" at="const float sine_step = 440.0f / lab::kSampleRate;">440 Hz</Src>, and nothing else.

<Still
  taps="in out"
  source="sine"
  view="spectrum"
  caption="How much of each frequency there is in the sine going in, and in what comes out of the curve. Going in there is one peak. Coming out there is a row of them, each further right and lower than the last."
/>

The new peaks are harmonics: sines at whole multiples of the first one's frequency. Measured on the output, <Claim id="sine-harmonics">the third harmonic stands 15.5 dB under the fundamental, the fifth 28 dB under and the seventh 41 dB under</Claim>, and the even ones are missing. Part 5 says why. A note with harmonics added is the same note with a different tone, and how much of that is wanted is for an ear to judge.

### The obvious way to compute it fails

In a computer the curve is one line of code: multiply the sample by the drive and look the result up on the curve. It looks finished, and for a low note it nearly is.

The trouble is the subject of <Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" />. A run of samples can hold no frequency above half its sample rate. This page runs at <Src path="site/labs/lab.h" at="constexpr float kSampleRate = 48000.0f;">48 kHz</Src>, so its limit is 24 kHz. The curve knows nothing of that. Its harmonics go on upwards, each a little weaker than the last, and a high note has harmonics above the limit almost at once.

### The fix is room

The cure is to run the same curve at a higher sample rate, where the limit is further up, and to filter the harmonics that have nowhere to go before coming back down. The engine's saturator does that at up to four times the rate, and this page starts it there.

So a saturator in a computer is two things. One is a curve, which takes a line of code. The other is a way of running a curve on samples without the folding, which takes most of the device. This lesson starts the engine's saturator driven harder than it starts by itself, so that the bend is easy to see, and part 9 puts the two beside each other.

<Part id="picture" title="Sample in along the bottom, sample out up the side.">

One picture holds what a curve does. Pick a sample on the bottom edge, go straight up to the line, then across to the left edge: that is the sample that comes out. The diagonal is a wire, where every sample comes out as it went in. A saturator's line runs straight through zero, steeper than the diagonal by as much as Drive adds, and then bends over towards a ceiling.

Check this against the picture. Leave the three controls where the lesson starts them and find 0.25 on the bottom edge, a quarter of full scale. Go up to the line and across. <Claim id="curve-reads">A sample of 0.25 comes out at 0.96, and one of 0.125 comes out at 0.76</Claim>. The first went in twice the size of the second and comes out about a quarter larger. That is the holding in: the larger a sample is, the less of its size gets through.

Each of the three controls moves the sound against the line, or changes the line.

- **Drive** is gain before the curve. The curve stays as it is. Drive decides how far along it the sound reaches, and from outside that looks like a steeper line with its bend closer to zero.
- **Curve** picks one of five shapes. Soft bends from the first and never quite arrives. Hard is straight and then flat. Tube bends its lower half sooner than its upper. Tape is the slowest to arrive. Fold turns round at the top and comes back.
- **Bias** slides the sound sideways along the curve before it is bent, so that the upper and the lower half of a wave meet different parts of the bend.

The compressor's picture in <Lesson id="compression" /> looks like this one, and means something else. Its edges are levels: how loud a stretch of sound is. A compressor turns the whole wave down by one gain and keeps its shape. This picture's edges are single samples. A curve never sees a stretch of sound. It changes the shape of the wave itself, which is where the harmonics of part 1 come from.

Notice what the picture leaves out. It treats a sound as a list of separate numbers, and a sound is a wave that passes through them. Everything the picture shows is true at each sample. What the bend does to the wave between its samples is not in it, and that is where the folding comes from.

<StageBlock tap="shaped1x" n={2} title="The curve, and Bias">
  This is the stage that makes the sound. Each sample is looked up on a curve, and what the curve says is put out in its place. The device has five.

  <Curve
    taps="soft:out hard:ctl tube:alt tape:alt2 fold:alt3"
    diagonal
    xTitle="sample in, after Drive"
    yTitle="sample out"
    min={-1.2}
    max={1.2}
    height={320}
  >
    The five curves as the device works them out, over four times full scale either way. Notice
    that all five leave zero along the diagonal, and that none goes above 1 or below −1. Over the
    upper half Tube lies on Soft, where the two are one curve.
  </Curve>

  Those two things are what every curve here has in common. Near zero each one is a wire, so a quiet sound at low Drive passes as it is. Measured on a sine 30 dB under full scale with Drive at 6 dB, what the Soft curve changed lies <Claim id="low-drive">59.6 dB under the sine</Claim>. And each one is bounded, so however hard it is driven the curve itself puts nothing out over full scale.

  Between those two ends the five differ, and the difference is the character of each. In each equation <M t="v" /> is the sample as the curve receives it and <M t="f(v)" /> is what the curve gives back.

  **Soft** is the hyperbolic tangent. It bends from the first, gently, and keeps bending.

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{curves-soft}{f(v)} = \tanh v`} />

  **Hard** is a wire up to full scale and flat beyond it. Under the corner it does nothing at all. Over it, it cuts the top off.

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{curves-hard}{f(v)} = \max(-1, \min(1, v))`} />

  **Tube** is Soft on the upper half of the wave. On the lower half it is the same shape, squeezed: it bends sooner and stops lower. <M t="c" /> is <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/Waveshapers.h" at="constexpr float kTubeNegativeKnee = 1.5f;">one and a half</Src>.

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\at{curves-tube}{f(v)} = \begin{cases} \tanh v & v \ge 0 \\ \tanh(c v)/c & v < 0 \end{cases}`}
  />

  **Tape** leaves the diagonal a little sooner than Soft and takes far longer to reach the ceiling, and behind it sits a high cut.

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{curves-tape}{f(v)} = \frac{v}{\sqrt{1 + v^2}}`} />

  **Fold** is a wire up to full scale, and then it turns round and comes back down. A louder sample comes out smaller.

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{curves-fold}{f(v)} = 4\,\bigl|\,t - \operatorname{round}(t)\,\bigr| - 1, \qquad t = \tfrac{v + 1}{4}`} />

  <Listing
    path="cpp/devices/saturator/Waveshapers.h"
    from="struct Soft"
    to="return 4.0f * std::abs (t - std::floor (t + 0.5f)) - 1.0f;"
    name="curves"
    at={{
      soft: 'static float shape (float x) noexcept { return tanhExp (x); }',
      hard: 'return x < -1.0f ? -1.0f : (x > 1.0f ? 1.0f : x);',
      tube: 'return x >= 0.0f ? tanhExp (x) : tanhExp (x * kTubeNegativeKnee) / kTubeNegativeKnee;',
      tape: 'return x / std::sqrt (1.0f + x * x);',
      fold: 'return 4.0f * std::abs (t - std::floor (t + 0.5f)) - 1.0f;',
      softarea: 'return a + std::log1p (std::exp (-2.0 * a)) - 0.6931471805599453;',
      hardarea: 'return a <= 1.0 ? 0.5 * x * x : a - 0.5;',
    }}
  />

  Two of the five carry a second function, `antiderivative`. Leave it for stage 4.

  **Why a bend makes harmonics.** Take Soft. For a small sample the tangent can be written out as a sum:

  <M block t={String.raw`\tanh u = u - \frac{u^3}{3} + \frac{2u^5}{15} - \cdots`} />

  The first term is the wire. The second is a cube, and the cube of a sine is not a sine at one frequency:

  <M block t={String.raw`\sin^3\theta = \tfrac{3}{4}\sin\theta - \tfrac{1}{4}\sin 3\theta`} />

  There is the third harmonic: a sine at three times the frequency, made by nothing but multiplying a sine by itself twice. The fifth power makes a fifth harmonic in the same way, and so on up. With the peaks of a sine reaching one half of full scale, the cube alone puts the third harmonic at one forty-eighth of the fundamental. Measured, it stands <Claim id="third-small">34 dB under the fundamental</Claim>, which is that fraction. Drive harder and the higher powers count for more: the harmonics rise faster than the sound does, so a saturator does different things to a loud sound and a soft one.

  Every power in that sum is odd, and so is every harmonic. That is true of any curve that treats the two halves of a wave alike. On Soft, Hard, Tape and Fold a 1 kHz tone has <Claim id="odd-only">every even harmonic more than 120 dB under the fundamental</Claim>. Tube does not treat the halves alike, and its second harmonic stands <Claim id="tube-second">22.5 dB under the fundamental</Claim>.

  **Bias** gives any of the curves that unevenness. It is a number added to the sample before the curve:

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y} = \at{shaped-curve}{f}(\at{shaped-lean}{\sig{alt}{u} + b}) - f(b)`} />

  <M t="u" /> is the driven sample of stage 1, <M t="b" /> is Bias and <M t="f" /> is the curve chosen.

  The second term is what the curve gives for silence. A sample of zero arrives at the curve as <M t="b" />, and <M t="f(b)" /> comes out, so that much is taken off again. With it gone, <Claim id="silence">silence in is exact silence out</Claim>, on every curve, whatever the bias. That matters to more than tidiness: a device that puts out a constant cannot go to sleep, and one that woke with a constant on its output would thump.

<StageBlock tap="shaped" n={3} title="Up, through the curve, and down again">
  Stage 2 is a whole saturator on paper. In a computer it has the fault of part 1: what the curve asks for above half the rate comes back below it, where it was never played. <Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" /> says why. All that is needed here is where a partial lands:

  <M block t={String.raw`f_{\text{heard}} = \left|\, f_{\text{asked}} - f_r \operatorname{round}\!\left( \frac{f_{\text{asked}}}{f_r} \right) \right|`} />

  <M t={String.raw`f_{\text{asked}}`} /> is the frequency asked for and <M t="f_r" /> is the rate the curve runs at. Take the nearest whole multiple of the rate away and drop the sign. A harmonic is a multiple of the note, and what this gives back is not, in general, a multiple of anything the note has. So what folds is not more top on the same note. It is other tones.

<StageBlock tap="foldedSet" n={4} title="The mean of the curve in place of the curve">
  More room helps a smooth curve a great deal and a curve with a corner much less. On Hard, 4x gains <Claim id="hard-4x">30 dB</Claim> where on Soft it gained 88.4: the corner's harmonics die away slowly, and plenty are left at any rate. For those the device has a second cure, of a different kind. ADAA stands for antiderivative anti-aliasing, and the long name says how it works.

  Between two samples, the sound went from one value to the other. The plain curve looks at the two ends and never at the way between. ADAA supposes the sound ran in a straight line from one to the other, and puts out the mean of the curve along that line.

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{mean-mean}{\sig{out}{y[n]}} = \frac{F(v[n]) - F(v[n-1])}{v[n] - v[n-1]}`} />

  <M t="v[n]" /> is this sample as the curve receives it and <M t="v[n-1]" /> the one before. <M t={String.raw`\at{mean-area}{F}`} /> is the antiderivative of the curve: the function whose slope at each point is the curve's value there. The difference of <M t="F" /> between two points is the area under the curve between them, and that area over the distance between them is the mean height. One subtraction and one division, and no sum to work out.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/devices/saturator/Adaa.h"
    from="float process (float x) noexcept"
    to="return (float) y;"
    name="mean"
    at={{
      area: 'const auto F0 = Shaper::antiderivative (xd);',
      mean: 'const auto y = std::abs (delta) > kMinDelta ? (F0 - F1) / delta',
    }}
  />

  <M t="F" /> has to be known as a formula. The device has it written down for two curves, the second function in each of their listings in stage 2:

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{curves-softarea}{F_{\text{soft}}(v)} = \ln \cosh v`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{curves-hardarea}{F_{\text{hard}}(v)} = \begin{cases} v^2 / 2 & |v| \le 1 \\ |v| - \tfrac{1}{2} & |v| > 1 \end{cases}`} />

  When two samples are nearly the same, the fraction is one tiny number over another and cannot be trusted. Closer than <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/Adaa.h" at="static constexpr double kMinDelta = 1.0e-4;">one ten-thousandth</Src>, the device takes the curve's value half way between the two, which is what the mean tends to.

  The mean smooths the bent wave before it is sampled again, which is the only place a smoothing can stop folding. It takes most from what changes fastest: the sharp corner.

<StageBlock tap="offset" n={5} title="The DC block, under the ceiling">
  A wave bent more on one side than the other does not lie about zero any more. The 1 kHz tone through Tube comes out lifted by <Claim id="tube-offset">0.097</Claim>, nearly a tenth of full scale, for as long as it sounds. An offset is a frequency of zero, which no loudspeaker can play, and the room it takes on one side is spent for nothing.

  <Controls ids="curve bias dcBlock" />

  <Trace taps="offset" min={-0.25} max={0.25}>
    Where the middle of the curve's output lies, over the loop. Try Curve on Tube, or Bias away from
    0, and notice the line move away from zero wherever the sound is loud. Then DC Block on, and
    notice it come back.
  </Trace>

  The cure is a filter that follows the mean of the sound very slowly, and takes that mean off.

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{ctl}{m[n]} = m[n-1] + \at{dc-step}{a}\,\operatorname{clip}\bigl(\at{dc-held}{y[n] - m[n-1]}\bigr)`} />

  <M t="y" /> is what the curve put out and <M t="m" /> is the mean being followed. On each sample <M t="m" /> closes a small share <M t="a" /> of the gap between itself and the sound. It is the one-pole low-pass again, set by its corner:

  <M block t={String.raw`a = 1 - e^{-2\pi f_c / f_r}`} />

  The corner <M t="f_c" /> is <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/Waveshapers.h" at="void prepare (double sampleRate, float cutoffHz = 10.0f) noexcept">10 Hz</Src>, under anything that is music, and <M t="f_r" /> is the rate the curve runs at. What leaves the stage is <M t="y - m" />, held inside full scale.

  The word clip in the middle is the part that is the device's own. A plain blocker follows the mean of what comes in. This one follows the mean of what leaves, after the ceiling. The reason is low notes driven flat. A plain blocker lets a long flat top sag towards zero, so the next edge starts from further off and lands past the ceiling the curve had just given it. Following what leaves, the blocker and the ceiling agree on where zero is, and the flat top stays where it was put. While the sound stays inside full scale the two blockers are the same.

  The block sits inside the higher rate, straight after the curve, so that the edge the ceiling makes is filtered on the way down like everything else the curve made.

  With DC Block on, the offset of the tone through Tube is <Claim id="dc-blocked">less than one millionth</Claim>. With it off, the stage is the ceiling alone, moved by whatever the bias gave the curve for silence.

<StageBlock tap="toned" n={6} title="Tone: a tilt about the middle">
  The curve adds harmonics above the notes, and how many is set by Drive. Tone sets how bright the result is, apart from how bent it is. It is a tilt: one straight slope across the whole band, turning about <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/saturator.h" at="static constexpr float kTonePivotHz = 1000.0f;">1 kHz</Src>.

  <Controls ids="toneDb" />

  <Spectrum taps="toneResponse:alt" min={-14} max={14}>
    What Tone does to each frequency. Try Tone either side of 0, and notice the line turn about one
    point in the middle, which stays at 0 dB.
  </Spectrum>

  <M slot="equation" block t={String.raw`H(s) = \at{tilt-k}{k}\,\frac{s + \omega_0 / k}{s + \omega_0 k}`} />

  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t={String.raw`\omega_0`} /> is the turning point and <M t="k" /> is Tone as a number to multiply by, <M t={String.raw`10^{T/20}`} />. Far below the turning point <M t="s" /> is small beside the other terms, and what is left is one over <M t="k" />: the lows come down by Tone. Far above it <M t="s" /> is all that counts, and what is left is <M t="k" />: the highs go up by Tone. At the turning point the top and the bottom have the same size and the gain is 1.

    So Tone at 6 dB is 6 dB down at one end and 6 dB up at the other, with a gentle slope between. With Tone at 6 dB, <Claim id="tone-tilt">a tone at 100 Hz comes out 5.84 dB lower, one at 10 kHz 5.9 dB higher and one at 1 kHz 0.0 dB from where it was</Claim>.

    It is one pole and one zero, the smallest filter that can do it. The listing turns the formula into the three numbers that filter needs.

  </Fragment>

<StageBlock tap="out" n={7} title="Output and Mix">
  Two things are left. Output is a gain after everything, to set the level the bent sound leaves at. Mix blends the bent sound with the sound as it went in.

  Nothing follows Output. The curve stops at full scale, and Output can put its flat tops many times over that. This page plays every stage <Src path="site/src/lab/player.ts" at="export const volumeGain = (volume: number) => 0.5 * volume * volume">at half its height with the volume all the way up</Src>, so a sound that Output takes past twice full scale is cut off on its way to your ears, and the pictures do not show it.

  <Lanes taps="in toned out">
    The sound going in, the bent sound after Tone, and what comes out. Try Mix at 0, and notice the
    last lane become the first.
  </Lanes>

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{\text{out}} = \sig{in}{d} + \at{blend-mix}{w}\,(\at{blend-trim}{g_o}\,\sig{alt}{z} - \sig{in}{d})`}
  />

  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t="z" /> is the sound after Tone and <M t="g_o" /> is Output as a number to multiply by. <M t="d" /> is the sound as it went in, 39 samples ago, so that it lines up with the bent copy. <M t="w" /> is Mix, from 0 to 1.

    At 1 the <M t="d" /> terms cancel and what comes out is all bent. At 0 the bracket is multiplied by nothing, and what comes out is <Claim id="mix-zero">the input, sample for sample</Claim>, 39 samples late.

    In between it is a straight blend: so much of one, the rest of the other. The two copies are the same sound at the same moment, so they add as amplitudes, and a blend that kept the power level would be too loud in the middle.

    Mix is not a control of how much distortion there is, though it is easy to take it for one. The harmonics exist only in the bent copy, so halving Mix halves them. The fundamental is in both copies, and falls far less. With Mix at one half and the other controls where the page starts them, the third harmonic of a 1 kHz tone comes out <Claim id="mix-half">6.0 dB lower and the fundamental 1.8 dB lower</Claim>. The shape of the distortion is set by Drive. Mix sets how far under the clean sound it sits.

  </Fragment>

Those are the seven stages. Around them the device has what the build of part 4 left out. Drive, Bias, Tone, Output and Mix glide to a new value and do not jump. Curve, Oversampling and ADAA cannot glide, so a second copy of the whole shaper is started on the new setting, given <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/saturator.h" at="static constexpr float kWarmUpSeconds = 0.002f;">2 ms</Src> to fill its filters, and faded in over <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/saturator.h" at="static constexpr float kFadeSeconds = 0.010f;">10 ms</Src>: that is what the middle lines of the last listing are doing. And after <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/saturator.h" at="idle_.reset(sr, 0.05f);">a twentieth of a second</Src> of silence the device stops working until sound comes back.

<Part id="ways" title="Five things people do with these controls.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the curve and the spectrum are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Match the level before judging" tag="Output, until both are as loud" set="matched">
    A driven sound is a louder sound, and louder nearly always seems better. So take the loudness out of the question first. Turn Output down until the bent sound is as loud as the one that went in, and then decide. <Claim id="way-matched">With Output at −15.6 dB the guitar comes out less than 0.1 dB from the level it went in at, its loudest sample 8.6 dB lower</Claim>. What is left to hear is the change of shape and the new harmonics, and nothing else. After the card, choose Input under Hearing in the play bar, and compare.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Blend it under the sound" tag="heavy settings, Mix near a third" set="under">
    Bend a copy hard and put it under the sound as it was. The hits keep their fronts, which are mostly the untouched copy, and the bent copy fills in between them. <Claim id="way-under">On the drums at a mix of 0.3 the loudest sample comes out more than 1.2 dB lower, standing 16.0 dB over the level where it stood 19.4 dB over it. The bent copy alone stands 9.4 dB over its level</Claim>, which is drums with their hits pressed nearly flat. After the card, raise Mix slowly and listen for the hits going flat.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Lean the curve for a second harmonic" tag="Bias, with DC Block on" set="lean">
    A second harmonic is the note an octave up, where a third is another note, an octave and a fifth up. Any curve gives a second when it is leaned. <Claim id="way-lean">With Bias at 0.3 the page's sine has a second harmonic 20.0 dB under the fundamental, where at 0 it has none</Claim>. Bias goes with DC Block on, as stage 5 shows: a leaned curve lifts the wave, and the block sets it down again.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Drive hard, then darken" tag="Tone below 0" set="tilted">
    A corner makes harmonics all the way up, and on a guitar or a voice the top ones can be more than the sound needs. Tone comes after the curve, so it shapes what the curve made without changing what the curve was given. Drive for the density wanted, and then bring Tone down until the edge sits back. After the card, move Tone up to 0 and hear what it had been holding down. Stage 6 has the tilt.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Clip without the folding" tag="4x and ADAA together" set="clean">
    The hardest curve needs both cures. On the rising sine this is Hard at 4x with ADAA on, and the tone climbs with nothing sliding down against it. After the card, set Oversampling to 1x and ADAA to Off, and the same sweep is the first fault of the next part. Stage 4 has the numbers for each cure alone and for both.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Six ways it goes wrong, four of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Folding" set="alias">
    The fault of part 1, at its worst: a curve with a corner, run once for each sample. As the sine rises, other tones slide down through it, and they are no part of any note. On the 5 kHz probe through Hard at 1x, what folds stands at <Claim id="fault-alias">−18 dB</Claim>. Set Oversampling to 4x, and on Soft or Hard turn ADAA on as well. A low note with little top has little to fold. A bright sound driven hard has a great deal: listen for it there first.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="More drive than the cure reaches" set="hardest">
    Oversampling makes room for harmonics, and more Drive makes more of them, higher up. At some point they outrun the room. <Claim id="hardest-4x">With Drive at 36 dB on the Soft curve, 4x gains only 16 dB</Claim> on the probe tone, where at the page's starting Drive it gained 88.4. Listen near the top of the sweep. With ADAA on as well the gain is <Claim id="hardest-both">40 dB</Claim>. After that the cure is less Drive, or a sound with less top going in.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Notes that nobody played" set="chord">
    One note through a curve gains harmonics, which belong to it. Two notes gain more than that: tones at sums and differences of the two, which belong to neither. With Drive where the page starts it, <Claim id="imd">two tones 100 Hz apart, at 1,000 Hz and 1,100 Hz, each an eighth of full scale, come out of Soft with tones at 900 Hz and 1,200 Hz as well, 17.5 dB under each. Out of Tube there is one at 100 Hz too, 21 dB under</Claim>. A chord makes many such tones, and more Drive makes them stronger. Listen for the chord thickening, and then for the notes becoming hard to tell apart. No setting of Oversampling helps, because nothing has folded: this is what a curve does. Use less Drive on chords, or bend the parts one at a time before they are mixed.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Over the ceiling" set="over">
    Every curve stops at full scale, and the output can still go over it. A wave with a flat top needs all its harmonics to stay flat. The filter on the way down takes the ones above the band, and what is left has ripples that stand above the old top. <Claim id="overshoot">On the saw through Hard at 4x the loudest sample stands 1.1 dB over the ceiling. At 1x it stands at exactly 1</Claim>, with all the folding that goes with it. <Claim id="overshoot-drums">On the drums with Drive at 36 dB it is more than 2.7 dB over</Claim>. The device leaves it, because clipping it again at the base rate would put the folding back. Leave that much room with Output, or follow with the limiter of <Lesson id="limiting" />.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="What lands on the slope">
    The first filter is flat to the top of hearing and shut a little above half the rate. Between the two is its slope, and a harmonic that falls there gets through in part. <Claim id="slope">At 4x the fifth harmonic of the 5 kHz tone still comes back at 23 kHz, 30 dB under full scale where 1x has it 17 dB under</Claim>. Nobody hears 23 kHz. It matters when something after the saturator bends the sound again, or when the file is later played at another rate.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The cure dulls the top">
    ADAA at 1x takes the folding down a little and the top of the sound down a lot: stage 4 has both numbers. Used that way it is a dull saturator that still folds. Use ADAA with 2x or 4x, where the loss is a small part of that: the picture of stage 4 draws all three.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="Oversampling gets rid of aliasing.">
    It turns it down, by an amount that depends on the curve and on Drive. Stage 3 shows 4x taking the Soft curve's folding down by 88.4 dB, and then shows the harmonic that still comes back. Stage 4 shows the same setting doing much less for a curve with a corner, and the second fault of part 7 shows it running out as Drive rises.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A soft curve is smooth, so it does not alias.">
    Smooth curves make harmonics that die away sooner, and they still have no last one. The number of stage 3 that started all this, the folding at 1x, was measured on Soft. Only a curve that is a wire makes nothing new.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Driving it sounds better.">
    Driving it is louder, and louder tends to win a quick comparison. Part 1 has the level the guitar gained without its loudest sample rising, and the first card of part 6 takes that gain away so that the two can be compared fairly. Sometimes the bent sound still wins. Often it was the level.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Even harmonics need a valve.">
    Even harmonics need a curve that treats the two halves of a wave differently, and nothing more. Stage 2 shows Tube doing it by its shape, and the third card of part 6 gives one to the Soft curve with Bias alone.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="It clips, so nothing can come out over full scale.">
    The curve cannot go over. The filter after it can, as the fourth fault of part 7 shows, and Output can put the whole sound wherever it is set. A saturator is not a limiter.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Mix turns the distortion down gently.">
    Mix leaves the distortion exactly as harsh as Drive made it and puts it lower under the clean sound. Stage 7 has the numbers: the harmonics fall by the blend and the fundamental by far less. For a gentler bend, use less Drive.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
