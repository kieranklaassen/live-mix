An echo is a sound kept for a while and played again. To keep it a program needs memory: a row of the most recent samples, long enough to reach back as far as the echo is late. That row is a _delay line_. Every sample period it gives back the sample it was handed some time ago, and takes a new one in.

By itself that makes one repeat. Send some of what comes out back in, and the repeat is repeated, a little quieter each time: the trail of echoes everybody knows. The rest of this lesson is about what shares the loop with the line. A filter makes each repeat duller than the last. A soft ceiling decides what happens when the repeats are asked to grow. And a delay time that will not hold still bends the pitch of everything on its way round.

The engine's Tape Echo is written as a machine: a loop of tape, one head that records, heads further along that play. In the code the tape is a delay line, recording is a write and playing is a read, and this lesson uses both sets of words. These are the stages of its loop, in the order the code runs them for each sample. Press Play, then Listen on any stage.

The lab starts with the transport steady, the tape straight and the filters as open as they go; each comes back as a control in its own stage below.

## Stage by stage

### 1. The line: a circular buffer

The line has to hold the last few seconds of sound and hand back any one of those samples at once. The obvious way is a row in which every sample moves along one place each sample period, which for this device would be 262,144 moves for every sample that arrives. A _circular buffer_ leaves the samples where they are and moves the place it writes to:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{write}(x):\; b[w] \leftarrow x,\;\; w \leftarrow (w+1) \mathbin{\&} (N-1) \qquad\qquad \text{read}(d) = b[(w-d) \mathbin{\&} (N-1)]`} />

Here <M t="b" /> is the buffer of <M t="N" /> samples, <M t="w" /> the write index and <M t="d" /> the delay in samples. The read index is simply <M t="d" /> places behind the write index. <M t="N" /> is a power of two, so wrapping is one AND with <M t="N-1" />: when <M t="w" /> steps off the end it lands on slot 0, and when <M t="w-d" /> goes below zero the same AND brings it round to the far end. That is all of <Src path="cpp/kit/delay.h" lines="17-37">the kit's `DelayLine`</Src>, which refuses any other size when it is compiled.

Every block in the kit reads first and writes second, `y = line.read(d); line.write(x);`, so `read(d)` is the sample from <M t="d" /> periods ago and the shortest delay is one sample. The Tape Echo keeps <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" lines="148-149">262,144 samples a side</Src>, 5.46 seconds at 48 kHz, of which Time uses at most two.

### 2. Reading between samples

250 ms happens to be a whole number of samples. 100.01 ms is 4,800.48 of them, and once the delay starts to move (stage 6) the read point is almost never on a stored sample. So a read has to make up a value between two samples it does have.

The cheap answer is a straight line between the two neighbours. With <M t="a" /> the sample at the whole part of the delay, <M t="b" /> the one before it and <M t="f" /> the fraction left over:

<M block t={String.raw`y = a + (b - a)\,f`} />

The better answer looks at four samples, two on each side, and draws a cubic through the middle two whose slope at each is set by the samples either side of it. The kit calls it <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" lines="68-74">`hermite`</Src>:

<M block t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} c_1 &= \tfrac12\,(y_1 - y_{-1}) \\ c_2 &= y_{-1} - \tfrac52\,y_0 + 2\,y_1 - \tfrac12\,y_2 \\ c_3 &= \tfrac12\,(y_2 - y_{-1}) + \tfrac32\,(y_0 - y_1) \end{aligned} \qquad\qquad y = \bigl((c_3 f + c_2)\,f + c_1\bigr)\,f + y_0`} />

The read lands between <M t="y_0" /> and <M t="y_1" />; <M t="c_1" /> is the slope at <M t="y_0" />, and the other two bend the curve so that it arrives at <M t="y_1" /> with the slope the samples around <M t="y_1" /> ask for. Both reads are a few lines in <Src path="cpp/kit/delay.h" lines="39-55">`read_linear` and `read_hermite`</Src>. The clearest way to see what each does is to store one sample and read all around it:

<Controls ids="fraction" />

<Curve taps="kernelLinear:alt kernelHermite:out" uprights="fraction" xTitle="samples past the stored one" min={-0.2} max={1.4}>
  One stored sample of height 1, read by the kit's line at every place from three samples before it to three after. Green is the linear read: a triangle, so a sample counts only towards reads less than one step away. Red is the Hermite read: it reaches two steps each way and dips below zero, taking a little off to keep the curve round. The dashed line is the fraction above. The height of a curve there, and at whole steps either side of there, is what the read multiplies its samples by.
</Curve>

Half way between two samples, the linear read is a half of each neighbour. The Hermite read is <M t={String.raw`-\tfrac1{16},\ \tfrac9{16},\ \tfrac9{16},\ -\tfrac1{16}`} /> of the four around it, and the Tape Echo, which reads with Hermite, turns one sample into <Claim id="device-hermite">those four numbers</Claim> when Time ends in half a sample.

Either way a sample has become a weighted sum of its neighbours, and a weighted sum of neighbours is a filter. Averaging smooths, so the filter is a low-pass, and how much it takes depends on the fraction:

<Spectrum taps="responseLinear:alt responseHermite:out" min={-12} max={3}>
  What each read does to each frequency as a delay of four samples and the fraction above: the lab puts one sample through the kit's line and takes the response. At 0 and at 1 the read lands on a stored sample and both lines lie flat on 0 dB. Half way is the worst there is.
</Spectrum>

At half a sample the linear read is the mean of two neighbours, whose response is <M t={String.raw`\cos(\pi f / f_s)`} />: it loses <Claim id="linear-half">2.0 dB at 10 kHz and 11.7 dB at 20 kHz</Claim>. The Hermite read loses <Claim id="hermite-half">0.53 dB at 10 kHz and 8.4 dB at 20 kHz</Claim>. Neither is flat, which is why a moving delay is never quite transparent, but the cubic loses a quarter as much at 10 kHz for the price of two more samples and a few multiplies.

### 3. Feedback: a geometric series

One read gives one echo. To get a trail, the device adds what the head reads back into what it records. With <M t="D" /> the delay in samples and <M t="g" /> the feedback gain:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{tape}[n] = x[n] + g\,\text{tape}[n-D] \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y[n] = \text{tape}[n-D] = \sum_{k \ge 1} g^{\,k-1}\, x[n - kD]`} />

Follow one sample round. It is read after <M t="D" /> samples at full size, written again at <M t="g" /> times that, read again at <M t="2D" />, written at <M t="g^2" />. Each repeat is the last one times <M t="g" />, which in decibels is the same step down every time:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{step} = 20\log_{10} g \ \text{dB a repeat} \qquad\qquad T_{60} = D\,\frac{60}{-20\log_{10} g} = \frac{3D}{\log_{10}(1/g)}`} />

<M t={String.raw`T_{60}`} /> is the time the trail takes to fall 60 dB, the usual measure of a decay. At a gain of 0.5 the step is 6.02 dB, so 60 dB takes 9.97 repeats, 2.49 seconds at 250 ms. At 0.9 the step is 0.92 dB and the same fall takes 66 repeats. The time grows without limit as <M t="g" /> nears 1, where the logarithm is zero. In the code the sum is one line, <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" lines="122-133">`record_left = in[0] + feedback * …`</Src>, followed by the write.

### 4. A filter in the loop

A wall, a room and a tape all lose more of the top of a sound than of its middle, so an echo in the world comes back duller than it left, and duller again each time round. To do the same, the feedback goes through a filter on its way back to the tape. The point to hold on to is that the filter is _inside_ the loop: the second repeat has been through it once, the third twice. If the filter's gain at a frequency <M t="f" /> is <M t={String.raw`|H(f)|`} />, then the step from one repeat to the next is no longer one number but one for each frequency:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{step}(f) = 20\log_{10}\bigl(g\,|H(f)|\bigr) \qquad\qquad T_{60}(f) = \frac{3D}{\log_{10}\bigl(1 / (g\,|H(f)|)\bigr)}`} />

The Tape Echo's filter is two of the kit's, <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" lines="96-102">set on a clock of 16 samples</Src> and <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" lines="124-127">run on what the heads read</Src>. High cut is <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="52-110">the state-variable filter</Src> as a low-pass, 12 dB an octave, with a <M t="Q" /> of 0.6, a little under the flattest setting, so there is no bump at the corner for the loop to feed on. Low cut is <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="8-23">a one-pole high-pass</Src>, 6 dB an octave. Neither is in the path from the heads to the output, so the first repeat is untouched.

Even wide open, High cut stops at 16 kHz, and a third of a click's energy lies above that. One sample loses <Claim id="click-step">8.4 dB on its first trip</Claim> and less on each trip after, 6.2 dB by the eleventh, as there is less of it left up there to lose. A filter also takes time to answer. Each trip through the open filter leaves the click's peak about half a sample later, so the eighth repeat peaks <Claim id="late">four samples late</Claim>. The loop is a little longer than the line.

### 5. Saturation: what stops a runaway

At a gain of 1 the series never dies, and above 1 each repeat is _louder_ than the last. In the plain loop of stage 3 that has no end but overflow. This device lets Feedback go to 1.1 on purpose, so that a trail can be made to build, and it puts a soft ceiling where the sum is recorded:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{tape} = \frac{\tanh(d\,s)}{d}, \qquad d = 1 + 3\,\text{Drive}, \qquad s = \text{in} + g\cdot\text{back}`} />

For a small <M t="s" /> the hyperbolic tangent is a straight line of slope 1, and dividing by <M t="d" /> again puts back what multiplying by it took, so a quiet sound is recorded at the level it arrived. A loud one is not: the tangent never passes 1, so nothing on the tape passes <M t="1/d" />. The engine uses <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" lines="37-42">a rational stand-in</Src>, <M t={String.raw`u\,(27 + u^2)/(27 + 9u^2)`} />, which reaches exactly 1 at <M t="u = 3" /> and is held there.

Now the loop. Its gain for a sound at some level is <M t="g" /> times the tangent's _chord_, <M t={String.raw`\tanh(ds)/(ds)`} />, which is 1 for small sounds and falls as they grow. ### 6. A delay that moves bends the pitch

Suppose the delay is not a number but a function of time, <M t="D(t)" /> in seconds, so that the output is <M t={String.raw`y(t) = x(t - D(t))`} />. Put a sine of frequency <M t="f" /> in. What comes out has the phase <M t={String.raw`2\pi f\,(t - D(t))`} />, and frequency is the rate at which phase advances:

<M block t={String.raw`f_{\text{out}} = f\,\Bigl(1 - \frac{dD}{dt}\Bigr) \qquad\qquad \text{cents} = 1200\log_2\Bigl(1 - \frac{dD}{dt}\Bigr)`} />

Only the _rate of change_ of the delay matters. While the delay grows, the read point falls behind the write point and the sound plays slow and flat; while it shrinks, the read point catches up and the sound plays fast and sharp. A delay growing by 1 ms every second plays 0.1 % flat, which is 1.7 cents. On a tape machine <M t={String.raw`1 - dD/dt`} /> is the speed of the tape past the head, and the device keeps exactly that as <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" lines="66-77">a meter</Src>, which the lab reads.

A real transport is never steady, and the Tape Echo has two controls for how unsteady. <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" lines="104-107">Each sample</Src> it adds to the read point Wow times 1.6 ms times a slow drift, <Src path="cpp/kit/lfo.h" lines="74-104">three sines</Src> near half a hertz that never line up the same way twice, and Flutter times 0.07 ms times a faster one, mostly a sine at 7.1 Hz. Both start at zero here.

Over an eight-second loop, Wow at 1 moves the read point by as much as <Claim id="wow-range">1.45 ms and the pitch between 6.5 cents flat and 7.8 sharp</Claim>. The wobble is added to the read point as a time, not as a share of Time, so the bend is <Claim id="same-wobble">the same at any Time</Claim>. To check the formula against the sound, a test plays a 1 kHz sine through the wobbling delay and counts its zero crossings: the pitch that comes out is within <Claim id="pitch-formula">0.1 cent</Claim> of what the meter says.

Turning Time is the same thing, larger. If the read point jumped to its new place the output would step from one part of the wave to another, which is a click. So <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" lines="37">Time has a lag</Src>, a one-pole smoother with a time constant <M t={String.raw`\tau`} /> of 120 ms where most other controls have 5 ms: a motor that takes a moment to change speed. A change of Time by <M t={String.raw`\Delta`} /> then follows <M t={String.raw`D(t) = D_1 - \Delta\,e^{-t/\tau}`} />, and by the formula above the speed starts at <M t={String.raw`1 - \Delta/\tau`} /> and returns to 1 as the motor settles. The last control above turns Time half way through the loop and back at its start. Lengthen Time by 60 ms and the tape drops at once to <Claim id="motor">half speed</Claim>, an octave down. Lengthen it by 120 ms and the tape stands still for an instant; by more, and it runs backwards.

### 7. More heads, two sides and the mix

The line can be read in more than one place for the price of another read. The Tape Echo has three heads, at Time, two thirds of Time and one third, and <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" lines="155-164">four ways of mixing them</Src>. The sum of the heads is both what you hear and what goes back round, so every head's repeats repeat. With Three chosen and Time at 600 ms, a click comes back <Claim id="three-heads">at 200, 400 and 600 ms</Claim>.

Each side has a tape of its own. Ping pong sends the input to the left tape only and crosses the feedback, so the left tape's echo is recorded on the right and the right's on the left: at 1 the repeats <Claim id="ping-pong">take turns</Claim>, left first.

Last, Mix sets the balance of the input against the heads with a sine and a cosine, <M t={String.raw`\text{out} = \cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2} m)\,\text{in} + \sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2} m)\,\text{heads}`} />. A sound and its echo are different signals whose powers add, so this crossfade keeps the total power level where a straight one would dip in the middle: at 0.5 each is at <Claim id="mix-half">0.707</Claim>. At 0 the output is <Claim id="mix-zero">the input, sample for sample</Claim>.

## What goes wrong

**Clicks when the delay jumps.** Any step in the read point is a step in the wave. The cure is always to move the read point smoothly and accept the bend in pitch, as the motor lag of stage 6 does, or to fade between two reads.

**The top of the sound breathing.** As the delay moves, the fraction sweeps from 0 to 1 over and over, and the read's response sweeps with it between flat and the half-sample curve of stage 2. With a linear read that is 2 dB coming and going at 10 kHz. It is the reason this device pays for the cubic.

**A loop that feeds on itself.** Any frequency at which <M t={String.raw`g\,|H(f)|`} /> is 1 or more grows. That is why the high cut has no resonant bump, why the low cut is there at all (its gain at 0 Hz is nothing, so the loop cannot pile up an offset), and why the record path saturates.

**A tail that never quite ends.** Below a gain of 1 the repeats shrink for ever without reaching zero, down into numbers so small that a processor handles them slowly. The record path snaps those to zero, and once the output has stayed under −140 dB for 2.5 seconds, longer than the longest Time, <Src path="cpp/kit/idle.h">the device goes to sleep</Src>. Given silence it puts out <Claim id="silence">exact silence</Claim>.

**The ends of the range.** A Hermite read needs a sample on the near side of the read point, so the device will not read closer than 4 samples, and Time itself stops at 30 ms and at 2 seconds.

## What to try

- Clicks, Time at 30 ms, Feedback at 0.9. The repeats come 33 times a second: listen for whether you still hear them one by one, or as a tone at one over Time.
- Drums, Time at 375 ms, Feedback at 0.5: three sixteenths at this loop's tempo, so each hit's echo lands three steps later in the pattern.
- Guitar, Wow at 1, then Flutter at 1. Watch the speed trace while each note's echo comes back.
- Drums, Feedback at 1.1, and move Drive. Listen on "On the tape" as the trail fills up to its ceiling.
- Guitar, High cut at 2 kHz, Feedback at 0.8. Listen for each repeat coming back duller than the one before it.
- Any source, "Move Time" at −125 ms. Half way through the loop, listen to the repeats already on the tape: the tape starts at more than twice its speed and settles.
- Clicks, Heads on Three, Ping pong at 1.
