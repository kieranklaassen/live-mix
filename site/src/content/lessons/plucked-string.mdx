<Part id="why" title="A string does not fade. It loses its partials one at a time.">

A guitar string is picked once and left alone. The pick touches it for a few thousandths of a second. The note that follows lasts for seconds, and in all that time nobody does anything to the string. The picture above shows both, as the engine's Guitar makes them: the pluck is the spike at the left edge, and everything after it is the string by itself.

Nothing goes into this lesson's lab. It presses one key <Src path="site/labs/plucked-string.cpp" at="constexpr int kKeyDown = 480;">10 ms into the loop</Src>, holds it for the time that Key held for sets, and every sound and picture on this page is read out of the Guitar while that one note rings.

Listen to the note for what changes in it. <Hear set="alone" label="Hear the string" /> Listen for a bright start with a snap in it, for a rounder note a second later, and for how little is left at the end besides the bottom of the note. The note gets quieter, and its colour changes while it does.

That change has an exact description. A steady note is a sum of sine waves: one at the note's own frequency, the fundamental, and others near twice, three times, four times that frequency and so on up. Each of those is a partial, and <Lesson id="fourier-transform" /> is about taking a sound apart into them. In the picture below, time runs across and frequency runs up, and each level line is one partial of the string.

Measured on that note, the tenth partial <Claim id="darkens">starts 22 dB under the fundamental and is 75 dB under it half a second later</Claim>. Both have fallen in that half second, the tenth partial far further than the fundamental. The top of the note has gone.

So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you make a note in which every partial dies at its own speed, the high ones first, without keeping a separate fade for each of them?

### The obvious answer fails

Take a wave that is as bright as the pluck and turn it down over time. An oscillator makes the wave (<Lesson id="oscillators-and-subtractive-voice" />) and one fade turns it down. Here it is: a saw wave at the same pitch, which has every partial, under a fade that falls exactly as fast as the string's fundamental does.

<Still
  taps="fixedPartials"
  view="spectrogram"
  caption="The fixed wave under its one fade, over the same four seconds. Each partial starts a little weaker than the one below it and all fall at one rate, so they leave the picture along a straight slope. In the string's picture the top was gone almost at once. Notice the axis, too: it runs twice as high here, because the top stays."
/>

Measured, <Claim id="fixed-together">the tenth partial of the fixed wave falls at 17 dB a second, and so does its fundamental</Claim>. A fade multiplies the whole wave by one number, so it cannot take more from one partial than from another. The wave ends as bright as it began, only smaller. <Hear set="fixed" label="Hear the fixed wave fade" /> Listen for the moment it turns round and soft, as the string did. It does not come.

A second fade could be added, on a filter that closes as the note dies. That is nearer, and it is how a synthesiser imitates a string from the outside: two fades, each with times to set, describing a result. A real string has no fades. It gets the same result from the way it moves.

### What a string does instead

Pull a string aside and let go, and the kink you made does not stay where it is. It splits in two, and each half runs along the string to an end. The end is fixed, so the kink is turned upside down there and sent back. It crosses the string, is turned over again at the far end, and arrives where it began, the right way up. Then it makes the same journey again.

The time of one round trip is one period of the note. A string that sounds the A below middle C sends its shape round 220 times a second.

Each trip costs a little. The ends give, the air drags, the string resists being bent. Sharp corners in the shape cost more than gentle curves, and sharp corners are what high partials are. So the shape comes round a little smaller and a little rounder every time. Nothing decides that the high partials should die first. They pay more for each trip, and every partial makes the same number of trips.

### A loop with a delay in it

A shape that goes away and comes back later is a delay, and a computer has delays (<Lesson id="delay-and-echo" />): a line of memory that gives back what was put into it a fixed number of samples ago. Make the line as long as one period. Send what comes out of it back in, through a filter that takes a little off the top. Whatever shape is put in then comes past again and again, once a period, a little rounder each time.

That is the whole technique, and textbooks call it the Karplus-Strong string. There is no oscillator in it and no recording of a string. The line only remembers its last period.

Here is a sentence to check against the picture. Leave the three controls where the lesson starts them, and count how often the later line passes through zero. <Claim id="noise-crossings">It crosses 8 times, where the noise it began as crosses 106 times</Claim>.

In those twenty trips <Claim id="noise-shrinks">the largest sample of the period falls from 0.83 to 0.19, and the steepest step between two neighbouring samples from 1.26 to 0.028</Claim>. The height is still nearly a quarter of what it was. The steepness is a forty-fifth. A low-pass takes most from whatever changes fastest, and the loop puts the line through the same low-pass once every trip.

Sustain sets how much each trip costs. With a longer Sustain the later line keeps its teeth for more trips. Pitch sets how long the period is: a higher note has fewer samples across the picture, and makes more trips in a second, which is why a high string dies sooner than a low one at the same setting.

Notice what the picture leaves out. It says nothing about whether the note is in tune, and a loop with a filter in it is not in tune by itself. It starts from noise, where a player starts with a pick at one place on the string. And it is one string, where the Guitar has two for each note. Those are the pieces the rest of the lesson adds.

<Part id="build" title="Start with a hiss. Add one piece. Listen. Add the next.">

The quickest way to understand the string is to make one. This part starts with a burst of noise and adds seven pieces to it, one at a time. After each piece the picture is drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

The build takes its numbers from the device as it runs: how much the filter keeps, how long the line is. It then does the arithmetic with them in its own lines. Where the numbers come from is the subject of part 5.

Some of the steps are wrong on purpose. Hearing what is missing is how the next piece earns its place.

  <M t={String.raw`T_0`} /> is a decay time as the note was ringing and <M t={String.raw`T_1`} /> the time under the finger. <M t="u" /> is how far the finger has come down, from nothing to 1 in those steps. Each step multiplies the time by the same amount, so neither the loop's gain nor its length jumps, and nothing clicks. Measured at the starting settings, <Claim id="damped">the level of the note falls at 449 dB a second from 30 ms to 80 ms after key up</Claim>.

  />

<StageBlock tap="pickup" n={9} title="The pickup is a second comb">
  The pickup is the pick's comb again, on the way out this time: it hears the string at one point, and not a partial that is still there. The Guitar's signal is each string, less itself a fraction of a period earlier:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`d_1[n] = \at{render-first}{y_1[n] - y_1[n - q P_1]}`}
  />

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sigma[n] = c_1\,d_1[n] + \at{render-second}{c_2\,d_2[n]}`}
  />

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{p[n]} = \at{render-curve}{\bigl(\sigma[n] + \kappa\,\sigma[n]^2\bigr)}\,\gamma[n]`}
  />

  <M t={String.raw`y_1`} /> and <M t={String.raw`y_2`} /> are the sounds of the two strings. <M t={String.raw`d_1`} /> is the first as the pickup hears it: the string, less itself <M t={String.raw`q P_1`} /> samples earlier, where <M t={String.raw`P_1`} /> is that string's period. <M t={String.raw`d_2`} /> is the same for the second string. <M t={String.raw`\sigma`} /> is the two together, with <M t={String.raw`c_1`} /> and <M t={String.raw`c_2`} /> how much of each the pickup hears, and <M t="p" /> is what the pickup hands on. <M t="q" /> is the pickup's distance from the bridge as a fraction of the sounding length. It grows with every fret, so the comb sits somewhere else on every note of the neck, where a fixed filter would cut the same frequencies on all of them. <M t={String.raw`\kappa`} /> is <Src path="cpp/devices/guitar/guitar.h" at="static constexpr float kPickupCurve = 0.07f;">a small number</Src> that adds a little second harmonic to hard notes. <M t={String.raw`\gamma`} /> is a gain that keeps notes about as loud wherever the pickup is, and carries the Swell.

<Part id="ways" title="Five things players do with these controls.">

Each of these is a habit of playing, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up to the engine and see what the comb, the loss and the level are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Pick by the bridge" tag="a low Pick position, a hard pick" set="bridge">
    Close to the bridge the comb of stage 6 has its first dive far up, so every low partial is let through, but weakly, and the high ones come out strong. <Claim id="way-bridge">The second pluck is 12 samples ahead of the first, and the fundamental comes out 9 dB lower than in the picked note</Claim>. The card is there to hear how thin that is. After it, move Pick position up a little at a time and listen for the body of the note coming back.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A soft thumb over the neck" tag="Hardness at nothing, a high Pick position" set="thumb">
    A soft pick is a low corner on the pluck's second low-pass, so the top partials are never put into the line at all. Picking further from the bridge takes more of them out. <Claim id="way-thumb">The tenth partial of the output stands 27 dB under the first, where the picked note has it 9 dB under</Claim>. Players use a note like this for a slow line or the bottom of a chord.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Leave it to ring" tag="a long Sustain, the key held" set="ring">
    A plucked string cannot be held at a level. It can only be left alone for longer. With a long Sustain the loss filter is nearly flat and the gain of the loop is nearly 1, and <Claim id="way-ring">the first string's fundamental falls at 5 dB a second</Claim>, less than a third of the rate stage 3 measured. After the card, listen to the last second for how much of the note is still there.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Set the two strings a little apart" tag="a slow Shimmer" set="apart">
    This is the device's own starting Shimmer, which this lesson takes off so that the string can be measured. With it the note is never quite still. Each partial drifts in and out at its own slow rate, as stage 7 worked out, and a held chord moves a little without anything being played. Only an ear can say how much is right. After the card, try Shimmer at nothing and listen for the note going still.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Hide the pick" tag="Swell, with a long Sustain" set="swell">
    Swell brings the pickup's signal up from nothing after the pick. The string is struck as hard as ever, but the strike is not heard: <Claim id="way-swell">the loudest sample of the first 20 ms comes out 41 dB under the picked note's</Claim>. What arrives is a string that is already ringing. It wants a long Sustain, or there is little left to arrive.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Eight ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="A note that dies dull" set="dull">
    Sustain is not a volume. A short Sustain makes the loss filter of stage 3 heavy, and a heavy low-pass in a loop takes the top first and fastest. <Claim id="fault-dull">The fundamental of this note's first string falls at 109 dB a second</Claim> and the upper partials are gone within a few trips. Listen for a thud with a pitch. For a short note that stays bright, keep Sustain long and let the key go early: the finger of stage 8 stops it.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A wobble" set="wobble">
    A slow Shimmer is movement. A fast one is two strings heard to be out of tune. <Claim id="fault-wobble">The level of this note dips more than 6 dB under what it is with the two strings in tune</Claim>, over and over, and the upper partials beat faster still. On one note it is a wobble. On a chord every note would wobble at a rate of its own. Turn Shimmer down until the movement is felt more than heard.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Nearly all pick" set="spike">
    A hard pick close to the bridge, heard by a pickup close to the bridge, puts nearly everything into the first few milliseconds. <Claim id="fault-spike">The loudest sample stands 23.6 dB over the level of the note from a tenth to six tenths of a second after the pick</Claim>, a good deal further than in the picked note of part 3. In a mix the meter reads the spike and the ear hears the body, so the part is loud on the meter and quiet in the room. Soften the pick or move it, before reaching for the compressor of <Lesson id="compression" />.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Only the fundamental is in tune">
    Stage 5 tunes the loop at one frequency, the note's own. The loss filter does not hold every frequency equally long, so the upper partials come round a little early and sound a little sharp. At the starting settings that is too small to matter. A heavy loss filter makes it larger: <Claim id="loss-sharpens">with Sustain at 1 s the fifth partial is more than 8 cents sharp, where the stiff string's law puts it 1.6 cents sharp</Claim>. A short Sustain changes where the partials stand as well as how long they last.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A loop that never lets go">
    A loop whose gain reaches 1 at any frequency holds what it is given there for ever. The kit guards against that in two places. `design` never lets the loop's gain go above <Src path="cpp/kit/string.h" at="static constexpr float kMaxLoopGain = 0.99999f;">a hair under 1</Src>, however long a decay is asked for. And the pick's comb puts the pluck in twice with opposite signs, so the two add up to nothing and no steady push is left standing in the line.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Numbers too small to hold">
    A tail that falls for long enough reaches numbers so small that a processor handles them very slowly, and a loop keeps feeding them back to itself. Each filter in the loop therefore <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h" at="inline float flush_denormal(float x) {">sets such a value to nothing</Src>. Nothing is heard of it. Without it, a quiet tail would cost more to compute than a loud note.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A pick on a string that is still moving">
    Striking a note again cannot simply clear the line: the string was somewhere, and a jump to nothing is a click. The Guitar fades the old sound out of the pickup quickly and only then puts in the new pluck. It takes <Src path="cpp/devices/guitar/guitar.h" at="static constexpr float kRepluckSeconds = 0.008f;">8 ms</Src> over it when the same note is played again and <Src path="cpp/devices/guitar/guitar.h" at="static constexpr float kStealSeconds = 0.003f;">3 ms</Src> when the string is taken for another.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Nothing at the very top">
    The higher the note, the shorter the line, and the more of the loop is filter and the less is delay. The kit stops at <Src path="cpp/kit/string.h" at="hz_ = clamp(hz, lowest_hz(sample_rate), sample_rate * 0.125f);">an eighth of the sample rate</Src>. A note asked for above that sounds at the limit, which is flat. A real neck ends well below it.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="The noise is the sound of the string.">
    The noise is only what the line is first filled with. Part 1 fed one period of it to a loop and a note came out, and the note's pitch was the loop's length. The kit's string is not fed noise at all: stage 1 shows a rounded pulse with a little scrape on it. What makes the sound a string is the loop.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The delay line is one period long.">
    The whole loop is one period long. The filters in it take up part of that, and the line has to be shorter by as much. Stage 5 has the sum for the starting note, and its picture shows what a line of one period would do to every note of the neck.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Sustain is a fade on the output.">
    A fade turns every partial down together, and part 1 has the sound of that beside the string. Sustain sets two decay times inside the loop, from which the kit solves the loss filter. The upper partials always go first, and a short Sustain takes them almost at once, as the dull note of part 7 shows.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="It plays back a recording of a string.">
    Nothing is recorded. The line holds the last period of what the loop itself made, and nothing else. That is why the pick's place, the pick's hardness and the note's length can change from one note to the next, and why step 8 of the build could arrive at the device's own signal from a few dozen lines.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The low-pass is a tone control.">
    A tone control filters a sound once. This one sits in the loop, so the sound passes it once every period, hundreds of times a second. One pass costs a small part of a decibel where most partials live, as the picture of stage 3 shows. The darkening is that small loss, paid again on every trip.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A high note rings as long as a low one.">
    A high note makes more trips in a second and pays the loop's loss on each. The Guitar shortens its ring further on purpose, as a short string's is. The high string of part 3 is two octaves above the starting note at the same Sustain, and its card has its rate to set beside the one stage 3 measured.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
