<Part id="why" title="A room answers a clap with thousands of echoes. A delay line answers with a buzz.">

Clap once in a tiled room. The clap reaches you directly, then again off the nearest wall, then off the others, then off two walls in turn, then three. Soon the copies arrive by the thousand, too close together to tell apart, and what is left is one tail that fades. Every bounce takes a little of the sound away, and more of its high frequencies than of its low ones, so the tail grows duller as it dies.

That is reverberation, and it comes down to three things. The echoes grow dense. The tail dies away. The highs die first.

Every reverb has to deliver those three, however it is made. This lesson is the first of four about ways to make one. <Lesson id="feedback-delay-network" cap /> gets there with a bank of delay lines that feed one another, <Lesson id="convolution-reverb" /> by replaying a room from its answer to a single click, and <Lesson id="spring-reverb" /> with a model of a steel spring. The three properties are stated here once, for all four lessons, each with the way it is measured.

A recorded sound usually has none of them. A microphone a hand's width from a drum hears the drum and little of the room. An instrument plugged straight into a desk was never in a room at all.

The simplest sound of that kind is a click: one sample that stands up from silence. It is the clap with nothing after it, and the page opens on a row of them. Here is the row as it went in. <Hear set="click" label="Hear the clicks alone" /> And here it is through the engine's plate reverb. <Hear set="answer" label="Hear the clicks through the plate" />

A click is a faint thing to listen to, and so is its answer. The plate spreads the energy of one sample over more than a second, so <Claim id="click-level">the tallest sample of the answer is 0.096 of the click's height, and its level over the loop is −46.8 dB</Claim> against full scale, the largest number a sample can be. Turn the playing level up for the clicks. On the drum loop the same settings come out <Claim id="drums-level">26 dB louder</Claim>.

So here is the question this lesson answers. What has to be done to one sample to turn it into that wash, with nothing but arithmetic on samples?

### The obvious answer fails

Play the sound again a moment later. That is a delay line, the ring of memory of <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" />: it hands back what it was given, some time on. Feed part of what it hands back into its own start and one click becomes a row of echoes, each lower than the last.

The lab runs such a loop beside the plate so that it can be heard and counted. It is a delay of <Src path="site/labs/plate-reverb.cpp" at="constexpr int kCombLength = 229;">229 samples</Src> with <Src path="site/labs/plate-reverb.cpp" at="constexpr float kInputGainA = 0.750f;">three quarters</Src> of its output fed back.

### What a reverb is made of, measured

Here are the three properties as the engine's plate has them, each read off its answer to one click, with the controls where the page opens them.

The echoes grow dense. That is the climbing line in the answer to the question above.

The tail dies away, and at close to a steady rate. The next picture shows, at each moment after the click, how much of the answer's energy is still to come.

<Still

A straight line in decibels means that each stretch of time keeps the same share of what the stretch before it had. The length of a reverb is by custom the time its tail takes to fall 60 dB, to a thousandth of its level. Nobody waits for the whole fall. As rooms are measured, the lab takes <Src path="site/labs/plate-reverb.cpp" at="const float from = time_at(data, length, x0, x1, -5.0f);" to="return from >= 0.0f && to > from ? 3.0f * (to - from) : 0.0f;">three times the stretch from 5 dB down to 25 dB down</Src> of that line. Read that way at the left output, the plate <Claim id="fall-start">falls 60 dB in 1.7 s</Claim>. The reading moves with the stretch it is taken from: <Claim id="fall-read">a decibel higher or a decibel lower, and it comes out between 1.64 s and 1.72 s</Claim>. So this lesson and the three after it write a fall time read this way to two figures.

Look again and the line is not quite straight. It starts a little steeper than it goes on: <Claim id="fall-whole">the line is 60 dB down 1.9 s after the click</Claim>, later than the reading says. The reason is the third property.

The highs die first. The lab reads the same fall in <Src path="site/labs/plate-reverb.cpp" at="constexpr int kBands = 13;">thirteen bands</Src>, each an octave wide, from <Src path="site/labs/plate-reverb.cpp" at="constexpr float kBandLowest = 250.0f;">250 Hz</Src> to <Src path="site/labs/plate-reverb.cpp" at="constexpr float kBandHighest = 16000.0f;">16 kHz</Src>.

<Claim id="fall-bands">The band round 250 Hz takes 2.1 s to fall 60 dB, the band round 4 kHz takes 1.8 s and the band round 16 kHz takes 1.2 s</Claim>. Over the right-hand half of that line, every band falls sooner than the band under it. Over the left-hand half the line goes up as well as down, <Claim id="fall-wander">by less than 0.2 s from one band to the next</Claim>. So the top of the sound is gone first and the bottom is what is left at the end, falling at its own slower rate, and that is the bend in the line of the picture before.

### Why it is called a plate

The name comes from a machine: a thin sheet of steel hung in a frame, with a driver to shake it and pickups to listen to it. Sound spreads through a sheet in every direction and comes back off every edge, so its echoes are dense almost from the start.

The engine's device is named for that sound and is not built like it. There is no steel in it and no model of steel. It is delay lines, and one small filter used over and over, arranged so that the three properties come out. <Lesson id="spring-reverb" cap /> is the other kind of reverb: arithmetic that follows the physical thing.

The lesson starts the device on the plate alone, with a shorter tail than the device's own default, and it holds still two delays that the device keeps moving, so that the structure can be seen before the movement. The dry sound and the movement both come back as controls, and part 9 sets the lesson's starting settings beside the device's.

</Part>

<Part id="build" title="Start with a gap. Add what makes it a plate, a piece at a time.">

The quickest way to understand the plate is to make one. This part begins with a single delay and adds seven pieces to it. After each piece the pictures are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

Two pictures follow most steps. The first is one click through the build so far, so that each piece can be seen doing its work on the simplest sound there is. The second shows the loop that is playing.

The build keeps its memory in the device's own kind of delay line and takes its lengths from the device, so it is the pieces and not a second copy of the numbers. Part 5 says where each number comes from.

  <Step title="Four all-passes" controls="predelay">
    An all-pass is the loop of part 1, a delay with part of its output fed back, and one line more: some of what has just gone in is taken straight off what comes out. That line cancels the buzz. Every frequency leaves at the level it came in at, and all that changes is when each one arrives. With a delay hundreds of samples long, what comes out is a train of echoes. Stage 3 of part 5 goes through its two lines of arithmetic.

    One train is still evenly spaced. So the build puts four all-passes in a row, of four different lengths. Each echo of the first sets off a whole train in the second, each of those a train in the third, and so on.

    The burst is dense, and it is gone at once. To make it last, the build sends it round a loop.   <M
    block
    t={String.raw`|D(f)| = \frac{1 - a}{\sqrt{1 - 2a\cos\omega + a^{2}}}`}
  />

  where <M t={String.raw`\omega`} /> is the frequency as an angle, <M t={String.raw`2\pi f / f_s`} />, and <M t="f_s" /> is the sample rate. The gain is 1 at the bottom of the spectrum and falls towards <M t={String.raw`(1-a)/(1+a)`} /> at the top. Measured with steady tones, <Claim id="damping-cutoff">it is 3.0 dB down at 10.6 kHz with Damping at 0.3, and at 2.75 kHz with Damping at 0.7</Claim>.

  The sound in the tank passes this filter twice a trip. So the gain round the loop becomes <M t={String.raw`d^{4}\,|D(f)|^{2}`} />, and every frequency gets a time of its own:

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`T_{60}(f) = \frac{-3\,T}{\log_{10}\bigl(d^{4}\,|D(f)|^{2}\bigr)}`}
  />

  <Fragment slot="readback">
    Read it against the picture. At the left of the line <M t="|D(f)|" /> is close to 1, the equation is that of stage 6, and Damping has almost nothing to say. Towards the right <M t="|D(f)|" /> shrinks, the logarithm grows more negative, and the time shortens. With Decay at 0.5 and Damping at 0.7 the equation gives <Claim id="damping-formula">1.8 s at 500 Hz and 1.2 s at 5 kHz</Claim>.

    The lab measures both, by putting the answer through two band-pass filters and reading a time off each, which are the two readings under the picture. With the delays still, at a Damping of 0.7 <Claim id="damping-bands">the band at 5 kHz falls 60 dB in 1.3 s, which is 0.7 of its time with no damping. The band at 500 Hz keeps 0.99 of its time</Claim>. Close to a third of the highs' time is gone and a hundredth of the lows'.

    The device lets Damping go no higher than <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb.cpp" at="if (damping > 0.9999f) damping = 0.9999f;">0.9999</Src>. Part 7 has what the top of that range does to the tail.
  </Fragment>

<Part id="ways" title="Five things people do with a plate.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the pictures of part 2 and part 5 are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Keep the note clear" tag="a longer Pre-delay" set="clear">
    A plate answers almost at once, and on a busy part its wash lands on top of the note that made it. A longer Pre-delay moves the whole answer later and leaves the front of the note alone. On this card <Claim id="way-clear">the plate first answers a click 88.9 ms after it, where with Pre-delay at 20 ms it answered 28.9 ms after</Claim>. After the card, lengthen Pre-delay further and listen for the point where the plate turns into an echo of the part.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A short plate" tag="Decay low" set="short">
    A short tail thickens a sound without filling the space after it. With Decay at 0.25 and the delays moving, the plate's answer to a click <Claim id="way-short">takes between 0.9 s and 1 s to fall 60 dB</Claim>. On drums that is enough to give each hit a body and leave the gaps between the hits open. After the card, raise Decay a little at a time and listen for the gaps closing.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A bright plate" tag="Damping at 0" set="bright">
    With no damping the top of the tail lasts nearly as long as the bottom. On this card the answer to a click <Claim id="way-bright">falls 60 dB in 2.4 s round 5 kHz, and round 500 Hz takes less than 0.2 s longer</Claim>. Listen to the cymbals, whose wash now rings on. After the card, raise Damping and listen for the top closing down while the length of the low end stays.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A long dark wash" tag="Decay up, Damping up" set="dark">
    A long tail that is also bright soon covers what is played over it. Raising Damping with Decay keeps the length where it does least harm, at the bottom. On this card the answer to a click <Claim id="way-dark">falls 60 dB in about 8 s round 500 Hz and in 2.6 s round 5 kHz</Claim>: the low end rings three times as long as the top. After the card, take Damping back to 0 and listen for the hiss of the strums building up.
  </Way>
  <Way title="One plate for several sounds" tag="Mix at 1" set="alone">
    A plate does not have to sit on one sound. Put one on a path of its own, send a little of several sounds to it, and they all seem to stand in one place. On such a path the dry sound is already in the mix, so the plate puts out the plate alone: with Mix at 1, <Claim id="way-alone">what the plate adds is the output, sample for sample</Claim>. The card plays the drums, the bass and the chords through one plate, and you hear only what would be added to them.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Seven ways it goes wrong, four of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Low notes that will not stop" set="mud">
    Damping takes the top off the tail and leaves the bottom its full length. Nothing in the device takes the bottom off. A bass line with a long Decay rings on under the notes that follow until the notes blur: on this card <Claim id="fault-mud">what the plate adds stands 1.8 dB over the bass line itself, and the quietest fifth of a second of the loop goes from −42 dB to −23 dB</Claim>. Shorten Decay, lower Mix, or keep the plate off the bass.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A held tone that swells and fades" set="swell">
    The movement of stage 8 is there to stop a tone ringing at one fixed level. On a single pure tone, with the plate alone, it does that by moving the level about: on this card a 440 Hz tone <Claim id="fault-swell">swings through 21 dB across the loop, and with the delays still it holds within 0.001 dB</Claim>. The device has no way to hold its delays still. On a sound with many frequencies in it the swings even out, as stage 8 of part 5 measures on the drums. On one bare tone the cure is a lower Mix, so the dry tone carries the level.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A gap long enough to hear" set="slap">
    Pre-delay is a gap, and a gap long enough is heard as an echo. On this card the plate <Claim id="fault-slap">first answers a click 259 ms after it</Claim>, and each drum hit is followed by a second, blurred hit a quarter of a second later. If the plate is meant to sit behind the sound, shorten Pre-delay until the two join.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Damping at the top shuts the loop" set="shut">
    The damping filter is inside the loop. With Damping at its top the filter keeps nearly all of its own past and lets almost nothing new through, so nothing goes round, whatever Decay says. On this card Decay is at 0.9 and the answer to a click <Claim id="fault-shut">falls 60 dB in 0.5 s, where with Damping at 0.3 it takes 10 s</Claim>. What comes out is the one pass of the burst and next to no tail. For a dark tail stop well short of the top.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A sound that is opposite on the two sides gets no plate">
    The device adds left and right before the plate. Whatever is the same on both sides goes in whole, and whatever is opposite on the two sides cancels. Stage 1 of part 5 measures the far case: a tone that is upside down on one side gets no reverb at all. A wide sound keeps the part its two sides share and loses the part in which they differ. Nothing in the device changes that. Where it matters, narrow the sound before the plate.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Highs lost with Damping at 0">
    Reading between two samples by a straight line is itself a gentle low-pass, and each half's first all-pass does it on every pass, still or moving, because its length at rest is not a whole number of samples. So even with Damping at 0 the top of the spectrum goes a little early: with the delays still <Claim id="still-loses-highs">the band round 16 kHz falls 60 dB in 1.6 s where the band round 1 kHz takes 1.9 s</Claim>. It leans the way a room leans. There is nothing to set: it is how the device is made.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A step when a control moves">
    The device takes Mix, Decay, Damping and Pre-delay <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb_device.cpp" at="void PlateReverbDevice::set_param(PlateReverbParam param, float value) {" to="reverb_.set_predelay_ms(value);">the moment they are set</Src>, with no glide from the old value to the new. A change of Mix made while a sound is playing is a step in the output at that sample. A change of Pre-delay moves the place the delay line is read from, and what is there need not be anything like what was being read: the jump goes into the plate, which answers it as it answers a click. Listen for a tick when either is moved under a held sound, and set them between phrases.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

One thing that would go wrong is taken care of. A loop that multiplies by less than 1 gets smaller for ever and never reaches zero, and numbers that small are slow to work with. Every write to a delay line in the plate goes through <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h" at="inline float flush_denormal(float x) {">`flush_denormal`</Src>, which turns anything smaller than <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h" at="constexpr float kDenormalThreshold = 1.0e-15f;">a thousand million millionth</Src> into an exact zero. So a tail does end, and the device can tell that it has, which is how it knows when to rest.

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A reverb is a lot of echoes, so a delay with its feedback turned up is a reverb.">
    A delay with feedback has echoes that die away, and it stops there. Its echoes never get any closer together, and evenly spaced echoes are a pitch. The answer to the question of part 1 counts them against the plate's, and stage 3 of part 5 measures the pitch.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="An all-pass does nothing, since it changes no level.">
    It changes when each frequency arrives. One all-pass turns a click into a train of echoes, and four turn it into a burst, with every frequency still at the level it came in at. Steps 1 and 2 of the build show the click before and after, and stage 3 of part 5 has the measurement.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Decay is the length of the reverb in seconds.">
    Decay is a gain: what the sound keeps each time it is multiplied on its way round the loop. The time comes from the gain through a logarithm, so equal steps of Decay are not equal steps of time. The table in stage 6 of part 5 has the time at four settings.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="With Decay at 0 there is no reverb.">
    With Decay at 0 nothing goes round the loop, and the burst still crosses each half once and is read at seven places on the way. Stage 6 of part 5 measures that as a tail of nearly half a second.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Damping is a tone control on the reverb.">
    A tone control would change the brightness of the whole tail by one amount. Damping is inside the loop, so it acts again on every trip: the start of the tail is hardly changed and the end of it is changed a great deal. What it shortens is the time the highs last, as the picture of part 2 and the line of stage 7 show.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A stereo reverb keeps the left and right of what goes in.">
    This one takes in the sum of the two sides, as stage 1 of part 5 shows. The two sides that come out are made inside the plate, by reading different places for each, and stage 9 measures how unlike they are. A sound placed hard to one side gets a plate that is as wide as any other's.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
