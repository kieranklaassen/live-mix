<Part id="why" title="A struck bell is bright for a moment and plain for a long time.">

Strike a bell, a wine glass or a metal bar. For the first instant the sound is bright and crowded. A second later it is close to one pure tone, and that tone rings on for a long time. The overtones were all there at the strike, and they died first.

Here is the engine's instrument doing that. The page strikes one note every two seconds.

/>

Listen for the brightness leaving before the note does. <Hear set="note" label="Hear the struck note" /> Then listen to the same note with nothing to make it bright. <Hear set="plain" label="Hear it as one sine" />

So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you make a note from sine waves whose brightness can change from one moment to the next, without building an oscillator for every overtone?

### The obvious answer fails

A sine wave is the plainest sound there is, one frequency and nothing else (<Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" />). The obvious way to make it brighter is to add another sine above it. Here that is done: a sine at the note, and a second one at three and a half times the note, added together. The lab holds both steady, so that a spectrum of them stands still.

<Still

  caption="The spectrum of the two sines added, on an even scale of hertz. There are two lines, the two sines that went in. Nothing new was made."

That is what adding does. On a note at 468.75 Hz, where each line falls on the middle of one of the measure's bins, the sum has <Claim id="added-two">2 lines, and nothing else within 100 dB of them</Claim>. <Hear set="added" label="Hear the two sines added" />

To make a dozen overtones this way takes a dozen oscillators. To make them die away at different speeds, as a bell's do, takes a dozen envelopes as well. It can be done, and it is a lot of machinery for one note.

### Put the second sine somewhere else

The answer of this lesson uses the same two sines and adds nothing to the sound. It moves the second sine to a different place.

A sine oscillator is a counter. The counter is the **phase**: a number that says how far round its cycle the sine has gone. Every sample the phase steps forward a little, and the sound is the sine of wherever it stands. Now let the second sine push that counter forward and pull it back, hundreds of times a second, while the counter goes on stepping.

<Still
  taps="steadyCarrier steady"
  from={0.5}
  span={0.012}
  caption="Twelve milliseconds. The carrier alone, and the held tone: the same carrier with the second sine pushing its phase. Where the push is forward the held tone runs ahead of the carrier, and where it is backward it falls behind."
/>

The sine that is heard is called the **carrier**. The sine that pushes is the **modulator**. The modulator is never heard, and only bends the carrier. Done a few times a second this would be a wobble in pitch. Done hundreds of times a second it is too fast to hear as a wobble, and what comes out is something else.

<Still

  min={-100}
  max={0}

/>

The same two sines, and on that note at 468.75 Hz the held tone has <Claim id="lines-thirteen">13 lines within 60 dB of its strongest</Claim>. <Hear set="held" label="Hear the held tone" />

Two numbers decide what the row of lines looks like. The **ratio** of the modulator's frequency to the carrier's decides where the new lines stand. The **index** is how far the phase is pushed, in radians, and it decides how many lines there are. At an index of 0 nothing is pushed and the tone is the carrier alone.

One more fact before going on. The pushed sine is still one sine of the same height. Only its timing has been bent. So the tone is as loud as it was: with the Index anywhere from 0 to 8, the level of the held tone stays <Claim id="power-same">less than 0.01 dB</Claim> from where it started. Whatever the new lines hold was taken out of the old one.

### And then the push is given an envelope

That is the whole answer to the question. One oscillator makes the note, a second one bends it, and one number, the index, sets how many overtones there are. Let the index be large at the strike and fall away, and the note is bright when it is struck and pure as it rings. Nothing is filtered and no overtone has an oscillator of its own.

The technique is called frequency modulation. The name is a little off, and part 8 says why: it is the phase that is moved. The engine's instrument for it is called Glass. It has four oscillators to a note, which it calls operators, and this lesson starts it as plainly as it goes: one pair of them on the left channel, struck the same way every time. Part 9 puts those settings beside the device's own.

</Part>

<Part id="picture" title="A ladder of lines: the ratio spaces the rungs, the index fills them.">

One picture holds most of what the technique does. It is the spectrum of the held tone, on an even scale of hertz, with the three numbers that shape it: the note's pitch, the ratio and the index.

<Controls ids="index ratio pitch" />

<Spectrum taps="steady" linear fMax={6000} min={-100} max={0}>
  The spectrum of the held tone up to 6 kHz. Try Index from 0 upwards, and notice lines appear
  further and further from the carrier's while the ones already there rise and fall. Try Ratio,
  and notice the lines keep their heights and move apart or together.
</Spectrum>

Check this against the picture. Leave the controls where the lesson starts them: the carrier's line is the one furthest to the left, at the note. Count to the right of it. At a Ratio of 3.5 the next four lines stand at <Claim id="ladder">2.5, 4.5, 6 and 8 times the note</Claim>.

Those four numbers are a ladder with the carrier in the middle. The modulator turns three and a half times as fast as the carrier, and the new lines stand a whole number of modulators above the carrier and below it. One modulator up is 4.5 times the note, and two up is 8. One modulator down is 2.5 times the note below zero. A frequency below zero is not lost: it is the same sine running backwards, and it shows in the spectrum at the same distance above zero. So the lower rungs come back up, at 2.5 and at 6, and stand between the upper ones. With the note at 468.75 Hz, and the Ratio at 1, at 2 and at 3.5, every line of the held tone stands <Claim id="sidebands-where">within one bin of the measure, 11.7 Hz</Claim>, of such a rung.

The new lines are called **side bands**. The ratio spaces them. Set Ratio to 2 and every rung, going up or coming back, lands on an odd multiple of the note. Set it to 1 and they land on every multiple, which is the pattern of a plucked string (<Lesson id="plucked-string" />) or a voice. At 3.5 half of them fall between the note's harmonics, and the tone has the uneven overtones of struck metal.

The index fills the ladder. Take Index to 0 and one line is left: the held tone is then one sine, with every other line <Claim id="one-sine">more than 125 dB under it</Claim> on the note at 468.75 Hz, which is as far down as the measure reaches. Raise it and the lines come in from the carrier outwards. They do not simply grow. Each one rises, falls back to nothing and rises again, as the carrier's own line did in part 1.

Notice what the picture leaves out: time. A held index gives a held tone. The note of part 1 was bright and then pure because its index moved, and most of this instrument is the machinery that moves it.

</Part>

  <Step title="A guard on the way out" controls="pitch index ratio decay attack sustain feedback detune spread">

  ### Lines that come back from below zero

  The lower rungs of the ladder run out of room. With the note at 468.75 Hz and a Ratio of 3.5, the first lower side band belongs two and a half notes below zero. A sine at a frequency below zero is the same sine running backwards, which is the same sine upside down, so it comes back up and stands two and a half notes above zero. With Index at 1 that reflected line reads <Claim id="reflected-level">7.1 dB under the bare carrier</Claim>, and so does the upper one.

  A reflected line that lands on another line adds to it or takes from it, by its sign. At a Ratio of 2 the first lower side band comes back exactly onto the carrier. With Index at 1 the line at the note then reads <Claim id="ratio-two">1.6 dB over</Claim> the bare carrier: more than the carrier had with nothing pushing it.

  ### Why it is called frequency modulation

  The device moves the carrier's phase, and the technique is named for frequency. The two are one thing seen two ways. Frequency is how fast the phase turns, so a phase that is being pushed forward is, for that moment, turning faster.

  <M block t={String.raw`f_{\text{now}} = f + I\,r f \cos\theta_{\text{mod}}`} />

Pushing the phase with a sine is the same as swinging the frequency with a cosine.

The modulator's attack is <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="const float mod_attack = kit::max(0.001f, attack * shape.mod_attack);">a share of the carrier's</Src>, half in this arrangement, so the brightness is there before the level is.
  </Fragment>
</StageBlock>

<StageBlock tap="pairB" n={6} title="The second pair: duller, shorter, and a little out of tune">
  The other two operators of the note make a second pair of the same kind. The device voices it apart from the first, so that one note is two sounds that agree at the strike and drift apart as they ring.

  <Controls ids="detune spread" />

  <Wave taps="modulated:out pairB:alt" from={0.002} span={0.012}>
    Twelve milliseconds at the strike: the first pair and the second. Notice the second is bent
    less. Try Detune at its highest, and notice the second pair slide against the first.
  </Wave>

  The numbers that set them apart are one row of a table in the device. In the arrangement this lesson uses, the second pair's index is <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="// Bell: the second pair is duller and dies first">0.7 of the first's and its modulator's decay 0.6 of the first's</Src>. Measured on the note at the settings the page opens on, the second pair has <Claim id="pair-b">0.70 times the index, and its index is gone in 0.26 s against 0.43 s</Claim>.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h"
    from="struct Shape {"
    to="// Pad: brightness blooms after the level and stays"
    name="shapes"
  />

  The other three rows are the device's other arrangements, which wire the same four operators differently. They are in the same function as the listing of stage 4, and this lesson stays on the first.

  Detune <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="detune_flat_ = kit::cents_to_ratio(-half);">tunes the first pair flat by half its amount</Src> and the second sharp by the other half. Two tones a little apart beat: together they swell and fade at the difference of their frequencies.

  <M block t={String.raw`f_{\text{beat}} = f\,\bigl(2^{\,c/2400} - 2^{-c/2400}\bigr)`} />

  <M t="c" /> is Detune in cents, hundredths of a semitone, and <M t={String.raw`f_{\text{beat}}`} /> is how many times a second the two pairs come round against each other. It grows with the note, so a chord's high notes shimmer faster than its low ones. Spread then leans the first pair to the left and the second to the right, which turns the beating into movement between the speakers.

  <Fragment slot="listen">Hear the second pair alone</Fragment>
</StageBlock>

<StageBlock tap="out" n={7} title="Placing the pairs, a gain, and two guards">
  What is left is plumbing. The two pairs are placed between the speakers, scaled, and passed through two guards.

  <M block t={String.raw`x_{\text{left}} = \at{out-left}{g\,\bigl(y_1 \cos\alpha + y_2 \sin\alpha\bigr)}`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`\alpha = \at{out-angle}{(1 - S)\,\tfrac{\pi}{4}}`} />

  <M t="y_1" /> and <M t="y_2" /> are the two pairs of stages 4 and 6, and <M t="S" /> is Spread. At a Spread of 1 the angle <M t={String.raw`\alpha`} /> is 0, the cosine is 1 and the sine 0: the first pair is alone on the left. At 0 the angle is an eighth of a turn, both are equal, and the pairs sit together in the middle. A cosine and a sine keep their combined power the same at every angle, so the note is as loud wherever the pairs sit. The right channel is the same line with the two pairs changed over. <M t="g" /> is the gain: <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="static constexpr float kVoiceGain = 0.29f;">0.29</Src> for one note at full level, times the Volume.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h"
    from="// Equal-power placement: A leans left by Spread, B right by as much."
    to="out_right_[i] = kit::soft_clip(dc_[1].process((group_a * other + group_b * own) * volume));"
    name="out"
    at={{ left: 'out_left_[i] = kit::soft_clip(dc_[0].process((group_a * own + group_b * other) * volume));', angle: 'const float angle = (1.0f - spread_.next()) * 0.125f;' }}
  />

  The first guard takes out anything that stands still. Part 7 shows where a standing offset comes from. The guard is the smallest high-pass there is:

  <M block t={String.raw`z[n] = \at{blocker-step}{x[n] - x[n-1] + \rho\,z[n-1]}`} />

  <M t="x" /> is what goes in and <M t="z" /> what comes out. The difference <M t={String.raw`x[n] - x[n-1]`} /> is zero for anything that stands still, so an offset cannot get through. The last term feeds most of the output back, with <M t={String.raw`\rho`} /> a little under 1, so that everything that does move passes almost untouched. The device sets it for <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="blocker.set_cutoff(12.0f, sr);">12 Hz</Src>, below any note.

  <Listing
    slot="listing"
    path="cpp/kit/filters.h"
    from="struct DcBlocker {"
    to="const float y = flush_denormal(x - x1 + r * y1);"
    name="blocker"
    at={{ step: 'const float y = flush_denormal(x - x1 + r * y1);' }}
  />
  <Fragment slot="after">
    The second guard is <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" at="inline float soft_clip(float x) {">a soft clip</Src>. Under half of full scale it is a wire, and one note at 0.29 never reaches it. It rounds off only what many notes at once pile up. When the last note has died and the output has been silent for <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="idle_.reset(sr, 0.25f);">a quarter of a second</Src>, the device stops working until the next key.

    <Check id="rebuilt" />

    That check is the lab's proof that the stages above are the device's own. On every sample it takes the phases, the envelopes, each modulator's output and the index out of every sounding note, runs them through the line of stage 4, the placing, the gain and the two guards, and compares the result with the device's two channels. At each of the settings the lab's test tries, the two are <Claim id="rebuilt-exact">at least 140 dB apart</Claim>, which is as far as that measure reads.
  </Fragment>

<Part id="ways" title="Six things people do with two operators.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the index and the lines are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="A bright tick on a plain note" tag="a high ratio, a small index, a short decay" set="tick">
    <Still

    A high ratio puts the side bands far above the note, and a small index makes only one pair of them. With a short Decay they are a click of brightness on the front of a plain tone. Held at this card's Ratio and Index on a note at 468.75 Hz, the carrier's line is <Claim id="way-tick">0.5 dB under the bare carrier, and the one pair, at 13 and 15 times the note, 12.8 dB under it</Claim>. After the card, raise Index and listen for the tick turning into a clang.

  </Way>
  <Way title="Let the brightness linger" tag="a longer Decay" set="linger">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="picture"
      view="spectrogram"
      set="linger"
      top={6000}
      min={-100}
      max={-10}
      caption="The note over the loop, to the same scale. The ladder of lines is still there a second in, and closes slowly."
    />

    Decay is how long the brightness lasts, and the note lasts four times as long. A long Decay makes the colour of the note change slowly while it rings, which is most of what a bell does. Half a second into this card's note the first pair still stands <Claim id="way-linger">9 and 10 dB under the carrier</Claim>, where stage 5 of part 5 found it all but gone in the note the page opens on.

  </Way>
  <Way title="Give the modulator an edge" tag="Feedback, at a Ratio of 1" set="edge">
    A sine makes a ladder that thins out quickly. A modulator with harmonics of its own fills it in, and at a Ratio of 1 every one of them lands on a harmonic of the note, so the tone moves towards a bowed or a brassy one. Held on a note at 468.75 Hz, this card's tenth harmonic stands <Claim id="way-edge">19 dB under the strongest line, and more than 100 dB under it with Feedback at 0</Claim>.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Two pairs a little apart" tag="Detune, and Spread under 1" set="apart">
    One pair is steady. Two pairs a few cents apart beat against each other, and with Spread under 1 both are on both sides, so the beat is heard as a slow swell that moves. The equation of stage 6 gives its rate. After the card, take Detune to 0 and listen for the note standing still.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Swell where there was a strike" tag="a long Attack, Sustain up" set="swell">
    Nothing says the index has to start at its highest. With a long Attack the brightness rises with the level, and with Sustain above 0 both stay up while the key is held. The same two operators then play a held, shifting tone. After the card, watch the two envelopes in the trace of stage 5.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Pick a ratio that fits no harmonic" tag="1.41 or 2.76" set="clang">
    At a whole ratio the lines are harmonics and the ear hears one note. At 2.76 only the carrier's line is on a harmonic, and nothing else lines up with anything. That is the recipe for struck metal: gongs, chimes, things with no clear pitch. After the card, step Ratio through its list and listen for the three that clang.
  </Way>

<Part id="wrong" title="Six ways it goes wrong, four of them to hear.">

The first fault is the one the device is built around, so it gets more room than a card.

### The ladder has no top rung

The lines of stage 4 get smaller as they go out from the carrier, and they do not stop. A sampled sound has a highest frequency it can hold, half the sample rate. A line that would stand above it folds back down (<Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" />) and lands at a frequency that belongs to no rung of the ladder. A high ratio and a large index send many lines there.

<Still
  taps="uncapped steady"
  view="spectrum"
  scale="linear"
  set="fold"
  min={-90}
  max={0}
  caption="The whole band, up to half the sample rate, at a Ratio of 14 and an Index of 8. The held tone at the index asked for runs into the right-hand edge and comes back between its own rungs. The held tone as the device plays it has fallen to almost nothing by the edge."
/>

The usual cure is to run the oscillators at several times the sample rate and filter. This device does something cheaper. It knows from the curves of stage 4 how far the lines worth worrying about reach, and when a note starts it lowers the index until they fit.

<M block t={String.raw`W = \at{room-reach}{I + 1.6\sqrt{I} + 1.8}`} />

<M t="W" /> is how many modulators from the carrier the lines above −60 dB reach, at an index of <M t="I" />. The first term is how far the frequency itself swings, and the rest is the skirt of smaller lines beyond the swing. The device gives a note the largest index whose reach stays under <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="static constexpr float kBandLimit = 0.47f;">0.47 of the sample rate</Src>.

  to="return root * root;"
  name="room"
  at={{ reach: 'out to I + 1.6' }}
/>

<Faults>
  <Fault title="High notes go dull" set="high">
    The cure has a price. The higher the note, the less room there is above it, and the less index it is given. With Index at 8 this card's note is granted <Claim id="fault-high">0.47 rad</Claim>. At 880 Hz and a Ratio of 3.5 the most the device grants is <Claim id="key-scale">2.64 rad</Claim>. A modulator that would itself stand above the limit is not run at all: at 1,760 Hz and a Ratio of 14 the note is <Claim id="modulator-silent">one sine</Claim>. If a high part must be bright, choose a lower ratio.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A note with nothing at its own pitch" set="hollow">
    Near an Index of 2.4 the carrier's line is gone, and at a ratio that is not whole nothing else stands at the note. Held on a note at 468.75 Hz, the first pair of this card's tone, at 2.5 and 4.5 times the note, stands <Claim id="fault-hollow">5.7 dB under the bare carrier</Claim>, and that is where the tone now is. Whether the note still seems to sit at its pitch is for your ear. If it has gone thin, move the index off 2.4 in either direction.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Too much index" set="harsh">
    More index is not more of a good thing. At an index of 8 the fan of stage 4 is wide open and no line leads. On the measured curves the carrier's line is then <Claim id="fault-harsh">15.3 dB under the bare carrier, and the third pair, at 10.7 dB under, is the strongest of the first four</Claim>. Listen for the note losing its pitch in the strike. Lower Index until the strike has a centre again, or shorten Decay so that the wide moment is over quickly.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The lines fold back" set="fold">
    This is the tone of the picture above, at the index asked for and with nothing held back. Listen for tones that do not belong to the note and that move the wrong way when Pitch is raised. The device does not make this sound. In an instrument that does, lower the index or the ratio on high notes.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="An offset that stands still">
    A side band can land on 0 Hz exactly: the first lower one does at a Ratio of 1. With two plain sines it arrives there with no size. With Feedback it does not, and what was a line becomes a standing offset, which is not sound and uses up room. With Ratio at 1 and Feedback at 1 the held tone stands <Claim id="dc-offset">1.3 % of its height below centre, and less than 0.1 % with Feedback at 0</Claim>. The first guard of stage 7 is there to take it out.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Clicks at the strike, steps in the tone">
    A new note that started wherever its operators happened to stand would begin differently every time, and a note cut off in mid-swing clicks. The device <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="for (int op = 0; op < 4; ++op) voice.phase[op] = 0.0f;">starts every fresh note at phase 0</Src>, and when a key is struck again while it sounds it <Src path="cpp/devices/fm-glass/fm_glass.h" at="if (held >= 0) pool_.voices[held].fast_release(0.03f);">fades the old note out over 30 ms</Src> beside the new one. A control moved under a held note is smoothed, as stage 3 says, so that the index slides.
  </Fault>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="Frequency modulation changes the note's frequency.">
    The modulator pushes the carrier's phase forward and back by the same amount, so over any whole cycle of the modulator the carrier has turned exactly as far as it would have alone. The device moves the phase, and the frequency swings only as a consequence. Stage 4 makes the tone both ways and measures how far apart they land.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="More index is the same tone, only brighter.">
    Each line rises, falls to nothing and comes back as the index grows, the carrier's included. The curves of stage 4 show where each one goes, and the answer to the question of part 1 is the first of those places.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The brightness dies because a filter closes.">
    There is no filter in the instrument apart from the guard of stage 7, which sits below any note. The index falls, and the side bands give their power back to the carrier. Stage 5 has the measurement, and the picture of stage 4 shows the same thing from right to left.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A brighter tone has more in it, so it is stronger.">
    The pushed sine has the height it had. Part 1 measured the held tone's level at every index from 0 to 8 and found it the same. Whether a tone with more lines seems louder is for your ear, and the meter does not move.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The modulator is one of the sounds in the note.">
    It never reaches the output. In the picture at the top of part 5 its row ends at the adder in front of the sine table. Only the lab lets you hear it, at stage 2.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A ratio between two on the list would give a tone in between.">
    Moving the ratio moves every line at once, each by a different distance. Between two whole ratios the lines stand on no harmonic of anything, so the tone on the way is a clang and not a blend. That is why the device's Ratio is a list of twelve places and not a dial, and why a sounding note keeps the one it was struck with.
  </Myth>
</Myths>
