Clap once in a tiled room. The clap reaches you directly, then again off the nearest wall, then off the others, then off two walls in turn, then three. Within a tenth of a second the copies arrive by the thousand, too close together to tell apart, and what is left is one tail that fades. Every bounce takes a little of the sound away, and more of its high frequencies than of its low ones, so the tail grows duller as it dies. That is reverberation: **echoes, dense, dying away, the highs first**.

A delay line with some of its output fed back to its input (lesson 10) makes echoes that die away, and it is not a reverb. Its echoes come one per trip round the delay and never get any denser. Worse, echoes evenly spaced in time are peaks evenly spaced in frequency: a 5 ms delay with feedback rings at 200 Hz and at every multiple of it. A reverb needs echoes that multiply, at times that never line up, with no frequency favoured.

The engine's plate gets there in two moves. First the sound goes through four *all-pass delays* in a row. Each turns one sample into a train of echoes and leaves the level of every frequency exactly as it was (lesson 12 has the all-pass as a filter), and four of them turn one sample into a burst. Then the burst enters the *tank*: two loops, each feeding the other, each with two more all-passes, two long delays and a filter that dulls the sound a little on every pass. The tank is what rings. Nothing is heard from the end of it, because a loop has no end: the two outputs are read from seven places each along its delays.

These are the stages in the order the signal meets them. The source is a click, one sample once a second, which shows the whole of what a reverb does. Press Play, then Listen on any stage.

## Stage by stage

### 1. One channel, a gap, and a filter left open

The plate takes one channel in. <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb_device.cpp">The device</Src> adds left and right and halves the sum, so the reverb is the same wherever a sound sat between the speakers; the width of what comes out is made later, by where the outputs are read. One thing follows from the sum: a sound that is opposite in the two channels adds up to nothing and <Claim id="one-channel">gets no reverb at all</Claim>.

Then comes the *pre-delay*, a plain delay line of up to 250 ms. In a room the first reflection arrives a little after the direct sound, and later the larger the room. A gap also keeps the front of a note clear of its own tail. The line reads after it writes, so a length of 1 is the sample just written: at 20 ms, which is 960 samples, a sample comes out <Claim id="predelay-one-less">959 samples later</Claim>, and at 0 ms it is not delayed at all.

Last before the all-passes is a one-pole low-pass, the *bandwidth* filter:

<M block t={String.raw`y[n] = (1 - a)\,x[n] + a\,y[n-1]`} />

Each output is mostly the new input and a little of the last output; the larger <M t="a" />, the more of the past is kept and the duller the result. In the code it is <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h">`OnePoleLowpass`</Src>. Here <M t="a" /> is <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb.h">fixed at 0.0005</Src>, which leaves the filter all but open: it takes <Claim id="bandwidth-loss">0.008 dB</Claim> off a tone at 20 kHz. This is the place where a plate can be told to take in less treble, and this device has no control for it. The same filter with a coefficient that matters is the damping of stage 6.

### 2. The all-pass: echoes that leave the spectrum alone

Start from the delay with feedback of the opening, <M t={String.raw`y[n] = x[n] + g\,y[n-M]`} />. One sample in gives 1, then <M t="g" />, then <M t="g^2" />, one every <M t="M" /> samples. Against frequency it is a comb: where a whole number of cycles fits the delay the echoes add, to <M t={String.raw`1/(1-g)`} />, and half way between they cancel, to <M t={String.raw`1/(1+g)`} />. With the first input all-pass's own numbers, 229 samples and a gain of 0.75, that is <Claim id="comb-peaks">12 dB up and 4.9 dB down, with a peak every 210 Hz</Claim>.

The all-pass delay is the same loop with one line added. Some of the input goes straight to the output, upside down:

<M block t={String.raw`w[n] = x[n] + g\,w[n-M] \qquad\qquad y[n] = w[n-M] - g\,w[n]`} />

Here <M t="w" /> is what is written into the delay line, the input plus <M t="g" /> times what the line gives back. The output is what the line gives back, less <M t="g" /> times what was just written. In <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb.h">`Allpass::process`</Src> these are `delayed`, `feed` and the returned `delayed - gain * feed`, in three lines. One sample in comes out as <M t="-g" /> at once, then as <M t={String.raw`(1-g^2)\,g^{k-1}`} /> after <M t="k" /> trips: <Claim id="allpass-echoes">−0.75, then 0.44, 0.33, 0.25, each 229 samples after the last</Claim>.

That first sample, upside down, is the whole trick. Written as a ratio of what comes out to what goes in at each frequency, the all-pass is

<M block t={String.raw`H(z) = \frac{z^{-M} - g}{1 - g\,z^{-M}}`} />

and for a steady tone, where <M t={String.raw`z^{-M}`} /> is a point on the unit circle, the top and the bottom have the same size: the top is the bottom reflected and turned. So <M t={String.raw`|H| = 1`} /> at every frequency. The echoes are still there, and their energy adds up to that of the one sample that went in, <M t={String.raw`g^2 + (1-g^2)^2(1 + g^2 + g^4 + \dots) = 1`} />. What changes is when each frequency arrives, which is exactly what a reverb wants to change.

### 3. Four in a row: one sample becomes a burst

One all-pass makes a train of echoes, evenly spaced, and that is still too regular. The input diffuser is four in series, so each echo of the first sets off a whole train in the second, each of those a train in the third, and so on. The lengths were chosen at a sample rate of 29,761 Hz; <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb.cpp">`init`</Src> multiplies each by the running rate over that one and rounds. At 48 kHz that makes <Claim id="input-lengths">229, 173, 611 and 447 samples</Claim>:

| All-pass | At 29,761 Hz | At 48 kHz | In time | Gain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 142 | 229 | 4.8 ms | 0.75 |
| 2 | 107 | 173 | 3.6 ms | 0.75 |
| 3 | 379 | 611 | 12.7 ms | 0.625 |
| 4 | 277 | 447 | 9.3 ms | 0.625 |

No two of these lengths share a factor, and that matters. After the first two all-passes there is an echo at every time that is some number of 229s plus some number of 173s. Were the lengths 200 and 300, every such sum would be a multiple of 100: the echoes would pile onto the same few instants, evenly spaced, and an even spacing of 100 samples is a pitch again, at 480 Hz. With no factor in common, two different sums first meet after 229 × 173 = 39,617 samples, 0.83 s, when the echoes are long gone. Until then every echo has an instant to itself.

</Curve>

To put a number on it, the lab counts echoes: samples larger than both their neighbours and within 60 dB of the largest in the same 10 ms. In the 10 ms that start 30 ms after the sample went in there are <Claim id="density-stages">2 echoes after one all-pass, 23 after two, 47 after three and 96 after four</Claim>. After the fourth the count climbs to <Claim id="density-four">186 in 10 ms</Claim>.

And the diffuser has still changed no level: all four together move no frequency by more than <Claim id="chain-flat">0.00001 dB</Claim>.

### 4. The tank: two halves, each feeding the other

The burst now goes into both halves of the tank at once. Each half is the same chain, with its own lengths, and <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb.cpp">`process`</Src> writes it out line by line:

1. Add the burst to what has just left the *other* half, times the decay.
2. An all-pass with a gain of −0.7. Its delay is the one that moves (stage 7).
3. A long delay.
4. The damping low-pass (stage 6), then times the decay again.
5. An all-pass with a gain of 0.5.
6. A second long delay. What leaves it crosses to the other half's step 1.

So the sound goes left half, right half, left half: a figure of eight, with no way out. The <Claim id="tank-lengths">eight lengths</Claim> at 48 kHz:

| In samples | Left half | Right half |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moving all-pass | 1,083.8 (22.6 ms) | 1,464.5 (30.5 ms) |
| First delay | 7,182 (149.6 ms) | 6,801 (141.7 ms) |
| All-pass | 2,903 (60.5 ms) | 4,284 (89.3 ms) |
| Second delay | 6,000 (125.0 ms) | 5,101 (106.3 ms) |

The two halves are deliberately unlike, so the loop does not repeat itself half way round. Once round both takes <Claim id="trip">0.725 s</Claim>, which is far too long to hear as a pitch, and the four all-passes inside the loop smear the burst again on every trip. What entered the right half first reaches the left <Claim id="crossing">11,901 samples later, at 248 ms</Claim>: the right half's two delays and one sample more, because the left half is worked out first and so takes what the right half left on the sample before. (The plain delays, like the pre-delay, read after they write, so each delays by one sample less than its length: <Claim id="one-less">7,181 samples for a length of 7,182</Claim>. The all-passes read first and delay by their full length.)

The outputs are sums of what the delay lines hold at seven places each. The left output reads four places in the right half and three in the left, the right output the mirror of that, with fixed signs and a gain of 0.6 on each (the list at the end of `process`). Reading at many places adds seven delayed copies of an already dense signal, and reading different places for each side makes two outputs out of one input. Left first sounds <Claim id="first-out">428 samples after the pre-delay and right 568, which are 8.9 and 11.8 ms</Claim>.

### 5. Decay: the gain round the loop

Nothing in the tank loses level except by being told to. The all-passes pass everything, the delays only wait. The loss is the decay control <M t="d" />, a plain multiplication, met twice in each half and so four times in one trip of length <M t="T" />. Each trip the level falls by <M t={String.raw`-20\log_{10} d^4`} /> decibels, and the time to fall 60 dB is as many trips as that takes:

<M block t={String.raw`T_{60} = \frac{60}{-20\log_{10} d^{4}}\;T = \frac{-3\,T}{4\log_{10} d}`} />

The decay time of a reverb is by convention this time to fall 60 dB, to a thousandth of the level. Note what the control is: the gain, not the time. Equal steps of the knob are not equal steps of time, and the time runs away as <M t="d" /> nears 1. The device stops at 0.9999 (<Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb.cpp">`set_decay`</Src>), where the formula gives three and a half hours. The lab's control stops at 0.9 so that its eight seconds of listening can still measure something.

### 6. Damping: the highs die first

In a room the highs go first, and in the tank they go first because step 4 of each half is the one-pole low-pass of stage 1, with the damping control as its coefficient <M t="a" />. Its gain at a frequency <M t="f" />, with <M t={String.raw`\omega = 2\pi f / f_s`} />, is

<M block t={String.raw`|H(f)| = \frac{1 - a}{\sqrt{1 - 2a\cos\omega + a^{2}}}`} />

which is 1 at the bottom of the spectrum and falls towards <M t={String.raw`(1-a)/(1+a)`} /> at the top. At a damping of 0.3 it is <Claim id="damping-cutoff">3 dB down at 10.6 kHz, and at 0.7 at 2.75 kHz</Claim>. One pass through such a filter is a mild thing. But the sound in the tank passes it twice a trip, trip after trip, so the gain round the loop becomes <M t={String.raw`d^{4}\,|H(f)|^{2}`} /> and every frequency gets a decay time of its own:

<M block t={String.raw`T_{60}(f) = \frac{-3\,T}{\log_{10}\bigl(d^{4}\,|H(f)|^{2}\bigr)}`} />

At a decay of 0.5 and a damping of 0.7 that gives <Claim id="damping-formula">1.8 s at 500 Hz and 1.2 s at 5 kHz</Claim>. The lab measures both, by putting the response through two band-pass filters and reading a decay curve from each.

### 7. The delays that move

A tank of fixed delays is a fixed pattern of reinforcement and cancellation. A steady tone held into it comes out at a steady level, which the lab finds <Claim id="still-steady">constant within 0.1 dB</Claim>, but what level depends on exactly where the tone falls in the pattern: with the delays still, a tone at 441 Hz comes out <Claim id="pitch-luck">3.5 dB louder than one at 440 Hz</Claim>. Each note of a tune has its own luck, and has it every time.

So the device never lets the pattern stand. The delay of each half's first all-pass is swept by a slow sine:

<M block t={String.raw`M(t) = M_0 + \tfrac12\,e\,\bigl(1 + \sin 2\pi f t\bigr)`} />

By the constants at the top of <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb.cpp">the plate's code</Src>, the excursion <M t="e" /> is 12 samples at the reference rate, so each delay grows by up to <Claim id="wobble-range">19.35 samples</Claim> and shrinks back, the left once a second and the right at 0.95 Hz, starting a quarter turn ahead; the two come back into step only every 20 seconds. Since the delay is no longer a whole number of samples, the all-pass reads between two samples by a straight line (`read_interpolated`, the linear interpolation of lesson 10). With the delays moving, the same 440 Hz tone drifts through peaks and troughs as the pattern slides under it: its level <Claim id="moving-swings">swings over more than 20 dB</Claim>. No note keeps its luck.

## What goes wrong

**A pitch in the tail.** Any regularity in the echoes is a comb, and a comb is a pitch: two delays that share a factor, an all-pass chain too short to break a click up, a loop short enough to hear. The engine's answers are the ones above: lengths with nothing in common, four all-passes before the loop and four inside it, a trip of 0.725 s, and the movement.

**Highs lost that nobody asked for.** Reading between two samples by a straight line is itself a gentle low-pass, and the moving all-passes do it on every pass, still or moving (their lengths at rest, 1,083.8 and 1,464.5, are not whole numbers). So even with the damping at 0 the top of the spectrum goes a little early: 16 kHz falls 60 dB in <Claim id="still-loses-highs">1.74 s where 500 Hz takes 1.98 s</Claim>, both read from the picture in stage 6.

**A tail that never quite ends.** A loop that multiplies by less than 1 gets smaller for ever and never reaches zero, and numbers that small are slow to compute with. Every write to a delay line in <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb.h">the plate</Src> goes through <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h">`flush_denormal`</Src>, which turns anything under 10⁻¹⁵ into an exact zero, so a tail does end. When the plate has put out nothing but zeros for a second with nothing coming in, <Src path="cpp/devices/plate-reverb/plate_reverb_device.h">the device</Src> stops running it and only keeps the two sweeps turning.

**Steps when a control moves.** The device stores mix, decay, damping and pre-delay the moment they are set, with no ramp between the old value and the new. A change of pre-delay moves the place the line is read from, and what was there need not be anything like what was being read.

**Dry and wet.** The mix is a straight crossfade, the dry signal times one minus the mix plus the plate times the mix. With the mix at 0 the output is <Claim id="mix-zero">the input, sample for sample</Claim>. The dry signal keeps its two channels; only the plate's input is summed to one.

## What to try

- Clicks, **decay** at 0. This is one pass with no loop at all. Listen along the stages from the first all-pass to the fourth, then bring the decay up.
- Clicks, **pre-delay** from 0 to 120 ms, and stage 1's figure. The answer comes away from the click.
- Drums, **damping** from 0 to 0.8. Watch the top of stage 6's picture go dark and the red line of the figure under it steepen.
- Guitar chords, **decay** at 0.9. Each chord is still ringing when the next one is played.
- The sine, the tank's delays **Still** and then **Moving**, and stage 7's figure.
- **Mix** at 0.35, the device's own default, with the drums: the plate under the sound, not in place of it.
