<Part id="why" title="A chord is one tangled line. The notes are in it somewhere.">

Here is a guitar chord, strummed once and left to ring. Four strings are sounding, tuned to <Src path="site/labs/bench.cpp" at="{110.00f, 164.81f, 220.00f, 261.63f},">110, 165, 220 and 262 Hz</Src>. An ear hears four notes. This is what the computer holds.

A sound in a computer is a list of samples: one height for each instant. The list holds everything there is to know about the chord, and it answers none of a player's questions. It does not say which notes these are, or how loud each one is.

Suppose the job is to take the two upper strings out and leave the two lower ones. Nothing that is done to a sample can do it. Each sample is all four strings added together, so whatever happens to the sample happens to all four. Halve every sample and the whole chord is half as loud. No sample belongs to the top string.

The Fourier transform writes the same stretch of sound as a different list: how much of each frequency it holds. An entry of that list is called a bin.

  taps="magnitude"
  view="spectrum"

  min={-80}
  max={-10}
  caption="The same stretch as a level for each bin, up to 1 kHz. Notice the four peaks furthest to the left: the four strings. The peaks to their right are the strings' harmonics."
/>

The four lowest peaks stand in the bins at <Claim id="chord-strings">105 Hz, 164 Hz, 223 Hz and 258 Hz</Claim>. Those are the bins nearest the four strings. A bin stands at a fixed frequency, and a string rarely lands exactly on one. Part 5 says where the bins stand and why.

Neither list says more than the other. The stretch in these pictures is 4,096 samples long. The transform turns them into 4,096 numbers, and the inverse transform turns those numbers back into the samples. What differs is what is easy. In the second list a string is a few numbers standing side by side, and they can be reached.

That is the use of the transform. It moves a sound to a place where its notes stand apart, something is done there, and the sound is brought back.

This lesson is about one such stretch, which from here on is called a frame. It shows what a bin is, why a frame is faded at both ends before it is transformed, and why the way back returns the samples that went in. <Lesson id="short-time-fourier" cap /> then slides the frame along a sound.

The opening picture of this page is the chord going in, and the chord rebuilt from the 48 lowest bins of each frame, which is where "Bins kept" starts.

</Part>

<Part id="picture" title="Every bin is an arrow that turns. Tip to tail, the arrows draw the frame.">

A bin holds two things about one sine: how large it is, and where in its swing it stands at the frame's first sample. Draw that as an arrow. Its length is the size, and its direction is the starting place.

Now let the arrow turn at a steady speed while the frame goes by. The arrow of bin 1 goes round once in the frame, the arrow of bin 2 twice, the arrow of bin 30 thirty times. The height of an arrow's tip, sample by sample, is that bin's sine.

To add the sines, lay the arrows tip to tail. The height of the last tip is the sum of all their heights, and that sum is the frame's sample.

<Part id="uses" title="Sounds that hold a pitch fit in a few bins. Hits and noise need nearly all of them.">

By itself the transform changes nothing. It is a place to work. A meter draws the bins as a spectrum. A device changes them and transforms back: Spectral Blur, the engine's device on this page, holds on to bins from one frame to the next so that a sound hangs in the air. Either works better the fewer bins a sound needs.

The lab puts a number on that. It sorts a frame's bins from the largest to the smallest and counts how many it takes to hold nine tenths of the frame's energy. Each card shows one frame's bins and gives the count. Its button plays the loop rebuilt from the bins the card keeps.

<Part id="build" title="Four pieces: one probe, a bank of probes, the window, the way back.">

The transform is put together here in the lab's own code, the slow way: plain sums, one after another, with nothing clever in them. It works on one frame, the one that begins at "Frame starts at". Each step adds one small function, and the last step is held to the engine's fast transform.

A frame is not a sound until something plays it. "Hear it so far" plays what the pieces so far make of the one frame, round and round with no gap. That is not a convenience of the page. It is how the transform itself takes a frame: as one turn of something that repeats for ever. The third step depends on it.

<Build>
  <Step title="One probe sine" controls="position size compare">
    To ask how much of one frequency a frame holds, make a sine of that frequency and multiply the frame by it, sample by sample. Then add the products up. Where the frame swings with the probe, the two are above the line together and below it together, every product is positive, and the sum grows. Where the frame holds nothing of that frequency, the products come out positive and negative in equal measure and cancel.

    One sine is not quite enough. If the frame's wave crosses the line where the probe is at its top, the products cancel although the frequency is plainly there. So a probe is a pair: a cosine, and a sine, which is the same wave a quarter of a cycle later and catches what the cosine misses. The two sums together say how much there is and where in its swing it stands.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/fourier-transform.cpp"
      from="// One probe sine: a cosine and a sine that turn k times in the frame, each"
      to="found_sin[k] = with_sin;"
      mark={['with_cos += frame[n] * turn_cos[at];', 'with_sin += frame[n] * turn_sin[at];']}
      name="probe"
    />

    The tables `turn_cos` and `turn_sin` hold one turn of each wave in as many steps as the frame has samples. A probe that turns `k` times in the frame walks them `k` steps a sample. The probe stands at the bin nearest "Compare with", and the sound of this step is its answer: a cosine and a sine as tall as the two sums say, added. On the chord that is one pure tone, as much of the lowest string as the frame holds.

  <Step title="A bank of probes" controls="position size compare">
    One probe answers for one frequency. To find everything in the frame, ask at every frequency that turns a whole number of times in it: no turns, one, two, and so on up to half a turn a sample. Beyond that a sampled sine is the mirror of a slower one and says nothing new. For a frame of N samples that is N/2 + 1 probes, and their answers are the bins.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/fourier-transform.cpp"

      to="for (int k = 0; k <= size / 2; ++k) probe(k);"
      mark="for (int k = 0; k <= size / 2; ++k) probe(k);"
      name="bank"
    />

These slow sums land on the bins of the engine's fast transform, <Claim id="built-bins">more than 130 dB under the largest of them</Claim>:

    The price is the work. Each of the 2,049 probes of the longest frame runs over all 4,096 samples: 8.4 million steps for one frame, with two multiplications in each. Part 5 shows how the engine gets the same bins with far fewer.

    The bank also shows a fault. Choose the sine in the row of sounds: one frequency and nothing else. The bank finds something in every bin, and ten bins above its peak the sine still reads <Claim id="built-leak-bare">28 dB under it</Claim>. The sine does not turn a whole number of times in the frame. No probe matches it, so every probe matches it a little.

    </Lanes>

  </Step>
  <Step title="The window" controls="position size compare">
    The leak comes from the two ends of the frame. Played round and round, the frame's last sample is followed by its first, and the wave jumps there. A jump is in no single sine. It takes a little of every frequency to draw one.

    So take the ends away. Before any probe sees the frame, multiply it by a curve that starts at nothing, rises to 1 in the middle and falls back to nothing. The curve used here is the Hann window: one turn of the slowest probe's cosine, turned over and lifted.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/fourier-transform.cpp"
      from="// The window: the frame fades in from nothing and out to nothing before"
      to="for (int n = 0; n < size; ++n) frame[n] *= static_cast<float>(0.5 - 0.5 * turn_cos[n]);"
      mark="for (int n = 0; n < size; ++n) frame[n] *= static_cast<float>(0.5 - 0.5 * turn_cos[n]);"
      name="hann"
    />

    A frame that begins and ends at nothing joins itself with no jump. On the sine, the leak ten bins above the peak falls to <Claim id="built-leak-windowed">70 dB under it</Claim>. Two prices are paid. A peak is now three bins wide where it could have been one, and the ends of the frame count for very little. Part 5 measures the first and part 7 the second.

  <Step title="The way back" controls="position size compare keep">
    Each probe's answer is a wave: its cosine and its sine, as tall as the two sums say. A probe between the first and the last answers for its mirror as well, so it counts twice, and the whole is divided by the number of samples.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/fourier-transform.cpp"

      to="answers[n] += share * (found_cos[k] * turn_cos[at] + found_sin[k] * turn_sin[at]);"
      mark="answers[n] += share * (found_cos[k] * turn_cos[at] + found_sin[k] * turn_sin[at]);"
      name="answer"
    />

    The way back is to add the answers up, and nothing more.

<StageBlock tap="real" n={4} title="All the bins at once">
  Written out as in stage 3, a frame of 4,096 costs 16.8 million multiplications. The kit gets the same bins in 24,576 steps of the kind listed here, with four multiplications in each. That is about 170 times less work, and it is what lets a device work on bins while the sound plays.

  <Curve taps="real:alt imag:alt2" xTitle="hertz" xMax={1000} stems height={220}>
    The two numbers of every bin up to 1 kHz, as the kit leaves them. Try "Frame starts at" a
    little later, and notice both sets of stalks change while the peaks stay at the same
    frequencies.
  </Curve>

  The kit's forward transform divides by nothing, so its numbers grow with the frame. A sine of amplitude 0.5 exactly on bin 100 of the longest frame gives that bin a size of <Claim id="scaling">1,024</Claim>, which is half the amplitude times N, and leaves every other bin empty. The half is there because the spectrum of a real signal is a mirror: bin <M t="N-k" /> holds the same size as bin <M t="k" /> with the opposite angle, so each sine's size is shared between two bins. That is why every picture here stops at bin N/2.

  A real frame needs only half the work for the same reason. <Src path="cpp/kit/fft.h" at="void forward_real(const Source& sample, float* out) const {">`forward_real`</Src> works out bins 0 to N/2 alone, and it is what the engine's devices call. Its bins and the full transform's agree to <Claim id="real-same">more than 130 dB under the largest</Claim>.

  <Check id="realSame" />

  The saving in work rests on one observation. Take the even-numbered samples of the frame as one signal and the odd-numbered ones as another. Each is half as long. Call their transforms <M t="E" /> and <M t="O" />, and let <M t={String.raw`W = e^{-j 2\pi / N}`} /> stand for one step of the turning. Then the transform of the whole frame comes from the two halves:

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{gathered} \at{butterfly-sum}{X[k] = E[k] + W^k\,O[k]} \\[6pt] \at{butterfly-difference}{X[k + \tfrac{N}{2}] = E[k] - W^k\,O[k]} \end{gathered}`}
  />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    Read it against the listing below. Two bins come from two bins with a single multiplication: `xr` and `xi` are the two parts of <M t={String.raw`W^k O[k]`} />, the element at `b` becomes the difference and the one at `a` the sum. The halves are split the same way, and theirs again, down to single samples, which are their own transforms. That is why N must be a power of two. It leaves <M t={String.raw`\log_2 N`} /> passes of <M t="N/2" /> such steps: twelve passes of 2,048 for a frame of 4,096.

  The kit runs this from the bottom up. All that halving sorts the samples by the bits of their index read backwards, so `init` fills a table of those indices, and `transform` begins by swapping each sample into its place. Then the loop in the listing joins neighbouring transforms into ones twice as long until one is left. One table of cosines and one of sines serve every pass, read at a `stride`. The inverse is the same loop with the sign of the sine turned round.

  </Fragment>

  The bins are evenly spaced from nothing up to half the sample rate. At the lab's four frame lengths they are <Claim id="bin-spacings">750 Hz, 187.5 Hz, 46.9 Hz and 11.7 Hz</Claim> apart. Set that beside the lengths of stage 1 and the whole trade is one line:

  <M block t={String.raw`\Delta f \cdot T = \frac{f_s}{N}\cdot\frac{N}{f_s} = 1`} />

<Part id="ways" title="Six things to do with a frame's bins.">

The first five cards set the page and play. The last is a habit, and plays nothing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Cut with a wall" tag="keep the lowest bins" set="wall">
    Set every bin above a line to nothing and transform back. The result is a low-pass filter far steeper than one made of a few delays, as stage 8 of part 5 measures. The card plays the mix from its 48 lowest bins. Listen for the bass and the weight of the kick, with everything bright gone.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Hear what was taken out" tag="the difference" set="rest">
    Whatever a change to the bins removes can be heard by taking the result away from the sound that went in. The card plays the mix less its 48 lowest bins: everything the wall stopped. The plain sound, the changed sound and the difference between them are the quickest way to judge any change made to bins. If the difference holds something that was wanted, the cut is in the wrong place.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Give the hits their time back" tag="a short frame" set="inplace">
    The same cut can be made in frames of 256, where it takes 3 bins to reach as high. Each frame is a sixteenth as long, so whatever the cut does to a hit stays close to the hit. The edge of the filter is far softer, and part 7 measures both sides of that bargain. The card plays the drums.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Read one note's level" tag="a single probe" set="second">
    The transform answers for every bin at once. For one frequency a single probe will do, and it need not stand on a bin. With "Compare with" on the chord's second string, the frame holds <Claim id="second-string">−26.1 dB of 165 Hz, and −68.5 dB of 140 Hz</Claim>, a frequency between two strings. The card plays the plain chord and sets the probe. Stage 3 of part 5 shows its sums.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Do it to every frame" tag="a device" set="through">
    One frame is a moment of a sound. A device takes a new frame every few milliseconds, each overlapping the last, and adds the results. The card plays the chord through Spectral Blur with nothing altered: it has been to bins and back for every frame, and it is still the chord. How the frames are laid over one another, and altered on the way, is the subject of <Lesson id="short-time-fourier" />.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Pick the frame for the question" tag="Frame length">
    A long frame answers which notes, and a short one answers when. For a chord or a bass line take the longest frame. For drums take a short one. Stage 5 of part 5 is the place to try both on one sound.
  </Way>

<Part id="wrong" title="Five faults, and what to do about each.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="The cut leaks" set="bare">
    With no window, a sine that does not fit the frame spills into every bin, the ones a cut keeps among them. With Window at None and 48 bins kept, <Claim id="cut-plain">a 1 kHz sine, far above the cut, comes through only 29 dB down</Claim>. The card plays the rising sine that way. Once the sine has climbed past the cut it should be gone. Over the last second of the loop it is <Claim id="sweep-bare">40 dB under itself with no window, where the Hann window holds it more than 90 dB under</Claim>. Listen for a buzz that carries on after the tone has left. What to do: window the frame. After the card, set Window to Hann and the buzz goes.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A long frame throws a hit ahead of itself" set="early">
    A bin holds one size and one angle for the whole frame, so a change to a bin is a change to the whole length of the frame. Cut at the same place in frames of 4,096, and a single click begins to sound <Claim id="pre-echo">73 ms before it happens, 43 dB under its peak between 20 ms and 40 ms ahead</Claim>. The card plays the clicks that way. Listen for a soft breath before each one. What to do: use a shorter frame. <Hear set="short" label="Hear the same cut in frames of 256" />, where nothing arrives <Claim id="pre-echo-short">more than 4 ms early</Claim>.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A short frame cannot tell notes apart" set="blunt">
    The cure for the last fault has its own price. In frames of 256 the bins are so far apart that the whole chord of part 1 lies in the first two. Keep those two, and <Claim id="blunt">the string at 220 Hz comes through less than 2 dB down and the one at 262 Hz less than 5 dB down</Claim>, where the long frame took both out. The card plays the chord cut that way, and it is the whole chord, a little dull. What to do: choose the frame for the sound. No length does both jobs.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The ends of a frame are deaf">
    The window is nothing at the frame's first sample and close to nothing beside it. A click that falls exactly there is <Claim id="edge-click">multiplied away altogether</Claim>: the bins of that frame are silence. What to do: overlap the frames, so that what one frame barely sees stands in the middle of another. Every device does. Spectral Blur starts a new frame a quarter of a frame after the last.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="It is late">
    A frame cannot be transformed until its last sample has arrived, so anything that works on bins answers at least a frame late. Stage 10 of part 5 measures Spectral Blur's delay. No faster machine shortens it. What to do: use a shorter frame where the delay matters, and otherwise let the host shift the other tracks to meet it. The device states its delay for that purpose, and delays its own plain signal by the same amount so that the two stay in step.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="The transform is an approximation of the sound.">
    It is another list of the same length, and the way back returns the samples to the last digits a float holds. Step 4 of the build measures it for the slow sums, and stage 7 of part 5 for the kit. Every loss on this page comes from bins that were thrown away on purpose.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A spectrum shows the frequencies in the sound.">
    It shows the bins of one frame, which stand at fixed places. The chord's lowest string shows in the nearest bin and not at its own frequency, as part 1 found, and a sine between two bins shows in all of them, as stage 2 of part 5 measures. Under the window even a sine exactly on a bin is three bins wide.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A longer frame gives a better spectrum.">
    It gives bins that are closer together, and it pays for them in time, one for one. Stage 5 of part 5 has the equation. The saw's harmonics come apart only in the long frame, and the click of part 7 is thrown furthest ahead of itself by the same long frame.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The window is a fade to stop clicks at the ends.">
    Nothing is played at the ends: the frame goes to the probes, not to a speaker. The window is there for the bins. Steps 2 and 3 of the build show the leak of a frame cut square, and what the fade does to it.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The levels are the sound, and the angles are a detail.">
    The angles are half of the numbers. Two frames with the same levels and different angles are different samples: stage 3 of part 5 cuts one sine at two moments and gets the same level with the angle a quarter of a turn on.
