<Part id="why" title="A delay can only say the same thing again.">

A guitar line goes into a delay, and a quarter of a second later the delay answers. The answer is the line: the same notes, at the same pitch, the same way round. Listen to the line by itself, <Hear set="dry" label="as it was played" />, and then with its answer under it, <Hear set="echo" label="with one repeat" />.

<Still
  taps="in grains"
  set="echo"
  from={0.5}
  span={2}
  caption="Two seconds of the line going in, and the repeat alone. Every note of the first stands again in the second, a quarter of a second to its right and no different."
/>

That is an echo, and <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" /> is about how one is made: a ring of memory that is always being written, and a read head that follows the write head round it at a fixed distance. Whatever passes under the write head passes under the read head a little later, unchanged.

So here is the question this lesson answers. How do you make what comes back something other than what went in (an octave higher, or backwards, or smeared into a cloud) and still have it come back on time?

### The obvious answer fails

Pitch is speed. A recording played at twice its speed sounds an octave higher, so the plain idea is to move the read head round the ring twice as fast.

It cannot keep that up. The write head lays down one sample for every sample of time that goes by. A read head that takes two samples in that time gains one on the write head, every sample. Start it a quarter of a second behind, and a quarter of a second later it has caught the write head and there is nothing left to read. Slow it down instead and it falls further and further behind, until the ring comes round and writes over what it was about to read. Turn it round and it runs away backwards at twice the speed of time. A read head can hold any speed but the recording's own only for a little while.

### So read for a little while

That is the way out. Read a short piece of the ring at whatever speed you like, stop well before the write head, and start again from further back. <Lesson id="pitch-shifting" cap /> does exactly this with two read heads that take turns. This lesson does it with as many as it likes, and lets each one go its own way.

A piece that starts and stops has two ends, though. Here is one piece as a read head lifts it out of the ring: a little over a tenth of a second of the guitar line, with nothing done to it.

Each end is a jump between silence and sound in full swing, and a jump is a click. A click is not a note: it has some of every frequency in it. The next picture shows that for the plainest sound there is, one steady tone. Cut a piece out of it and the piece no longer holds one frequency. <Claim id="click">A piece cut from a 440 Hz tone averages 65 dB under its own peak between 8 and 12 kHz</Claim>, where the tone itself has nothing at all.

### Fade each piece in and out

The cure is older than computers: do not switch a sound on, fade it in. Multiply the piece by a curve that starts at nothing, rises smoothly to one and falls smoothly back. The curve is called a window, and a piece of sound under a window is called a _grain_.

<Still
  taps="piece bell:ctl grain:out"
  view="curve"
  caption="The same piece of the line, the window drawn at the piece's height, and the grain: the piece multiplied by the window, sample by sample. Notice that the grain starts and ends at nothing, however the piece does."
/>

<Claim id="windowed">Under the window the piece of the tone leaves under −135 dB a bin between 8 and 12 kHz</Claim>: nothing that the measurement can find. Here are the same pieces as before, each under its window: <Hear set="windowed" label="the pieces as grains" />.

One grain alone is a blip, too short to be a note or a word and long enough to keep the colour of what it was cut from. Each has four things of its own: where on the ring it reads, how long it lasts, how fast it reads, and when it starts. Granular sound is what happens when a great many are played, each started a little after the last. Give them all the same place and the recording's own speed, and they add back up to the echo this part began with. Give each its own, and the echo becomes something the player never played.

<Part id="picture" title="Each grain is a bead: across is when it sounds, down is how old the sound is.">

One picture holds nearly everything a grain delay does. Along the top is what the device has recorded. Under it, on the same clock, every grain is a bead. Where a bead lies from side to side is when the grain sounds. How far down it lies is how far behind the write point it is reading: how old the sound is. A bead is as long as its grain lasts and as thick as its window is open, so it swells in the middle and comes to a point at each end.

One grain is picked out and joined to the piece of the recording it reads. The piece is always to the left of the bead, because that sound was recorded earlier.

  Then try Pitch up, and notice every bead tilt towards the write point while its middle stays on
  the dashed line of Time.
</GrainCloud>

Check this against the picture. Leave the three controls where the page starts them and read the key under the figure. It counts <Claim id="open-count">33 grains in the loop, one every 120 ms, each reading 250 ms behind the write point</Claim>. Size is the length of one of those steps and Density is 1, so each grain starts as the one before it ends, and the beads lie end to end in a row on the dashed line.

Now the three controls. Size is how long a grain lasts, and makes the beads longer or shorter. Density is how many grains sound at once: it packs more beads into the same time by starting each one before the last has finished. Pitch is how fast a grain reads. At 0 a grain reads the ring exactly as fast as the ring is written, stays the same distance behind, and its bead lies level. Raise Pitch and the grain gains on the write point, so the bead climbs. Lower it and the bead falls away.

Notice what the picture does not show: how loud the result is, and what the beads add up to where they overlap. Those are the business of part 5.

</Part>

<Part id="build" title="Start with a ring that remembers. Add one piece. Listen. Add the next.">

The quickest way to understand a grain delay is to make one. This part begins with a ring and one read head, which is a plain delay, and adds five pieces to it, one at a time. After each piece the pictures are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

The ring, the pool that plays the grains and the dice are the engine's kit, the same classes the device is made of. What the build writes for itself is everything that decides where a grain goes.

    One grain at a time leaves a gap at every joint. Start each grain before the last has finished and the gaps close. Density is how many grains sound at once: the wait between two starts becomes Size divided by Density.

    Windows that overlap add up, though, so eight grains at once would be far louder than one. The piece that comes with Density is a gain. It works out how much power that many overlapping windows carry, and divides it out. There are two answers, because grains that read the same sound add like waves in step, and grains that read unrelated sound add like a crowd. The listing has both, and stage 7 of part 5 reads them term by term.

    A grain's speed is the last thing of its own. Pitch sets it in semitones, twelve to a doubling. Pitch Spray moves each grain's pitch by a random amount, up to an octave either way at its fullest. Reverse is the share of grains that read backwards.

    Speed changes where a grain must start. A grain that reads faster than the ring is written covers more ring than it lasts, so it starts further back by half the extra, and its middle still reads the sound that is Time old. A grain read backwards starts nearer the write point and runs away from it. Neither is allowed within four samples of the write point, and that puts a floor under Time, which part 7 comes back to.

    Speed also changes what may be recorded. Reading at twice the speed doubles every frequency on the ring, and anything that would land above half the sample rate folds back down as a wrong note (<Lesson id="samples-and-aliasing" /> shows how). So from this step on, what goes onto the ring is low-passed first, to what the highest pitch in use can carry.

<StageBlock tap="recorded" n={1} title="The ring: what is recorded">
  The device never plays its input late. It writes onto a ring, and everything that comes out is read from the ring by grains. So the first stage is what gets written. The ring is the delay line of <Lesson id="delay-and-echo" />, long enough that no grain can reach its far side.

  Three things are done to the sound on its way onto the ring. What the grains made is added to it, times Feedback, which stage 10 comes back to. The sum is kept inside full scale. And it is low-passed, which matters only when Pitch is up. A grain that reads at twice the speed doubles every frequency it reads, so nothing may be on the ring that would be doubled past half the sample rate. The device finds the fastest upward speed that Pitch and Pitch Spray can ask for and sets the corner of the low-pass to match.

<StageBlock tap="window" n={3} title="The window">
  Part 1 showed why a piece needs a window. This is the one the kit uses. It rises along half a cosine wave, may hold flat in the middle, and falls the way it rose. One number, Shape, sets how much of the grain is fade.

  <Controls ids="shape" />

  <Curve taps="window" xTitle="ms into the grain" yTitle="window">
    The window of the grain looked at. Try Shape down from 1, and notice the bell grow a flat top
    while its two ends get steeper.
  </Curve>

  The rise, with <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} /> the part of the grain that has gone by, from 0 to 1, and <M t="e" /> the part of it that each fade takes:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{ctl}{w(\varphi)} = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 \cos\!\Bigl(\pi\,\frac{\varphi}{\at{window-rise}{e}}\Bigr) \qquad \text{while } \varphi < e`}
  />

  Read it against the picture. At the start <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} /> is 0, the cosine is 1 and the window is nothing. When <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} /> reaches <M t="e" /> the cosine has gone half way round to −1, and the window is 1. After that it stays at 1 until the same distance from the end, where it falls by the same curve run backwards. <M t="e" /> is half of Shape. At a Shape of 1 the two fades meet in the middle with no flat part between them, and the window is one whole smooth bell, the Hann window.

  The device always asks for the full bell. Its reason is in what bells add up to: started at even steps of half their length or less, they sum to a constant, so overlapping grains of one sound can put that sound back together exactly. <Lesson id="short-time-fourier" cap /> shows why that holds and which windows have it. The flat-topped windows are in the kit for other uses, and in this lab for the lesson's own pool. At the least Shape the kit allows, <Src path="cpp/kit/grains.h" at="grain.edge = clamp(shape, 0.02f, 1.0f) * 0.5f;">0.02</Src>, the window is fully open for <Claim id="shape-least">98 %</Claim> of the grain. That is the nearly square piece of part 1.

A faster grain closes on the write point as it plays, so it must start further back:

    block

  <M t="t" /> is the write point, the sample being recorded now. The last term is half of how much more ring the grain covers than time passes while it sounds. At a speed of 1 it is nothing. <StageBlock tap="cover" n={7} title="The windows added up, and the gain that holds the level">
  Each grain is quiet at its ends and full in its middle. Add the windows of all the grains that are sounding, each times its gain, and the result says how much of the recording is getting through at each moment.

  <Controls ids="density" />

  <Trace taps="cover" min={0} max={2}>
    The windows of the device's grains added up, across the loop. Try Density from 1 to 2, and
    notice a row of humps turn into a level line at one.
  </Trace>

  At a Density of 1 the windows stand end to end, and their sum falls to nothing at every joint and rises to <Claim id="bells-peak">1.63</Claim> in the middle of each grain. The grains are then the recording, late, times that sum: at the opening settings <Claim id="grain-in-device">the grains differ from the recording 250 ms earlier times the sum by more than 130 dB under</Claim>. From a Density of 2 up, at whole numbers, the bells add to exactly one and the device is a plain delay made the long way round: on the guitar <Claim id="plain-delay">the grains differ from the recording 250 ms earlier by more than 85 dB under it</Claim>.

  The gain is what puts the peak at 1.63 and the level line at one. The device asks how much power a sound of power 1 would come out with, and divides by its square root:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`A = \frac{\at{birth-gain}{\sqrt 2}}{\sqrt{P}} \qquad P = \at{birth-power}{c\,P_{\text{same}} + (1 - c)\,P_{\text{apart}}}`}
  />

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\at{alike-same}{P_{\text{same}} = \tfrac14 D^2\,(1 + 2\rho^2)} \qquad \at{alike-apart}{P_{\text{apart}} = \tfrac38 D}`}
  />

  There are two cases, and <M t="c" /> says how much of each applies. When every grain reads the same sound, the grains add like waves in step. <M t="D" /> bells at once average half of <M t="D" /> in height, and height squared is power: a quarter of <M t="D" /> squared. <M t={String.raw`\rho`} /> is the size of the ripple in that sum. It is a half at a Density of 1, where the sum swings between nothing and its peak, and nothing from 2 up at whole numbers. When the grains read unrelated sound they add like a crowd of voices, power to power. One bell lets through three eighths of the power of what is under it, and there are <M t="D" /> bells. The square root of two on top undoes the pool's pan law at the centre.

  <M t="c" /> is the device's estimate of how alike the grains' sound is, from 1 down to 0:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\at{alike-exp}{c = \exp\!\bigl(-\tfrac12\,(s/\tau)^2\bigr)\,(1 - \text{Reverse})}`}
  />

  <M t="s" /> is how far apart in time two overlapping grains read, as the device reckons it from Pitch, Spray, Pitch Spray and Size, and <M t={String.raw`\tau`} /> is the time over which a sound stays like itself, taken as <Src path="cpp/devices/grain-delay/grain_delay.h" at="static constexpr float kAlikeSeconds = 0.0002f;">0.2 ms</Src>. Grains that read places closer together than that count as reading the same sound. It is an estimate fitted to one kind of sound and not a measurement of the sound that is playing, and part 7 shows where it misses.

  Where it holds, the level stays put while Density moves. On white noise from a Density of 1 to 8, at a Pitch of 0 and an octave up, on every one of the eight dice, the grains come out <Claim id="level-holds">less than 1 dB</Claim> from the level of what was recorded.

<StageBlock tap="steady" n={9} title="Why the starts are scattered">
  One throw of stage 6 has not been explained: the wait. Why would a device make its grains uneven on purpose?

  Because evenly spaced grains have a pitch of their own. Play a steady tone through grains that start at an even rate, and what comes out repeats at that rate. The lab plays <Src path="site/labs/granular.cpp" at="constexpr float kToneHz = 1000.0f;">a 1 kHz tone</Src> through a second copy of the device, and through a pool of its own that reads the same ring by the same rule, except that its waits are as uneven as Scatter says.

    max={6}

    The spectrum of the grains of the lab's tone, either side of where Pitch puts it, from the
    device and from the lab's own pool. Try Size at 20 ms, and notice three lines stand where one

  With <M t={String.raw`f_0`} /> the tone that went in and <M t={String.raw`f_g`} /> the number of grains started each second, evenly spaced grains can put sound only at

  <M block t={String.raw`f_\ell = f_0 + \ell\,f_g`} />

  where <M t={String.raw`\ell`} /> is any whole number, positive or negative. The window decides how strong each of those lines is.

<StageBlock tap="out" n={10} title="Limit, tone, and round again">
  The grains' sum goes through three small stages. It is limited by <Src path="cpp/devices/grain-delay/grain_delay.h" at="wet[c] = 2.0f * kit::soft_clip(0.5f * wet[c]);">a curve</Src> that is a straight line for anything under full scale and bends above it, so normal levels pass untouched and no pile-up of grains can run away. It is low-passed at Tone. And it is high-passed at <Src path="cpp/devices/grain-delay/grain_delay.h" at="rumble_[c].set_cutoff(30.0f, sample_rate());">30 Hz</Src>, because a grain that reads slowly can turn a low note into something under hearing that only eats headroom.

  Then the result goes two ways. One way is out, blended with the dry sound. With <M t="m" /> for Mix, <M t="x" /> the sound coming in and <M t="z" /> the grains after those three stages:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[t]} = \at{loop-dry}{\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2} m\bigr)}\,\sig{in}{x[t]} + \at{loop-wet}{\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{\pi}{2} m\bigr)}\,z[t]`}
  />

  The cosine and the sine keep the power of the blend steady as Mix moves: at a half, each side is at 0.707 and not at 0.5.

  The other way is back onto the ring. With <M t="F" /> for Feedback:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`q[t] = \operatorname{clip}\Bigl(\operatorname{lp}\bigl(\sig{in}{x[t]} + \at{loop-record}{F\,z[t]}\bigr)\Bigr)`}
  />

  Here lp is the low-pass of stage 1 and clip the limit. This one line is why a grain delay with Feedback does something no chain of separate effects does. The grains read what the grains made. A repeat that was shifted an octave is recorded, read by new grains and shifted an octave again. <Claim id="octave-stair">With a short 220 Hz note, Pitch at +12 semitones, Time at 500 ms, Size at 100 ms, Density at 4 and Feedback at 0.5, the first three repeats come back strongest, of every semitone, at 440 Hz, 880 Hz and 1,760 Hz</Claim>. Tone is inside the loop as well, so each trip darkens the sound once more.

  How fast the repeats die is Feedback's to say. With Pitch at 0, Density at 2 and the rest as in that measurement, each repeat comes back <Claim id="repeat-level">6.1 dB</Claim> under the one before. Six of that is the half that Feedback multiplies by, and the rest is the filters.

<Part id="ways" title="Five things people do with these controls.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the beads of stage 5 and the lanes under stage 10 are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Stack repeats a fifth apart" tag="Pitch up a fifth, and Feedback" set="fifth">
    Feedback sends each shifted repeat round to be shifted again, so the pitch steps by the same interval on every trip. An octave stays in key however far it climbs. A fifth makes a chord. On this card's settings <Claim id="way-fifth">a short 220 Hz note comes back strongest at 330 Hz and then at 494 Hz</Claim>: a fifth up, and a fifth above that. The third trip is already a note that most keys do not hold, so Feedback here is a choice of how many of those steps are heard. After the card, try Pitch at 5 and at 12, and listen to how soon each one leaves the key.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Turn a chord into a cloud" tag="long grains, many of them, read from far apart" set="smear">
    Long grains, as many as the device allows, each read from a different place across more than a third of a second, and most of the result sent round again. No one grain can be picked out, and what was a rhythm becomes a texture that changes when the harmony does. After the card, lower Spray a little at a time and watch the beads of stage 5 gather under the line of Time. The rhythm of the chords comes back as they do.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Make the grains themselves a note" tag="short grains, one at a time" set="rattle">
    At a Density of 1 every grain fades to nothing before the next begins, and with Size short enough that happens too often to hear as separate fades: <Claim id="way-rattle">200 grains start in the four seconds of the loop, one every 20 ms</Claim>. A fade that comes round that often is a tone of its own, laid on whatever is playing. Stage 9 measured what it adds to a single frequency. After the card, raise Size and listen for the buzz falling in pitch. Then raise Density to 2 and listen for it going out.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Answer backwards" tag="Reverse all the way, with long grains" set="back">
    Each grain starts at the late end of its piece and reads towards the early end. The pieces still come in the order they were played, since the rule of stage 5 puts the middle of each where Time says, so the phrase keeps its shape while every piece of it swells where it used to die away. Long grains are the point here: the shorter a grain is, the less of the sound's own shape there is in it to turn round. After the card, try Reverse at 0.5. The direction is then thrown for each grain, and about half go each way.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Spread one sound from side to side" tag="a short Time, and Spread all the way" set="wide">
    Spread throws each grain to a place between the two sides. Keep Time short and Spray small, so that the grains are near copies of what is playing now, thrown from side to side. Listen for the sound getting wider with no echo standing behind it. After the card, bring Spray down to nothing. Every grain then reads the same sound, and the two sides differ only in how much of each grain they got.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Five ways it goes wrong, four of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="Grains you can count" set="single">
    One grain at a time means a gap at every joint. On a sound that should hold steady, listen for the gaps. <Claim id="fault-single">On the pad at a Density of 1 the sum of the windows falls to nothing 33 times in the loop</Claim>, once for each grain. This is how the lesson starts, on purpose, to show one grain by itself. For anything held, raise Density to 2 or more, where the windows add to one and the gaps close.

    <Hear set="closed" label="Hear the same pad at a Density of 2" />

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A shifted note that all but vanishes" set="locked">
    This is stage 9's trap, and it is the one a first try at a grain shifter falls into. Start the grains at an even rate with the pitch shifted and a steady note is cancelled nearly everywhere. The card plays the lab's own pool with its Scatter at 0. <Claim id="fault-locked">The 440 Hz tone, shifted seven semitones at a Size of 100 ms and a Density of 4, comes out 31 dB under the same tone with the starts scattered and the device's own dice, and more than 30 dB under it on every one of the eight</Claim>. The device does not let this happen: it scatters the starts by itself once the grains read different sound. If you ever build a pool of your own, scatter them.

    <Hear set="unlocked" label="Hear the same note with the starts scattered" />

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A Time the grains cannot keep" set="floor">
    A grain that reads faster than the ring is written must start far enough back to last. Ask for a short Time with long grains and Pitch up, and the device moves the grains back without saying so. On this card Time is at its least, and <Claim id="floor-middle">the middle of every grain reads what is 250 ms old</Claim>. The answer arrives a quarter of a second after the note, whatever the control says. To get it nearer, shorten Size: the floor is the extra ring a grain covers in its lifetime, and a short grain covers little.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A level that sags a hair off pitch" set="dip">
    The gain of stage 7 rests on a guess of how alike the grains' sound is, and the guess takes the sound to be like itself over a fixed stretch of time. Bright sound is not: it changes faster than that. So when grains of a bright sound read from places only a little apart, the device counts them as in step, turns them down as if they were, and they are not. <Claim id="level-model">White noise at a Density of 4, with Pitch a fiftieth of a semitone up, comes out more than 5 dB under what was recorded</Claim>. On pitch, nothing is lost. The card plays that setting. The estimate was <Src path="cpp/devices/grain-delay/grain_delay.h" at="// were fitted to band-limited noise (see the harness).">fitted to noise with its top taken off</Src>, and white noise has more up there than that. The trap is narrow, though: a wider move of Pitch takes the grains out of step, the device counts them as apart, and stage 7 measured the level holding on this same noise an octave up.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A grain too short for the note">
    A grain keeps the colour of what it reads only if it holds a few cycles of it. Shorten Size until a grain is no longer than one cycle of a low note and the note is gone: what is left is the grain's own bell, repeated. Short grains suit drums and bright sound, and low notes need long ones. This is why the bass card of part 3 uses longer grains than the drums' card does.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A grain delay is a delay with an effect after it.">
    Nothing in it plays the recording late and then changes it. Every sample that comes out was read by a grain, and the pitch, the direction and the scatter are all in how the grains read. With two or more grains overlapping, all reading the same place at the recording's speed, the grains happen to add up to a plain delay, and stage 7 measures how exactly. That is one setting among many, and not a stage inside.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Pitch up makes the answer come sooner.">
    Each grain plays its piece faster. The grains still start at the pace of the clock, and each is placed so that its middle reads what is Time old. The prediction of part 1 has the measurement. <Lesson id="pitch-shifting" cap /> is the same trade with two long grains that take turns, and it says what the trade costs.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Spray scatters the grains either side of Time.">
    The throw for place only adds. No grain reads sound newer than Time, and stage 6 holds that on eight dice. So Spray makes the answer later on average as well as looser. To keep a sprayed cloud where it was, shorten Time as Spray goes up.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="It is random, so it never plays the same way twice.">
    The dice are arithmetic, started from a fixed number when the device starts. From the same start they give the same grains, to the sample, as stage 6 shows. What changes between two passes of a song is where in its run of throws the device has got to, and not the run itself.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="More grains is louder.">
    The device works out how much power the overlap adds and divides it away as each grain starts. Stage 7 moves Density through its whole range on white noise and the level stays where it was. More grains is denser and smoother. Where the level does move, it is the estimate of part 7 that has missed.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Size is how long the echo is.">
    Size is how long each piece is, and Time is how old the sound in it is. The two are separate controls, and the beads of part 2 show them apart: Size is a bead's width and Time is its depth. They meet only at the floor of stage 5, where long, fast grains cannot start as near as Time asks.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
