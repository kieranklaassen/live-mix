A one-pole filter keeps one number. Each time a sample arrives it moves that number a fixed part of the way towards the sample, and the number is its output. That is the whole filter: one multiply, one add and one float of memory.

Because the number only ever goes part of the way, it cannot follow anything fast. A slow swell drags it along. A quick wiggle is over before it has moved. What comes out is a running average of what went in, in which the newest samples count most and old ones fade: a **low-pass**. Take that average away from the input and what is left is the quick part: a **high-pass**.

The engine gives this filter two jobs that look unrelated. On a sound it takes the top off. On a control, such as a fader's position, it turns a jump into a glide, so that a gain moving under a held note does not click. More than a hundred of the engine's devices have one in the second job on their knobs. The lab runs both. The first three stages below are the filter on the sound you chose; the last four are the same filter on a fader.

## Stage by stage

### 1. One line: an average with memory

The filter is for averaging without keeping the samples it averages. Its recurrence, with <M t="x" /> the input and <M t="y" /> the output:

<M block t={String.raw`y[n] = x[n] + a\,\bigl(y[n-1] - x[n]\bigr)`} />

The coefficient <M t="a" />, between 0 and 1, is how much of the old output survives each sample. It is the filter's *pole*, and the only setting it has. In the code this is <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h">`OnePole::lowpass`</Src>, one line: `state = x + (state - x) * a`. Multiplied out it reads <M t={String.raw`(1-a)\,x[n] + a\,y[n-1]`} />: a little of the new sample and most of the old output. Follow that back through time and it unrolls into a sum over everything that ever went in:

<M block t={String.raw`y[n] = (1-a)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a^{k}\,x[n-k]`} />

This is a weighted average. The newest sample has the weight <M t="1-a" />, and every older one <M t="a" /> times the weight of the sample after it. The weights add up to 1, so a steady input comes out at its own level. A plain average of the last 100 samples would have to store 100 samples; this one stores a single float, at the price of never wholly forgetting.

Put in one sample of 1 and then nothing, and the filter reads its weights out one after another. That is its *impulse response*, and for any filter made of delays, multiplies and adds it says everything there is to say (lesson 15 builds a reverb on that fact).

### 2. The time constant

A cutoff in hertz says little about a filter used as a smoother. The useful question there is how long its memory is, and a step answers it. Hold the input at 1 from rest, and after <M t="p" /> samples the output stands at <M t={String.raw`1 - a^{p}`} />. It closes the same fraction of the remaining distance on every sample, so it never arrives. But there is a natural length in it, the *time constant* <M t={String.raw`\tau`} />: the time in which the remaining distance falls to <M t="1/e" />, 37 % of what it was.

<M block t={String.raw`a = e^{-1/(\tau f_s)} \qquad\qquad \tau = \frac{1}{2\pi f_c}`} />

The kit sets the pole both ways and they are the same formula. <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h">`time_to_coeff(seconds, sample_rate)`</Src> is the left side as written. <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h">`OnePole::set_cutoff`</Src> computes `exp(-kTwoPi * hz / sample_rate)`, which is the left side with the right side put in. A cutoff of 500 Hz is a time constant of 0.32 ms, 15.3 samples at the lab's 48 kHz; the engine's usual smoothing time of 5 ms is a cutoff of 31.8 Hz.

### 3. The response to frequency

What a filter does to a steady tone is to change its level and slide it in time, by amounts that depend on the tone's frequency. The level against frequency is the *frequency response*. The lab measures it. It puts one unit sample through the kit's filter, keeps what comes out, and works out how much of each of 241 frequencies that response holds (the sum of lesson 2). The textbook's answer for one pole, with <M t="f_c" /> the cutoff, is

<M block t={String.raw`|H(f)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^2}}`} />

Far below the cutoff this is 1. At the cutoff it is <M t={String.raw`1/\sqrt2`} />, half the power, 3 dB down. Far above, it halves each time the frequency doubles: a tone twice as fast gives the average half as long to move before the wave turns round.

### 4. The high-pass: what the average leaves out

A high-pass needs no second filter. If the low-pass is the slow part of a signal, the rest of the signal is the fast part:

<M block t={String.raw`h[n] = x[n] - y[n]`} />

<Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h">`OnePole::highpass`</Src> is that line, `x - lowpass(x)`. Its response is the low-pass's mirrored: nothing at 0 Hz, rising <Claim id="high-octave">6.0 dB an octave</Claim> towards the cutoff, flat above it.

<Curve taps="response:out responseHigh:alt" xLog xMin={20} xMax={20000} uprights="cutoff" min={-48} max={3} xTitle="hertz" yTitle="level, dB" xUnit="Hz" height={260}>
  The low-pass (red) and the high-pass (green), both measured through the kit's filter. They cross beside the cutoff, about 3 dB down. Listen to each on the drums: one keeps the kick, the other the hats.
</Curve>

The two halves are made to add back to the input, and they do: on the noise the sum is the input sample for sample, and on the drums it is <Claim id="complement">within one rounding of a float, 7.5e-9</Claim>, because a subtraction and an addition each round once. What the split does not keep is the textbook's symmetry. There, both halves are 3 dB down at the cutoff. Here a tone at the cutoff comes through the high-pass <Claim id="high-at-cutoff">3.3 dB down</Claim>: the complement is exact in samples, not in power.

A device built on this split is the <Src path="cpp/devices/tremolo/tremolo.h">Tremolo</Src>. Its Harmonic mode sends the input through one `OnePole` per channel, takes `x - low` as the other band, and turns the two up and down in turn. The lab runs that device beside its own filter and reads the device's low band out of it; the two are <Claim id="split">the same, sample for sample</Claim>. The device is on the bench at the foot of this page.

### 5. The same filter on a control

A host does not send a control's position on every sample. It sends a value when the fader has moved, a few dozen times a second at best, and the device holds it until the next. Applied as it arrives, a moving fader is a staircase. The engine's rule is that every device smooths what it is sent, and <Src path="cpp/kit/smooth.h">`Smoother`</Src> is the one-pole with its input renamed: `target` is what arrived, `value` is where the control is now, and `next()` is one step of the recurrence, <M t={String.raw`v \leftarrow t + c\,(v - t)`} />, with <M t="c" /> from `time_to_coeff`. The time every device uses unless it has a reason not to is 5 ms, <Src path="cpp/kit/device.h">`kSmoothingSeconds`</Src>.

`Smoother` differs from the bare filter in three ways. It *lands*: once the distance left is under a millionth of the target, or the float has stopped moving, `next()` puts the value exactly on the target, so a settled control costs nothing and can be tested with `settled()`. It can *snap*: `set(v, primed)` jumps while the device has not yet run a block, so the settings a device is created with do not glide in from the defaults. And it runs at whatever rate it is called. Many devices call `next()` once every 16 or 32 samples, on a <Src path="cpp/kit/smooth.h">`ControlClock`</Src>, and hand `set_time` that slower rate so the 5 ms stays 5 ms.

<Src path="cpp/kit/smooth.h">`LinearRamp`</Src> is the other smoother: a straight line that takes a fixed number of samples and is exactly on the target at the end. The engine uses it where being finished on time matters more than the shape, such as a crossfade.

### 6. What a step does to the sound: zipper noise

A gain that jumps multiplies a jump into the wave. The ear hears a jump as a click, because a sudden edge holds every frequency at once, and a moving fader makes a row of them. That rattle is called *zipper noise*. The lab's fader rides a steady 220 Hz tone, where there is nothing to hide it.

## What goes wrong

**Set high, the cutoff is only a name.** The kit takes the pole from the time constant, so time is exact at every setting (stage 2) and frequency is the part that bends. The response is 3 dB down where <M t={String.raw`\cos\omega = 2 - \cosh\omega_0`} />, with <M t={String.raw`\omega_0 = 2\pi f_c/f_s`} />, and the measurement lands <Claim id="corner-formula">on that within 0.01 %</Claim>. That point is <Claim id="corner-2pc">within 2 % of the cutoff up to 3.6 kHz</Claim>. Set to 8 kHz the filter is 3 dB down at <Claim id="corner-8k">8.86 kHz</Claim>. <Claim id="corner-never">From 13.5 kHz up it never falls 3 dB at all</Claim>: set to 20 kHz, it is <Claim id="corner-20k">1.2 dB down at 20 kHz</Claim>. A pole placed for an exact corner would be <M t={String.raw`a = b - \sqrt{b^2 - 1}`} /> with <M t={String.raw`b = 2 - \cos\omega_c`} />. The engine's one-poles set that high are gentle roll-offs, where the name is enough; a filter that must be in tune uses the state-variable filter of the next lesson.

**It never arrives.** The recurrence closes a fraction of the distance, and a float cannot hold a distance smaller than its own step. At 500 Hz the low-pass stops <Claim id="never-arrives">7 float steps short of a steady 1</Claim> and stays there, which leaves the high-pass 128 dB down for ever, not silent. A slower pole stops further out: the bare filter at 5 ms ends <Claim id="stall">120 float steps short, 103 dB down</Claim>. That is what `Smoother`'s landing is for. Falling towards zero has the opposite trouble: the state shrinks into numbers so small that arithmetic on them is slow, so `lowpass` passes its state through <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h">`flush_denormal`</Src>, which makes anything under 1e-15 an exact 0.

<Check id="stall" />

**Smoothing is lag, and it does not remove the stairs.** At 5 ms a control is where it was sent about 15 ms later. Longer is smoother and later. And a smoother only rounds each stair's corner: at ten values a second the red line in stage 5 is still a staircase. The click goes; the stepping goes only when the values come as often as the smoothing is long.

**One pole is all the slope there is.** Two in a row give 12 dB an octave, but a soft corner. A steep filter with a sharp corner, or one that rings, needs two poles that work together.

## What to try

- Noise, Listen on the low-pass, and sweep the **cutoff** from 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Then the same on the high-pass.
- Drums at 200 Hz. The low-pass is the kick alone and the high-pass is everything else.
- Clicks, cutoff at 100 Hz, Listen on the low-pass: each click is now the impulse response of stage 1, a soft thump 1.6 ms long.
- The sweep, and the small pictures in the strip of stages: the low-pass fades as the sweep rises and the high-pass grows. That outline is the response of stage 3, drawn by the sound.
- **Smoothing** at 0, Listen on the last stage, then 5 ms, then 20 ms.
- **Fader values** at 200 a second with the smoothing at 0: the clicks run together into a buzz. Then 5 ms.
- Switch **The fader rides** to the source and choose the drums, then the saw. The clicks are as large as before; a busy sound covers them and a held one does not.
