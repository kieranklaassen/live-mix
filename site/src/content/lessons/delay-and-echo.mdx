<Part id="why" title="A sound that answers itself needs a memory.">

A guitar line is picked one note at a time. Each note starts loud and fades until the next one is picked.

<Still
  taps="in"
  set="dry"
  caption="Four seconds of the picked line. Every note starts tall and falls away, and the next one starts before it has gone."
/>

Listen to it first. <Hear set="dry" label="Hear the line as played" />

Played across a valley, or at the foot of a stairwell, the same line comes back from the far wall a moment later and quieter, and then again from further off. That answer does two things a player can use. It fills the gaps between the notes with the notes themselves. And it sets a second rhythm against the first, because the answer arrives at its own time and not at the player's.

How does a program make a sound answer itself, later and quieter, again and again?

It has to remember. To play now what was played a quarter of a second ago, a program must still have it: every sample of the last quarter of a second, kept in the order they came. That store is called a delay line. Each sample period it takes one new sample in, and hands back the one it was given a fixed time before.

### One copy is not an echo

The obvious answer is to keep the sound and play it once more, late. This page opens on the plainest sound there is for watching that happen: a click, which is <Src path="site/labs/bench.cpp" at="clicks.set(i, i % kBar == 0">one sample at full scale, twice a bar</Src>, and at this bench's tempo <Src path="site/labs/bench.cpp" at="// The tempo is 120: a bar is two seconds">a bar is two seconds</Src>. One sample shows exactly what is done to it.

  span={2}
  min={-0.25}
  max={1}
  caption="Two seconds of the click going in and of what the delay line gives back. Each click has one copy behind it, and then nothing until the next click."

The page starts Time at <Src path="site/labs/delay-and-echo.cpp" at={'lab::Control time{"time", "Time", "ms"'}>250 ms</Src>, and the lab runs at <Src path="site/labs/lab.h" at="constexpr float kSampleRate = 48000.0f;">48,000 samples a second</Src>. With Feedback at 0, a click <Claim id="returns">comes back once, 12,000 samples later, and nothing follows it</Claim>.

A wall does more than that, because the sound that comes back from it goes out again and comes back again. One copy has no trail. On the guitar it is a doubling of each note, and it stops there. <Hear set="once" label="Hear the line played once more" />

### So the copy is copied

The fix is to record the copy as well. Some of what the delay line gives back is sent into the delay line again, beside the new sound. The copy is then copied in its turn, and that copy too, each one smaller than the last by the same factor. The factor is called Feedback.

  set="trail"
  source="clicks"

  span={2}
  min={-0.25}
  max={1}
  caption="The same two seconds with Feedback raised. Each click is now followed by a row of copies, evenly spaced, each smaller than the one before it."
/>

Now the guitar has its answer. <Hear set="trail" label="Hear the line with a trail" />

<Predict

About ten times. Halving a size takes 6 dB from it, and decibels add where sizes multiply, so ten halvings take 60 dB.

The device agrees. A short burst of a 1 kHz tone is played through it at a Feedback of 0.5 and each repeat is measured as it comes back. Ten trips after its first repeat, the burst is <Claim id="t60">60.4 dB</Claim> down.

A trail has no length of its own. It is a number of trips, and each trip takes one Time. So the same Feedback gives a trail four times as long when Time is four times as long.

</Predict>

Memory and feedback are the whole of the idea. The rest of this lesson is about what else is in the loop. A filter in it makes each repeat duller than the last. A ceiling in it decides what happens when the repeats are asked to grow and not to die. And a memory that is read at a place which moves bends the pitch of everything in it.

The engine's echo is written as a tape machine: a loop of tape, a head that records, and heads further along that play. In its code the tape is a delay line, recording is a write and playing is a read. This lesson uses both sets of words. It starts the device as a plain echo, with a steady transport, a straight tape and filters as open as they go, so that each stage is easy to see. Each of those comes back as a control where its stage is opened, and part 9 puts the lesson's settings beside the device's own.

</Part>

<Part id="picture" title="A ring of slots, a head that writes and a head that reads.">

A delay line is a row of slots, each holding one sample, and two places in the row. The write head is where the next sample goes in. The read head is where a sample is taken out. Every sample period the read head hands back what its slot holds, the write head puts the new sample in its slot, and both step on by one slot.

Nothing in the row ever moves. A sample stays in the slot it was written to, and the heads walk past it. When the write head steps off the last slot it comes round to the first, and writes over the oldest sample there is. That is why the row is drawn as a ring. A ring of sixteen slots remembers the last sixteen samples and no more.

The delay is the distance between the two heads. A read head that stands five slots behind the write head is reading what the write head wrote five steps ago. A delay time is no more than that: a distance in slots, which is a time in samples. A ring has one write head. It can have as many read heads as are wanted, each at its own distance, because reading changes nothing in the ring.

The picture below is the kit's own delay line, <Src path="site/labs/delay-and-echo.cpp" at="constexpr int kRingSlots = 16;">sixteen slots long</Src> so that all of it can be seen. One click of size 1 is written at step 0. On every step the read head reads, and the write head writes Feedback times what was read.

Check this against the picture. Leave the read head 5 slots behind and Feedback at 0.5, and go on from the start to step 5. R stands on slot 0, where the click lies: the click is read <Claim id="ring-returns">5 samples after it was written, and is written again at 0.5</Claim>, into slot 5. On step 10, the step the picture opens on, R stands on slot 5 and that copy is read in its turn, and W writes it again at 0.25, into slot 10. <Claim id="ring-round">On step 16 the write head is back on slot 0</Claim> and writes over the click. The ring kept it for sixteen samples, and has no slot for a seventeenth.

Two things in this picture carry the rest of the lesson. The trail of an echo is one sound going round a ring, losing a little on each trip. And the time between its repeats is nothing in the sound at all: it is how far apart two heads stand.

The engine's ring is the same thing at another size: <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="static constexpr int kTapeSize = 262144;">262,144 slots</Src> for each side, enough for several seconds. Stage 6 of part 5 draws all of it.

The three lessons after this one stand on this ring, and each writes to it just as this one does. They differ in where they read. A chorus, in <Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" />, reads at a distance that sways. A pitch shifter, in <Lesson id="pitch-shifting" />, reads at a distance that keeps growing or shrinking. A granular player, in <Lesson id="granular" />, reads many short pieces of the ring at once.

</Part>

<Part id="engine" title="The engine's own echo, opened up.">

On the drums, at the starting settings and again with every control at work, each reads <Claim id="rebuilt">−140 dB</Claim>, which is the floor of the scale: whatever difference there is lies more than 140 dB under the device's own signal.

<StageBlock tap="delay" n={1} title="Where the head stands: Time, a motor and two wanderings">
  The first thing the device does for a sample is work out how far behind the write index its Time head should read. The answer is a number of samples, and it is hardly ever a whole one. It has three parts.

  Time does not jump when it is turned. It is followed by a smoother with a time constant of <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="static constexpr float kMotorLagSeconds = 0.12f;">120 ms</Src>, where the device's other controls have one of <Src path="cpp/kit/device.h" at="static constexpr float kSmoothingSeconds = 0.005f;">5 ms</Src>. It is a motor that takes a moment to change speed.

  On top of that come two wanderings of the head, each a length of time scaled by its control. Wow is up to <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="static constexpr float kWowSeconds = 0.0016f;">1.6 ms</Src> of a slow drift: <Src path="cpp/kit/lfo.h" at="static constexpr float kWeights[3] = {0.5f, 0.3f, 0.2f};">three sines</Src> around <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="wow_drift_.set_rate(0.5f, sr);">half a hertz</Src> whose rates do not divide into one another, so the pattern does not come round. Flutter is up to <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="static constexpr float kFlutterSeconds = 0.00007f;">0.07 ms</Src> of something faster: <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="(0.7f * flutter_lfo_.next(kit::Lfo::kSine) + 0.3f * flutter_drift_.next());">seven parts of a sine to three of a drift</Src>, the sine at <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="flutter_lfo_.set_rate(7.1f, sr);">7.1 Hz</Src> and the drift around <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="flutter_drift_.set_rate(1.9f, sr);">1.9 Hz</Src>. Both controls start at 0 in this lesson, which is a steady transport.

<StageBlock tap="speed" n={2} title="A head that moves bends the pitch">
  A head that is moving along the tape passes it at another speed than the one it was recorded at, and that changes the pitch of everything the head reads. The device keeps that speed as a meter, and the lab reads it. At 1 the transport is steady.

  <Controls ids="wow flutter nudge" />

  <Trace taps="speed">
    The tape's speed past the head, from the device's own meter. Above 1 the repeats play sharp, and
    below it flat. Try Wow at its highest, and notice that this line swings furthest where the line
    of stage 1 is steepest, not where it is highest.
  </Trace>

  <Still
    taps="speed"
    values={{ nudge: 60 }}
    caption="The same meter with the last control at 60 ms, so that Time is lengthened half way along and turned back at the start. Half way along the speed drops at once to 0.5 and climbs back to 1 as the motor settles. At the start it stands at 1.5 and falls back."
  />

  To see why, put a sine of frequency <M t="f" /> into a delay whose length <M t="T" /> changes with time. What comes out at the time <M t="t" /> is what went in at <M t="t - T(t)" />, so its phase is <M t={String.raw`2\pi f\,(t - T(t))`} />. Frequency is how fast phase advances.

  <M slot="equation" block t={String.raw`f_{\text{out}} = f\,\bigl(\sig{ctl}{1 - T'(t)}\bigr)`} />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t="T'(t)" /> is the rate at which the delay is changing: seconds of delay gained in each second that passes. Only that rate is in it. While the delay grows, the head falls behind the write index, and what it reads plays slow and flat. While the delay shrinks, the head catches up and the sound plays fast and sharp. A delay that holds still changes no pitch, however long it is. The bracket is the speed of the tape past the head, and it is the number the meter keeps: <M t={String.raw`\at{meter-speed}{\text{one less the step of the place read}}`} /> from one sample to the next.

    As a musical distance, the bend is

    <M block t={String.raw`\text{cents} = 1200\,\log_2\bigl(1 - T'(t)\bigr)`} />

    Turning Time is the large case. By the motor's curve of stage 1, a turn of <M t={String.raw`\Delta`} /> starts the speed at <M t={String.raw`1 - \Delta/\tau`} />, and the speed comes back to 1 as the motor settles. <Claim id="motor">Lengthen Time by 60 ms and the tape drops at once to 0.5 of its speed</Claim>, an octave down. Lengthen it by as much as the motor's time constant and the tape stands still for an instant. Lengthen it by more and it runs backwards. Shorten it and the tape speeds up.

    The wanderings are the small case. Neither comes round, so how far each is seen to go depends on the stretch it is watched over. The figures here were read off the device's meter by the lab's test over the second pass of an eight-second loop, which is the device's ninth second to its sixteenth. Over that stretch Wow at 1 moves the place read by as much as <Claim id="wow-range">1.45 ms, and the pitch between 6.5 cents flat and 7.8 cents sharp</Claim>. Flutter at 1 moves it by only <Claim id="flutter-range">0.07 ms, and the pitch between 4.2 cents flat and 4.1 cents sharp</Claim>. That is a twentieth of the distance for more than half of the bend, because it is the rate that counts and Flutter is the faster of the two. Another stretch gives other extremes, and the page's own four-second loop is one.

    Each wandering is added as a time and not as a share of Time, so the bend does not depend on Time. The lab's test reads the meter with Time at its shortest, at its longest and at two places between, and the readings are <Claim id="same-wobble">the same, point for point</Claim>. A long echo does not waver more than a short one.

    To hold the formula to the sound, a test plays a 1 kHz sine through the wandering head and counts its zero crossings as it comes back. The pitch it finds is <Claim id="pitch-formula">less than 0.1 cent</Claim> from what the meter says.
  </Fragment>

<StageBlock tap="heads" n={3} title="The heads read between samples">
  With the place worked out, the device reads the tape there. The place is almost never a slot: step 4 of the build met a Time that falls between two, and a head that moves passes through every place between two slots on its way. So a read has to make up a value between the samples it has.

  The cheap way is a straight line between the two nearest samples, a linear read. The better way looks at four, two on each side, and puts a cubic through the middle two whose slope at each is set by the samples either side of it. A cubic made that way is a Hermite cubic, and the kit calls the read by that name. The clearest way to see what each does is to store one sample and read all round it.

  <Controls ids="fraction" />

  <Curve
    taps="kernelLinear:alt kernelHermite:out"
    uprights="fraction"
    xTitle="samples from the stored one"
    min={-0.2}
    max={1.2}
  >
    One stored sample of size 1, read by the kit's line at every place from three samples on one
    side of it to three on the other. Read linearly it is a triangle: a sample counts only towards
    reads less than one slot away. Read by the cubic it reaches two slots each way and dips below
    zero.
    The dashed line is Between samples. The height of a curve there, and at whole steps either side
    of there, is what that read multiplies its samples by. Try it at either end, and notice that
    only one sample is left with any weight.
  </Curve>

  The cubic is worked out from three numbers.

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} c_1 &= \tfrac12\,(v_1 - v_{-1}) \\ c_2 &= v_{-1} - \tfrac52\,v_0 + 2\,v_1 - \tfrac12\,v_2 \\ c_3 &= \tfrac12\,(v_2 - v_{-1}) + \tfrac32\,(v_0 - v_1) \end{aligned}`}
  />

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{out}{p(\mu)} = \at{hermite-curve}{\bigl((c_3\,\mu + c_2)\,\mu + c_1\bigr)\,\mu + v_0}`}
  />

  The read lands between the samples <M t="v_0" /> and <M t="v_1" />, a fraction <M t={String.raw`\mu`} /> of the way from the first to the second, and <M t="v_{-1}" /> and <M t="v_2" /> are their outer neighbours. The listing calls the four `ym1`, `y0`, `y1` and `y2`, and the fraction `t`. At a fraction of 0 only <M t="v_0" /> is left, which is the read on a slot. <M t="c_1" /> is the slope at <M t="v_0" />, taken from the samples either side of it. The other two bend the curve so that it arrives at <M t="v_1" /> with the slope that the samples round <M t="v_1" /> ask for.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/kit/math.h"
    from="inline float hermite(float ym1, float y0, float y1, float y2, float t) {"
    to="return ((c3 * t + c2) * t + c1) * t + y0;"
    name="hermite"
    at={{ curve: 'return ((c3 * t + c2) * t + c1) * t + y0;' }}
  />

  Half way between two samples the straight line takes a half of each neighbour. The cubic takes <M t={String.raw`-\tfrac1{16},\ \tfrac9{16},\ \tfrac9{16},\ -\tfrac1{16}`} /> of the four round it. The device reads by the cubic: when Time ends in half a sample it turns one sample into four, <Claim id="device-hermite">−0.06, 0.56, 0.56 and −0.06 of it</Claim>.

  Either way a sample has become a weighted sum of its neighbours, and a weighted sum of neighbours is a filter. Averaging smooths, so the filter is a low-pass, and how much it takes depends on where between the samples the read lands.

  <Spectrum taps="responseLinear:alt responseHermite:out" min={-12} max={3}>
    What each read does to each frequency, as a delay of a few samples and the fraction that Between
    samples shows: the lab puts one sample through the kit's line and takes the response. Try
    Between samples at either end, and notice both lines lie flat on 0 dB: a read on a slot loses
    nothing. Half way is the worst there is.
  </Spectrum>

  Half way, the linear read is the mean of two neighbours, whose gain at a frequency <M t="f" /> and a sample rate <M t="f_s" /> is

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{|H_{\text{line}}(f)|} = \cos\Bigl(\frac{\pi f}{f_s}\Bigr)`} />

  The linear read loses <Claim id="linear-half">2.0 dB at 10 kHz and 11.7 dB at 20 kHz</Claim>. The Hermite read loses <Claim id="hermite-half">0.5 dB at 10 kHz and 8.4 dB at 20 kHz</Claim>. Neither is flat, which is why the top of a sound read by a moving head comes and goes a little. The cubic costs two more samples and a few multiplications, and at 10 kHz it loses about a quarter as much.

  The tape can be read in more than one place. The device has three heads, <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="static constexpr float kHeadRatio[kNumHeads] = {1.0f, 2.0f / 3.0f, 1.0f / 3.0f};">at Time, at two thirds of it and at a third</Src>, and Heads picks one of <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="static constexpr float kHeadGains[4][kNumHeads] = {">four mixes of them</Src>. With Three chosen and Time at 600 ms, a click comes back <Claim id="three-heads">at 200 ms, 400 ms and 600 ms</Claim>. The sum of the heads is both what is heard and what goes back round, so every head's repeats repeat.

The filters are inside the loop, and that is the whole point of them: the second repeat has been through them once, and the fifth four times.

  High cut is the kit's state-variable filter, the subject of <Lesson id="state-variable-filter" />, as a low-pass.   <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t="S(f)" /> is the step in decibels from one repeat to the next at the frequency <M t="f" />. <M t="g" /> is Feedback, and <M t={String.raw`|H(f)|`} /> is the gain of the two filters together at that frequency.     The device itself starts High cut at <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/device.json" at={'"default": 4500,'}>4.5 kHz</Src> and Low cut at <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/device.json" at={'"default": 120,'}>120 Hz</Src>. <StageBlock tap="tape" n={6} title="Recording, under a soft ceiling">
  The sum is not recorded as it comes. At a Feedback of 1 the series of stage 5 never dies, and above 1 each repeat is larger than the last. This device lets Feedback go to <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/device.json" at={'"max": 1.1,'}>1.1</Src> on purpose, so that a trail can be made to build, and it puts a soft ceiling where the sum is recorded.

  <Curve taps="saturate" diagonal xTitle="sent to the tape" yTitle="recorded" min={0} max={1.25}>
    What the record path writes against what it was sent, as sample values, with the diagonal of a
    tape that records everything as it comes. Near zero the two lie together. Try Drive higher, and
    notice the line bend away sooner, towards a lower ceiling.
  </Curve>

  <M slot="equation" block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{r} = \at{record-write}{\frac{\tanh(u\,s)}{u}}`} />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t="s" /> is the sum of stage 5 and <M t="r" /> is what is recorded. <M t="u" /> is how hard the tape is driven:

    <M block t={String.raw`\at{sum-drive}{u = 1 + 3 \times \text{Drive}}`} />

    For a small sum the hyperbolic tangent is a straight line of slope 1, and dividing by <M t="u" /> puts back what multiplying by it took. So a quiet sound is recorded at the level it arrived. A loud one is not. The tangent never passes 1, so nothing on the tape passes one over <M t="u" />. The engine uses a quotient of two polynomials in place of the tangent, which reaches exactly 1 where its input is <Src path="cpp/kit/math.h" at="x = clamp(x, -3.0f, 3.0f);">3</Src> and is held there.

    The bend starts early. Even with Drive at 0, a full-scale click goes on to the tape at <Claim id="tape-click">0.778, which is 2.2 dB down</Claim>. That is why the first repeat in the picture the page opens on is shorter than the click that made it.

    Now the loop. A sound of some size goes round at a gain of <M t="g" /> times <M t={String.raw`\tanh(u s)/(u s)`} />. For small sounds that ratio is 1, and it falls as they grow. With Feedback above 1 a quiet repeat comes back larger, and the next larger still, until the ratio has fallen to one over <M t="g" />. The gain round the loop is then 1, and the growing stops. <Claim id="settles">A burst of a 1 kHz tone is left to go round at a Feedback of 1.1 and a Time of 30 ms. In the last second of an 8 s loop its peaks on the tape stand at 0.63 with Drive at 0 and at 0.16 with Drive at 1, each less than 1 % from where it stood the second before</Claim>. The second height is a quarter of the first, because <M t="u" /> is four times as large. On the drums and on the clicks, with Drive at 1 and Feedback at its highest, the largest sample on the tape is <Claim id="ceiling">0.25</Claim>.

    The ceiling is on the tape and not on the sum. With Drive at 0 the tape gives back up to full scale, and Feedback over 1 adds more than that to a sound going in that may be at full scale itself. <Claim id="sum-loud">A 1 kHz tone at full scale, at a Time of 30 ms with Feedback at 1.1 and Drive at 0, makes a sum that peaks at 2.09 times full scale, on a tape that peaks at 0.99 and an output at 1.4</Claim>. The page's player <Src path="site/src/lab/player.ts" at="export const volumeGain">plays every stage at half its size when the volume is all the way up</Src>, so the sum of stage 5, heard through its button at such a setting, is cut off flat in the ear, though the pictures show it whole.

    At a Feedback of exactly 1 a trail still dies, slowly. The loop's filters take a little on every trip, even wide open, and the bend takes more from anything loud. For the lab's burst of a 1 kHz tone, which peaks at <Src path="site/labs/delay-and-echo.cpp" at="constexpr float kBurstPeak = 0.1f;">a tenth of full scale</Src>, the 24th repeat is <Claim id="unity">0.7 dB</Claim> under its first.

      path="cpp/kit/math.h"
      from="inline float fast_tanh(float x) {"
      to="return x * (27.0f + x2) / (27.0f + 9.0f * x2);"
      name="tanh"
    />

    The tape itself is the ring of part 2, the kit's delay line. Writing is two lines and reading is one.

    <M block t={String.raw`b[w] \leftarrow \sig{out}{r}, \qquad w \leftarrow (w + 1) \mathbin{\&} (N - 1)`} />

    <M block t={String.raw`\text{read}(d) = b\bigl[(w - d) \mathbin{\&} (N - 1)\bigr]`} />

    <M t="b" /> is the ring of <M t="N" /> slots, <M t="w" /> is the write index and <M t="d" /> is a delay in whole samples. <M t="N" /> is a power of two, which the kit's line insists on when it is compiled. So coming round is one AND with <M t="N - 1" />: a write index that steps off the end lands on slot 0, and a read that reaches back past slot 0 lands at the far end. The device reads before it writes, so the shortest delay is one sample.

<Part id="ways" title="Seven things people do with an echo.">

Each of these is a habit of working, not a preset. Press "Try it" and the page moves to a setting that shows the idea, so you can go back up and see what the stages of part 5 are doing with it.

<Ways>
  <Way title="On the beat" tag="Time at one note of the part" set="eighths">
    Set Time to the length of one note of the part and every echo lands on the note after it. Nothing new is added to the rhythm: each note carries a little of the one before. Compare the first card of part 3, where the echo falls between the notes. After the card, move Time a little off the note and listen to the echo come apart from the playing.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Darker each time" tag="High cut low, Feedback high" set="dark">
    With High cut open a long trail keeps its top on every repeat. Lower High cut and each repeat comes back with less top than the last. On this card a trip round the loop costs <Claim id="way-dark">3 dB at 1 kHz and 15.3 dB at 4 kHz</Claim>, so what a note has at 1 kHz goes on for many repeats while what it has at 4 kHz is 60 dB down in four. After the card, raise High cut and listen for the repeats coming forward.
  </Way>
  <Way title="From side to side" tag="Ping pong at its highest" set="sides">
    With Ping pong at its highest each hit is answered on the left, then on the right, then on the left again. The two waves of stage 5 show the turns. It widens a part that stands in the middle without moving the part itself. After the card, bring Ping pong half way down and notice the repeats close in towards the middle.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Three heads" tag="Heads at Three, Feedback low" set="three">
    Three heads make a small rhythm out of one hit. On this card a click comes back <Claim id="way-three">at 250 ms, 500 ms and 750 ms</Claim>. At the bench's tempo those are an eighth, a quarter and a dotted quarter after it. Feedback is kept low, because the sum of all three heads goes round again and a high setting fills every gap. After the card, try the other choices of Heads.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A worn transport" tag="Wow and Flutter up, Drive up, High cut low" set="worn">
    Wow and Flutter make every repeat a little out of tune with the playing, differently each time. On this card the repeats move <Claim id="way-worn">between 8.7 cents flat and 6.8 cents sharp</Claim> over the loop. Against chords that hold still, listen for a slow shimmer. Drive takes the peaks off the trail and a low High cut takes its top. After the card, take Wow and Flutter to 0 and notice how fixed the same trail sounds.
  </Way>
  <Way title="A trail that builds" tag="Feedback over 1, Drive at its highest" set="swell">
    Above a Feedback of 1 each repeat is recorded louder than the last, and the trail swells until the ceiling of stage 6 holds it. With Drive at its highest the ceiling is low, so the swell stays under the playing. On this card <Claim id="way-swell">the heads read 3.0 dB more than the line going in, and the largest sample on the tape is 0.25</Claim>. The trail is held at that height and cannot run away. After the card, bring Feedback under 1 and listen to it drain.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Turn Time while it plays" tag="Time, moved half way through the loop" set="dive">
    Half way through the loop this card shortens Time. The motor of stage 1 follows, the head races along the tape, and everything already recorded is read fast. <Claim id="way-dive">The tape starts at 2.67 times its speed, 17 semitones up</Claim>, and falls back to pitch as the motor settles. At the start of the loop Time is turned back, by more than the motor's time constant: the tape runs backwards for a moment, stops, and comes up to pitch from below. After the card, watch the two traces of stages 1 and 2 while it plays.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Six ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="A trail that takes over" set="runaway">
    Feedback above 1 with little Drive lets the trail grow until the ceiling stops it, and with little Drive the ceiling is high. On the drums <Claim id="fault-runaway">the heads read 7.0 dB more than the drums going in, and the largest sample on the tape is 0.52</Claim>. Every hit is still going round, bent by the ceiling, at a level over the drums themselves. The cure is Feedback under 1. If the swell is wanted, raise Drive and lower Mix, as the sixth card of part 6 does.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The echo buries the part" set="mud">
    A busy sound, a high Feedback, filters wide open and Mix at a half. Each repeat keeps all of its lows and lands off the beat. In the output <Claim id="fault-mud">the echoes stand 4.5 dB over the mix itself</Claim>. Take them in this order: lower Mix, then lower Feedback, then raise Low cut, then set Time to a note of the part.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Too short to be an echo" set="buzz">
    With Time at its shortest a click comes back <Claim id="fault-buzz">33 times in a second, the first after 1,440 samples</Claim>. Listen for repeats that close running together into a buzz, with a pitch at the rate of the repeats. A delay this short, on anything but a click, is the subject of <Lesson id="chorus-and-flanging" />. For an echo, lengthen Time until the repeats come apart.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A click when Time is moved">
    A head that jumped to a new place would cut from one part of the wave to another in a single sample: the click of step 7 of the build. This device never jumps, since the motor of stage 1 carries the head there, and the price is the bend in pitch of stage 2. A delay that must change length without a bend has to fade from one head to another, which is where <Lesson id="pitch-shifting" /> begins.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The top comes and goes">
    A read between two samples loses some of the top, by an amount that depends on where between them it lands, as stage 3 measures. A head that wanders passes through every place in turn, so the top of the repeats rises and falls a little with Wow. It is small with this read and larger with a linear one.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A trail that never quite ends">
    A loop with a gain under 1 gets smaller for ever and never arrives at nothing. A processor slows down on numbers that small, and a device that never rests costs time while it plays silence. This one does two things about it. What it records is <Src path="cpp/common/dsp_util.h" at="return (x > -kDenormalThreshold && x < kDenormalThreshold) ? 0.0f : x;">set to exactly zero</Src> once it is too small to matter. And once nothing is going in and its output has stayed under <Src path="cpp/kit/idle.h" at="static constexpr float kFloor = 1.0e-7f;">a ten-millionth of full scale</Src> for <Src path="cpp/devices/tape-echo/tape_echo.h" at="idle_.reset(sr, kParamMax[kTime] * 0.001f + 0.5f);">the longest Time and half a second more</Src>, it sleeps: it stops working and puts out exact zeros. Fed silence from the start, it leaves <Claim id="silence">nothing on the tape and exact silence coming out</Claim>.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

<Part id="myths" title="Seven things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="An echo is the same sound, played again.">
    Each repeat is a recording of a recording. On every trip it passes the loop's filters, the ceiling and a read between samples. The spectra of stage 4 and the full-scale click of stage 6 show how much a single trip changes. Only the first repeat of a quiet sound is close to a copy.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Half the Feedback, half the trail.">
    Feedback sets the step from one repeat to the next, and the step is in decibels. At a Feedback of 0.75 a burst loses <Claim id="myth-feedback">2.5 dB</Claim> a repeat, where at 0.5 it loses the step of stage 5. So that trail takes well over twice as many repeats to fall as far. Near the top of the range a small turn changes the length a great deal.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Feedback at 1 repeats for ever, and above 1 it blows up.">
    Neither, in this device. At exactly 1 the filters and the bend of the ceiling still take a little on every trip, and stage 6 has the number. Above 1 the trail grows only until the ceiling holds it. A loop with no ceiling and no filters would do both.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A longer echo wavers more.">
    Wow and Flutter move the head by a length of time that does not depend on Time, and the bend in pitch comes from how fast the head moves. Stage 2 measures the same bend at a short Time and at a long one.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Turning Time only changes the spacing of the repeats.">
    While Time is moving, the head is moving along the tape, and everything it reads changes pitch. The spacing is new only once the motor has settled. Stage 2 has the size of it, and the last card of part 6 plays it.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Mix at one half is half of each.">
    The two gains are a cosine and a sine, so that the power of the whole holds level. At the middle each is the number of stage 7, well over a half. Turning Mix up from 0 brings the echo in faster than it takes the sound away.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A delay line moves its samples along.">
    Nothing in the ring moves. Only two indices advance, and the delay is the distance between them. That is the ring of part 2, and it is why a delay of a second costs no more work than a delay of a millisecond.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
