The one-pole filter of lesson 4 has one memory, and one memory can do one thing: lean towards its input at a fixed rate. It cuts at a gentle 6 dB an octave and it cannot favour any frequency. Give a filter two memories and let each feed the other, and it can do what a weight on a spring does: swing. A filter that can swing cuts twice as steeply, and it can be made to favour the frequency it swings at. That favouring is **resonance**.

The state-variable filter is the plainest way to build one. It is two integrators in a row, with the output of each fed back and taken away from the input. An integrator is a running total: slow movements pile up in it and fast ones average away. What goes into the first integrator is the input with the lows and the middle already taken out, which is a high-pass. After one integrator it is a band-pass. After two it is a low-pass. All three exist at once, at three points of one loop, and the filter costs the same whichever you take.

Two numbers set it. The **cutoff** is how fast the integrators run, and it slides the whole picture along the frequency axis. The resonance, written **Q**, is how little of the band-pass is fed back. That feedback is the loop's brake: the less of it there is, the longer the loop goes on swinging at its cutoff.

## Stage by stage

### 1. Two integrators in a loop

The whole filter is three lines. With <M t="x" /> the input and <M t={String.raw`\omega_c`} /> the cutoff as an angular frequency:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{high} = x - k\cdot\text{band} - \text{low} \qquad \text{band} = \omega_c\!\int \text{high}\;dt \qquad \text{low} = \omega_c\!\int \text{band}\;dt`} />

An integrator's gain is <M t={String.raw`f_c / f`} />: large for slow signals, 1 at the cutoff, small for fast ones, falling 6 dB for every octave. Far below the cutoff both integrators have gain to spare, and a feedback loop with gain to spare forces its output to match its input: low follows <M t="x" />, and high, which is what is left over, is next to nothing. Far above the cutoff the integrators pass almost nothing back, so high is <M t="x" /> itself, band is that once integrated and 6 dB an octave down, and low is twice integrated and 12 dB an octave down. Writing <M t={String.raw`w = f / f_c`} /> for frequency in units of the cutoff, the three responses are

<M block t={String.raw`|H_{\text{low}}| = \frac{1}{D} \qquad |H_{\text{band}}| = \frac{k\,w}{D} \qquad |H_{\text{high}}| = \frac{w^2}{D} \qquad\quad D = \sqrt{(1 - w^2)^2 + k^2 w^2}`} />

They share a bottom line and differ only on top: 1, <M t="kw" />, <M t="w^2" />. The band-pass here is the band output times <M t="k" />, which is what the kit's `bandpass()` returns: scaled so that it passes its centre at exactly 0 dB.

### 2. The cutoff: how fast the integrators run

A program cannot integrate; it can only add up samples. The kit uses the trapezoid rule: on each sample the running total grows by the mean of this input and the last, times a gain <M t="g" />. For slow signals that is as good as the real thing, with <M t={String.raw`g = \pi f_c / f_s`} />. But a trapezoid integrator's idea of frequency bends. It treats a sine at frequency <M t="f" /> as an ideal integrator would treat one at <M t={String.raw`(f_s/\pi)\tan(\pi f / f_s)`} />, so the whole endless frequency axis is folded into the span from zero to half the sample rate. Left like that, a cutoff asked for at 8 kHz lands <Claim id="no-tangent">7.9 % flat, at 7.37 kHz</Claim>. The cure is to bend the request the other way first, which is called pre-warping:

<M block t={String.raw`g = \tan\!\left(\frac{\pi f_c}{f_s}\right)`} />

In the code this is the first line of <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="73-80">`Svf::set`</Src>, after the cutoff is kept between 5 Hz and 0.49 of the sample rate. The tangent is <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="44-50">`tan_prewarp`</Src>, which below 0.7 radians (a cutoff of 10.7 kHz at 48 kHz) is not the library's tangent but a ratio of two short polynomials, <M t={String.raw`x(15 - x^2)/(15 - 6x^2)`} />. It is there so that a filter being swept can afford to be retuned on every sample.

With that one tangent the band-pass peaks <Claim id="band-peak">within 0.01 % of the cutoff</Claim>, from 100 Hz to 16 kHz and at every Q tried. At a Q of 0.7071 the low-pass and the high-pass both pass the cutoff at <Claim id="minus-3">−3.01 dB</Claim>, half the power each.

For contrast the lab also runs the loop written the obvious way: each integrator adds <M t={String.raw`2\pi f_c / f_s`} /> times its input to its total, and the feedback is whatever the last sample left behind. Call it the late loop. It errs the other way, and further: at a Q of 4 its peak is <Claim id="late-sharp-4k">4.9 % sharp at 4 kHz</Claim>, most of a semitone, and <Claim id="late-sharp-8k">15 % sharp at 8 kHz</Claim>.

### 3. Resonance: the loop's brake

The band output is how fast the low output is changing, so feeding <M t={String.raw`k\cdot\text{band}`} /> back against the input is friction: a force against the motion, in proportion to its speed. The code keeps <M t={String.raw`k = 1/Q`} />, the damping, and the control is its inverse.

At the cutoff <M t="w = 1" />, the first term of <M t="D" /> is zero, and the low-pass and the high-pass both have gain <M t={String.raw`1/k`} />. **Q is the gain at the cutoff**: <Claim id="peak-is-q">+12.04 dB at a Q of 4 and +20.00 dB at 10</Claim>. The two integrators' feedback cancels there and the brake is all that holds the loop. A Q of <M t={String.raw`1/\sqrt2`} />, 0.7071, is the largest with no bump at all, the flattest pass band two memories can give (the Butterworth response), and it is where the kit and the device both start.

The band-pass peaks on the cutoff whatever the Q. The low-pass peaks a little under it, where <M t="D" /> is smallest: <Claim id="low-peak-under">1.6 % under at a Q of 4</Claim> and <Claim id="low-peak-q2">6.5 % under at a Q of 2</Claim>.

A peak in the response and a ring in time are one fact seen twice. Hit the filter with a single sample and it swings at its cutoff, each swing smaller than the last by a fixed ratio; after <M t="Q" /> cycles the ring is <Claim id="ring-27">27 dB down</Claim>. At 1 kHz and a Q of 0.7071 it is 60 dB under its largest sample in <Claim id="ring-flat">1.8 ms</Claim>, before two cycles are over. At a Q of 10 that takes <Claim id="ring-60">22 cycles</Claim>.

### 4. Solving the loop with no delay

The three lines chase each other. High needs this sample's band and low; band needs this sample's high. The obvious way out is to use last sample's band and low, and that is the late loop of stage 2. At a low cutoff being one sample late does no harm. But one sample is a phase lag that grows with frequency, and in a feedback loop enough lag turns the brake into a push. With a Q of 0.7071 that loop runs away once the cutoff passes <Claim id="late-blows-up">7.9 kHz</Claim>, a sixth of the sample rate. Less brake makes it last longer, not shorter: at a Q of 4 it holds until <Claim id="late-limit-q4">13.5 kHz</Claim>.

The kit does a little algebra instead. A trapezoid integrator's output is <M t="g" /> times its input plus what it remembers, so with memories <M t="s_1" /> and <M t="s_2" /> the loop on one sample is three equations in three unknowns:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{high} = x - k\,\text{band} - \text{low} \qquad \text{band} = g\,\text{high} + s_1 \qquad \text{low} = g\,\text{band} + s_2`} />

Put the first and the third into the second and band stands alone:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{band} = \frac{s_1 + g\,(x - s_2)}{1 + g\,(g + k)} \qquad\quad \text{low} = s_2 + g\,\text{band}`} />

This is <Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="82-91">`Svf::process`</Src>, term for term. The division is done once, in `set`, as `a1 = 1 / (1 + g * (g + k))`, with `a2 = g * a1` and `a3 = g * a2` beside it. Then on each sample `v3 = x - ic2` is the input less the second memory; `v1 = a1 * ic1 + a2 * v3` is band; `v2 = ic2 + a2 * ic1 + a3 * v3` is low, with band already multiplied in; and `high = x - k * v1 - v2` is the first line, now that everything in it is known. Last, each memory steps on by the trapezoid rule, `ic1 = 2 * v1 - ic1` and `ic2 = 2 * v2 - ic2`: twice the new output less the old memory. That is a handful of multiplications and additions a sample, and no tangent.

Because the feedback is this sample's and not last sample's, the digital loop is the pre-warped formula exactly, and that formula has no cutoff and no Q at which it gains energy. With the cutoff anywhere from 20 Hz to 20 kHz and Q from 0.5 to 25, the kit's filter answers a unit sample with <Claim id="kit-stable">nothing larger than the sample that went in</Claim>.

### 5. Moving the cutoff

A filter that only ever stood still could be built several ways. This one is built to be swept, and what makes it good at that is what its two memories hold. They are the integrators' totals, which are near enough the band and low outputs themselves: quantities that mean the same thing at any cutoff. Change <M t="g" /> between two samples and both memories are still right. The next sample integrates at the new rate and that is all.

The other standard way to write the same response is the direct form, which the kit keeps for equalisers that stay put (<Src path="cpp/kit/filters.h" lines="112-128">`Biquad`</Src>, lesson 6). Its two memories are mixtures of past inputs and outputs, weighted by the coefficients, and they mean something only alongside the coefficients that made them. Standing still, the two forms are the same filter: on the noise their low-passes differ by <Claim id="direct-still">more than 100 dB under the signal</Claim>. Move the cutoff and the direct form has to recover from memories that no longer fit.

In the device the cutoff is a sum in octaves, so a sweep covers the same musical distance up as down (<Src path="cpp/devices/auto-filter/auto_filter.h" lines="137-178">the top of its sample loop</Src>):

<M block t={String.raw`f_c = \text{Cutoff}\cdot 2^{\,5\,\cdot\,\text{Env}\,\cdot\,\text{envelope}\;+\;3\,\cdot\,\text{Osc}\,\cdot\,\text{oscillator}}`} />

The envelope is the louder channel's size, followed by a one-pole (lesson 4) with an attack of 10 ms and a release of 200 ms: at full amount, a full-scale sound opens the filter five octaves. The oscillator runs free between −1 and 1 and at full amount swings the cutoff three octaves each way; the lab offers four of its six shapes. The sum is taken on every sample, and the coefficients are made again on every sample where it has changed.

### 6. Notch and peak: sums of the three

Since the three outputs are parts of the input, adding some of them back gives more shapes for no more filtering. Low plus high is everything but the band, a **notch**:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{notch} = \text{low} + \text{high} = x - k\cdot\text{band} \qquad\qquad \text{peak} = \text{low} - \text{high}`} />

At the cutoff low and high are the same size, and one is a quarter of a cycle behind the input and the other a quarter ahead, so they are opposite and their sum is nothing: the kit's notch is <Claim id="notch-depth">more than 100 dB down</Claim> at the cutoff. Q sets how wide the hole is: an octave under the cutoff the notch has taken off <Claim id="notch-width">2.75 dB at a Q of 0.7071 and 0.21 dB at a Q of 3</Claim>. Take the difference and the two opposites add: the **peak** is <M t="2Q" /> at the cutoff, <Claim id="peak-2q">+15.6 dB at a Q of 3</Claim>, and 0 dB far away on both sides. It is not the bell of an equaliser (lesson 6): its height cannot be set apart from its width, and the sound far above the cutoff comes out upside down.

The kit's `notch()` is the one line `low + high`, and the lab ticks the filter with it. The kit has no peak. The device has both, as rows of <Src path="cpp/devices/auto-filter/auto_filter.h" lines="212-217">a table of weights</Src> for low, <M t="k" /> times band, and high: (1, 0, 0) for the low-pass, (0, 0, 1) for the high-pass, (0, 1, 0) for the band-pass, (1, 0, 1) for the notch and (1, 0, −1) for the peak. Changing the type slides the three weights to their new row over 10 ms, so the switch does not click.

### 7. The device: the same loop at twice the rate

The Auto Filter's stage is <Src path="cpp/devices/auto-filter/SvfTptTone.h" lines="51-87">its own code</Src>, with the coefficients in a struct apart from the memories, so both channels share one set, and with the library's tangent in place of the kit's short cut. The lab runs that stage beside the kit's, at the kit's rate and on the same cutoff. At 1 kHz the check reads <Claim id="same-stage">its floor, −140 dB</Claim>: the two cannot be told apart. At 8 kHz and a Q of 4 they are <Claim id="same-stage-8k">82 dB apart</Claim>, which is the size of the short cut's error.

<Check id="sameStage" />

What the device adds is around the stage (<Src path="cpp/devices/auto-filter/auto_filter.h" lines="185-195">the wet path</Src> and <Src path="cpp/devices/auto-filter/auto_filter.h" lines="308-320">`filter`</Src>):

- **Twice the rate.** The stage runs between two of <Src path="cpp/kit/oversample.h">the kit's half-band converters</Src>, so its frequency axis bends towards 48 kHz and not 24 kHz, and the audible band is nearly straight. A 1 kHz low-pass is at <Claim id="device-top">−54.8 dB at 20 kHz in the device and −70.2 dB in one stage at 48 kHz</Claim>; a true 12 dB an octave would be −52 dB. The converters cost <Claim id="latency">31 samples</Claim> of delay.
- **24 dB an octave.** Two stages in a row, each at <M t={String.raw`\sqrt Q`} />, so the gain at the cutoff is still <M t="Q" />: <Claim id="q-both-slopes">+20.0 dB at a Q of 10 on either slope</Claim>. With the cutoff at 250 Hz the pair falls <Claim id="slope-24">24.3 dB between 1 and 2 kHz</Claim>.
- **Drive.** The input is turned up and a tanh is put on both memories of each stage, in full from 3 dB of drive. Half of that gain, in decibels, is taken off again at the output.

## What goes wrong

**The top octave bends.** Pre-warping puts the cutoff in the right place and nothing else. Every other frequency is still squeezed towards half the sample rate, so at 48 kHz a low-pass falls faster than it should as it gets there: the kit's filter at 1 kHz drops <Claim id="warp-slope">13.3 dB between 4 and 8 kHz</Claim>, not 12. For a high-pass on a detector that does not matter. For a filter that is the sound, the device pays for twice the rate.

**Resonance is gain.** A tone on the cutoff comes out <M t="Q" /> times as large: at a Q of 25, <Claim id="gain-q">28.0 dB up</Claim>. A full-scale tone would leave the kit's filter at 25 times full scale. The kit's `Svf` has no guard, which is why devices built on it keep Q modest. The Auto Filter's guard is its drive: the tanh holds each memory inside ±1, so the loop cannot store more than that however long it is pushed. With 6 dB of drive that full-scale tone at a Q of 25 comes out at <Claim id="drive-caps">0.24 of full scale</Claim>.

**A jump is still a jump.** The state-variable form does not thump when its cutoff moves, but the sound on either side of a jump is two different sounds, and the join can click. The device slides every knob over 5 ms and the type and the slope over 10 ms. Its oscillator is not smoothed: a square is meant to snap.

**Rings that never quite end.** A ring dies away by a fixed ratio for ever, into numbers so small that some processors handle them very slowly. Both memories are passed through `flush_denormal`, which calls anything under 10⁻¹⁵ zero, so a filter left alone reaches exact silence.

**Late.** The device's output is 31 samples behind its input. The stages from the kit's filter in this lesson are not delayed at all, so the output drawn against the input sits two thirds of a millisecond to the right.
