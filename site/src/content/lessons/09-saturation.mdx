A saturator has no memory. It takes one sample, finds where that number falls on a fixed curve, and puts out what the curve says. The curve is a straight line near zero and bends over towards a ceiling as the number grows, so a quiet sound passes as it was and a loud one is held in.

Bending a wave changes its shape, and a wave of another shape has other frequencies in it. A sine goes in and comes out with *harmonics*: sines at whole multiples of its frequency, heard as grit or bite according to which ones there are and how strong. **Drive** is nothing more than gain before the curve. It decides how far round the bend the sound is pushed.

The trouble is that the curve knows nothing of the sample rate. Its harmonics go on upwards without end, and those above half the sample rate have nowhere to go: they fold back down (lesson 1) and land between the true harmonics as tones that belong to no note. So the device runs its curve at two or four times the rate, where there is room above, and filters before it comes back down. Beside that it has a second remedy, which changes what is computed in place of the curve.

## Stage by stage

### 1. Drive: gain before the curve

The curve never changes. What changes is how large the signal is when it meets it:

<M block t={String.raw`u[n] = g\,x[n], \qquad g = 10^{D/20}`} />

<M t="D" /> is the drive in decibels, from 0 to 36, so <M t="g" /> runs from 1 to 63. In <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/saturator.h">`saturator.h`</Src> it is `gain_`, worked out in `set_drive` and ramped over 5 ms when the control moves. The device does not keep <M t="u" />, so the lab multiplies the input by the device's own `gain_`; a check in stage 2 holds that to the device.

Seen from outside, drive turns one curve into a family of them. The figure is measured, not drawn from a formula: a steady level is held at each of 257 heights through a copy of the device at the base rate, and what comes out is read once it has stopped moving.

  One sample out against one sample in, at the drive set above. The dashed diagonal is no change. More drive steepens the middle and brings the bend closer to zero. While the loop plays, the dot rides the curve at the height the input's peaks reach.

Near zero every curve the device has is the diagonal times <M t="g" />: each passes through the origin with a slope of exactly one. A sine 30 dB under full scale with 6 dB of drive comes out of the Soft curve as the input doubled; the difference between the two is <Claim id="low-drive">59 dB under the signal</Claim>. (Tape manages <Claim id="tape-null">38.5 dB</Claim>, and not because of its curve: the low-pass that follows it holds the sound back by a fraction of a sample.) Saturation is a matter of level. The setting that leaves a quiet passage alone squares off a loud one.

### 2. The curve: five ways to bend

The device has five curves, in <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/Waveshapers.h">`Waveshapers.h`</Src>. Each takes the driven sample <M t="u" /> and stays inside ±1:

- **Soft**, <M t={String.raw`\tanh u`} />: bends from the first, and never quite arrives.
- **Hard**, <M t={String.raw`\max(-1, \min(1, u))`} />: straight, then flat, with a corner between.
- **Tube**, <M t={String.raw`\tanh u`} /> above zero and <M t={String.raw`\tanh(1.5u)/1.5`} /> below: the lower half gives way earlier and stops at −⅔.
- **Tape**, <M t={String.raw`u/\sqrt{1+u^2}`} />: the slowest of the soft ones to arrive. The device follows it with a one-pole low-pass that closes from 16 kHz to 6 kHz as the drive rises.
- **Fold**, a triangle wave in <M t="u" />: past ±1 the curve turns round and comes back.

<Curve taps="soft:out hard:ctl tube:alt tape:alt2 fold:alt3" xTitle="driven sample" yTitle="sample out" min={-1.15} max={1.15} height={280}>
  The five curves as the device computes them, over four times full scale each way. All five leave zero along the same line. Tube is the only one whose two halves differ.
</Curve>

Why a bend makes harmonics shows in the first terms of the Soft curve's series. Put a sine of height <M t="A" /> through it and use <M t={String.raw`\sin^3\theta = \tfrac14(3\sin\theta - \sin 3\theta)`} />:

<M block t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} \tanh u &= u - \frac{u^3}{3} + \dots \\[4pt] \tanh(A\sin\theta) &\approx \Bigl(A - \frac{A^3}{4}\Bigr)\sin\theta + \frac{A^3}{12}\sin 3\theta \end{aligned}`} />

Two things happened. The sine came out smaller than it went in, by <M t={String.raw`A^3/4`} />: that is the holding in. And a new sine appeared at three times the frequency. The term in <M t={String.raw`u^5`} /> adds a fifth harmonic, and so on upwards. With the peaks at one half, the third harmonic measures <Claim id="third-small">34 dB under the fundamental</Claim>, half a decibel from what that first term alone predicts.

Odd powers of a sine hold only odd multiples of its frequency. A curve that treats both signs alike, <M t={String.raw`f(-u) = -f(u)`} />, has only odd powers, so it makes **only odd harmonics**. The engine takes care to keep this exact: its `tanhExp` is built from one exponential of <M t={String.raw`|u|`} /> with the sign put back afterwards. Through Soft, Hard, Tape and Fold, every even harmonic of a 1 kHz sine stays <Claim id="odd-only">more than 120 dB under the fundamental</Claim>, which is rounding and nothing else.

An even power does something different: <M t={String.raw`\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\theta)`} />, a harmonic at twice the frequency and a constant. Tube has even powers because its halves differ. On a sine 12 dB under full scale at 18 dB of drive, its second harmonic stands <Claim id="tube-second">22.5 dB under the fundamental</Claim>, and the constant lifts the whole wave by <Claim id="tube-offset">0.097</Claim>. **Bias** gets the same from any curve by moving the signal off centre, and <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/saturator.h">`shaped`</Src> takes the curve's own value at the bias off again, so that silence in is <Claim id="silence">exact silence out</Claim>:

<M block t={String.raw`y = f(u + b) - f(b)`} />

The constant the *signal* makes is still there. **DC Block** removes it by following the output with a 10 Hz low-pass and subtracting what that finds; with it on, Tube's offset falls <Claim id="dc-blocked">below one millionth</Claim>.

### 3. At the base rate: what lands above 24 kHz comes back

A corner in a wave needs harmonics without end, and they die away slowly. At 48 kHz nothing above 24 kHz can be written down. A partial the curve asks for at frequency <M t="f" /> comes out at its distance from the nearest multiple of the sample rate (lesson 1):

<M block t={String.raw`f_{\text{heard}} = \bigl|\,f - 48\,\text{kHz}\cdot\operatorname{round}(f / 48\,\text{kHz})\,\bigr|`} />

Take a sine at 5 kHz. Its third harmonic, 15 kHz, is fine. The fifth, 25 kHz, comes back at 23. The seventh, 35 kHz, at 13. The ninth, 45 kHz, at 3. None of those is a multiple of 5 kHz, and when the note goes up they go down.

To show it, the lab has a probe tone of its own: a sine 6 dB under full scale, through a copy of the device at the base rate with the curve, drive and bias set here. The tone and everything it can fold to repeat exactly within the stretch measured, so the lab can take the tone and its true harmonics out with nothing spilt. What is left is the folded partials alone.

### 4. Oversampling: the same curve with room above it

Run the curve at 192 kHz and its harmonics have until 96 kHz before they fold, and what folds lands first in the empty band above the audible one, where a filter can remove it. Only a partial within 24 kHz of a multiple of the higher rate still lands in the sound: for the 5 kHz probe at 4x, the 35th harmonic where it was the 5th. The three paths are side by side in <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/saturator.h">`run`</Src>.

**Up.** To double the rate, put a zero between every two samples and low-pass at the old half rate. The zeros make a mirror image of the spectrum above 24 kHz; the filter removes it, which is the same as filling in the zeros with the values that belong between. <Src path="cpp/kit/oversample.h">The kit's `Halfband2x`</Src> does this with a *half-band* filter: a low-pass whose corner is exactly a quarter of its own rate, which makes every second coefficient zero and the middle one a half. Of its 63 taps only 16 pairs are multiplied. In `up`, one of each two outputs is the input itself and the other is the sample half way, worked out from the 32 inputs around it.

**Down.** The same filter, then keep every second sample. `down` works out only the ones it keeps.

For 4x the device nests a second pair inside the first, <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/halfband.h">`Halfband<8>`</Src> with 31 taps. It can be short because after the first doubling the sound fills only the bottom quarter of its band, and the filter has from 28 kHz to 68 kHz to fall.

The inner pair costs 15 at the doubled rate, and one more is added to make a whole number at the base rate: 8. The device takes those <Claim id="latency">39 samples</Claim> at every setting. It feeds the 2x path 8 samples late and the 1x path 39, so switching never moves the sound, and the dry signal that Mix blends in is delayed once.

### 5. The antiderivative: the mean of the curve between two samples

The other remedy leaves the rate alone and changes the sum. In place of the curve's value at this sample, <Src path="cpp/devices/saturator/Adaa.h">`Adaa1`</Src> puts out the curve's *mean* along the straight line from the last sample to this one. With <M t="F" /> the antiderivative of the curve, <M t={String.raw`F' = f`} />, that mean needs no integration:

<M block t={String.raw`y[n] = \frac{F(v[n]) - F(v[n-1])}{v[n] - v[n-1]}, \qquad v = u + b`} />

Think of it as joining the samples with straight lines, bending that continuous line, and averaging over one sample's time before sampling again. The averaging is a low-pass applied *before* the sampling, while the high harmonics are still where they belong. A plain curve also throws away *when* between two samples the wave crossed the corner; the mean keeps it.

Term by term in the code: `F0` is <M t={String.raw`F(v[n])`} />, `F1` the one kept from last time, `delta` the difference below the line. When `delta` is under `kMinDelta`, one ten-thousandth, the division is nought over nought and the code takes the curve at the midpoint. All of it is in double precision, since it subtracts two nearly equal numbers. The device has antiderivatives for two curves only: <M t={String.raw`\ln\cosh v`} /> for Soft, written as <M t={String.raw`|v| + \ln(1 + e^{-2|v|}) - \ln 2`} /> so it cannot overflow, and <M t={String.raw`v^2/2`} /> inside ±1 with <M t={String.raw`|v| - \tfrac12`} /> outside for Hard. On Tube, Tape and Fold the switch does nothing.

The price is treble. Where the curve is straight, <M t={String.raw`F = v^2/2`} /> and the mean is <M t={String.raw`\tfrac12(v[n] + v[n-1])`} />: the average of two samples, which is a low-pass, and half a sample late. At 1x a quiet signal loses <Claim id="droop-1x">2 dB at 10 kHz and 11.7 dB at 20 kHz</Claim>. At 4x the two samples are a quarter as far apart and the loss at 20 kHz is <Claim id="droop-4x">0.5 dB</Claim>.

### 6. Output and mix

Drive makes the sound louder as well as rougher, so **Output** is a gain on the bent signal to take that off again, and **Mix** blends it with the input, delayed by the same 39 samples:

<M block t={String.raw`\text{out} = \text{dry} + m\,\bigl(g_{\text{out}}\,y - \text{dry}\bigr)`} />

The crossfade is linear because the two are the same sound, lined up; an equal-power one would add 3 dB half way. With the mix at 0 the output is <Claim id="mix-zero">the input, sample for sample</Claim>. The device also has a Tone control, a tilt about 1 kHz between the curve and Output, which this lab leaves flat.

## What goes wrong

**A square wave is never cured.** The more drive, the nearer every curve comes to a hard corner, whose harmonics fall away only as one over their number. At 36 dB on the Soft curve, 4x brings the probe's folded partials down by <Claim id="hardest-4x">16 dB</Claim>, not 88; with the antiderivative as well, by <Claim id="hardest-both">40 dB</Claim>.

**The filter's slope is part of the sound.** A filter that is flat to 20 kHz cannot be all the way down by 24. The probe's fifth harmonic, 25 kHz, sits on that slope, so at 4x it still comes back at 23 kHz, <Claim id="slope">30 dB under full scale</Claim> where it was 18 under at 1x. It is above what most people hear, and it is why the counts above stop at 20 kHz.

**Over the ceiling.** A squared-off wave with its top harmonics filtered away is no longer flat on top. At 1x the Hard curve's output peaks at exactly 1; at 4x, on the saw at 18 dB of drive, it peaks <Claim id="overshoot">1.1 dB over</Claim>, and on the drums at 36 dB, <Claim id="overshoot-drums">2.4 dB over</Claim>. The device leaves it, because clipping it again at the base rate would put the folding back.

**Latency.** 39 samples is 0.8 ms. A host that lines its tracks up by reported latency hides it; a player monitoring through the device does not.

**Louder is not better.** Drive raises the level, and louder sounds better for a moment. Bring Output down until the two match before judging the curve.

## What to try

- The sine, **Curve** on Hard, **Oversampling** at 1x, and sweep **Drive** from 0 to 36 dB while listening to the third stage. Then the same at 4x on the fourth.
- The saw at 36 dB of drive: Listen to stage 3 and stage 4 in turn. The fizz that is in one and not the other is the folding.
- The probe figure in stage 3, with the **Probe tone** swept from 1 kHz to 10 kHz. Watch which way each red peak moves.
- **Curve** on Tube with **DC Block** off, then on, and the wave in stage 2.
- **Curve** on Fold on the guitar, with **Drive** near 12 dB: each note changes colour as it dies away and its peaks cross fewer folds.
- Hard, 1x, **Antiderivative** on: flip between the plain and the mean in the last figure of stage 5, then go to 4x.
- The drums with **Mix** at 0.3 and **Drive** at 24 dB: the hits keep their fronts and the bent copy fills in behind them.
