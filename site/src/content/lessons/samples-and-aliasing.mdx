<Part id="why" title="A computer cannot hold a wave. It holds numbers.">

A sound in the air is a pressure that rises and falls smoothly. A microphone turns it into a voltage that rises and falls the same way. A computer cannot store "smoothly". It stores numbers. So the wave is measured at even instants, and each measurement is kept as one number. That number is a _sample_.

How often the wave is measured is the _sample rate_. Every lab on this site runs at <Src path="site/labs/lab.h" at="constexpr float kSampleRate = 48000.0f;">48,000 samples a second</Src>. The loop this page plays is two seconds long, so it is <Claim id="loop-samples">96,000 samples</Claim> and nothing else. Everything the engine does to sound, it does to lists like that one.

Start with the plainest sound there is, a sine wave: one pitch and nothing more. Here is a little of one as the engine stores it.

<Still
  taps="sine"
  from={0}
  span={0.002}
  caption="Four cycles of a sine wave as it is stored. Each cycle is two dozen numbers, and the picture joins them with straight lines so that the eye can follow them. Nothing is kept between one number and the next."
/>

A sample says how high the wave stood at one instant. It says nothing about the time until the next one. That looks like a great deal thrown away, and the surprise of this lesson is how little is lost. As long as the wave holds no frequency as high as half the sample rate, exactly one such wave passes through the samples, and it can be drawn back whole.

Half the sample rate has a name, the _Nyquist frequency_. Half of 48,000 is 24,000, so on this page it is 24 kHz, above the highest pitch a person hears. The reason for the half is simple. A wave goes up and comes down once in every cycle, and to catch both, a cycle needs more than two samples. A wave at exactly half the rate gets two. A faster one gets fewer.

So here is the question this lesson starts from. What comes out when a sound is asked to go higher than half the rate?

Measured on the loop, the sine <Claim id="sweep-turns">climbs for a second to 24 kHz and falls for a second</Claim>. <Claim id="sweep-mirror">Half a second in it was asked for 12 kHz, and it stands at 12 kHz. A second later it was asked for 36 kHz, and it stands at 12 kHz again</Claim>.

Nothing was dropped and nothing broke. The oscillator did as it was told: no line of its code knows about half the rate. But the samples of a sine at 36 kHz are also samples that a sine at 12 kHz could have left, and a loudspeaker is given the lowest wave that fits them. Part 2 shows why.

</Predict>

That is _aliasing_: one frequency going by the name of another. The frequency that was asked for is said to _fold_ about half the sample rate, like a strip of paper folded there.

### A bright sound is full of frequencies that do not fit

Nobody asks an oscillator for a note at 36 kHz. The trouble arrives another way. A sine is the only wave with one frequency in it. Every other repeating shape is a stack of sines, called its _partials_: one at the note, one at twice the note, one at three times it, and on up. The sharper the corners of the shape, the further up the stack goes.

A _saw wave_ climbs in a straight line and drops back in no time at all. It is the bright, buzzing wave under most synthesiser strings and brass, and its stack has no top. The plain way to make one is to work that shape out, a sample at a time. This lesson calls the result the naive saw. The picture at the head of this page draws two saws across the whole loop, and at that scale each is a solid band with nothing to tell them apart. What tells them apart is a _spectrum_: frequency runs along the bottom, and a peak stands wherever the sound holds a tone. Here is the naive saw on a high note, <Src path="site/labs/samples-and-aliasing.cpp" at={'lab::Control note{"note", "Note"'}>3,840 Hz</Src>.

### Why it folds

A sine measured at even instants is this list:

<M block t={String.raw`x[n] = \sin\!\left(2\pi\,\frac{f}{f_s}\,n\right)`} />

<M t="f" /> is the frequency asked for, <M t="f_s" /> the sample rate, and <M t="n" /> counts the samples: 0, 1, 2 and on. The only thing in the list besides the count is the fraction <M t={String.raw`f/f_s`} />, which is how much of a cycle goes by between one sample and the next.

Now add one whole cycle to that fraction. No sample changes, because the sine of an angle and the sine of that angle plus a full turn are the same number. So <M t="f" /> and <M t={String.raw`f - f_s`} /> leave the same samples. And a sine at a negative frequency is the same sine upside down. So 30 kHz leaves the samples of 18 kHz below zero, which are the samples of 18 kHz <Claim id="fold-same-samples">with the sign turned</Claim>, number for number.

Put as a rule, a frequency comes out at its distance from the nearest multiple of the sample rate:

<M
  block
  t={String.raw`f_{\text{out}} = \left|\, f - f_s \cdot \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right) \right|`}
/>

Read it against the spectrum above. <M t={String.raw`\operatorname{round}(f/f_s)`} /> is the nearest whole number of sample rates. Up to half the rate that number is zero, the rule gives back <M t="f" />, and the peak stands on the upright. From half the rate to the rate itself the nearest multiple is the rate, and the distance to it shrinks as <M t="f" /> grows: the peak walks back down while the upright walks up. <Claim id="fold-47k">At 47 kHz the sine comes out at 1 kHz</Claim>.

Drawn against the frequency asked for, the rule is a zigzag. The lab measured it as well: the kit's sine at <Src path="site/labs/samples-and-aliasing.cpp" at="constexpr int kFoldPoints = 48;">48 frequencies</Src>, each put through a Fourier transform (<Lesson id="fourier-transform" />) and its peak found.

The rule keeps no record of where a frequency came from. Once a partial has landed at 18 kHz it is a tone at 18 kHz like any other, and nothing after it can tell that it was asked for at 30. So the cure has to come before the samples are made. That is the subject of everything below.

</Part>

<Part id="build" title="Build the oscillator: a phase, a ramp and two small corrections.">

The kit's shaped oscillator is a class called <Src path="cpp/kit/osc.h" at="class BlepOsc {">`BlepOsc`</Src>. This part builds it again from nothing, in five pieces. After each piece the pictures are drawn from what has been built so far, "Hear it so far" plays it, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures.

Two of the steps are wrong on purpose. Hearing what is wrong with them is how the piece after each earns its place.

<Build>
  <Step title="Start with a phase that wraps" controls="note">
    An oscillator has to know where in its cycle it is. That is the _phase_: a number from 0 to 1, where 0 is the start of a cycle and 1 is the start of the next. On each sample it moves on by the same step, which is the note's frequency divided by the sample rate. When it reaches 1 it drops back by exactly 1, so the part that went over is kept and the phase does not lose its place.

    The first line of the listing keeps the step under half a cycle, as the kit does: a shaped wave is not asked for a note above half the rate.

    A phase is not yet a sound. To hear it, this step reads the kit's sine table at the phase, which gives a pure tone at the note. Stage 2 of part 5 opens the table.

    This is the naive saw of part 1, and it brings part 1's fault with it. The step before was one clean line in the spectrum. <Claim id="build-ramp">This one has a line at 1,920 Hz that reads −21.9 dB</Claim>, and with the piece taken out again nothing stands there.

    Glide now has something to show. It bends the note up and back over the loop. Raise it and press "Hear it so far", and listen for tones that slide down while the note slides up.

    />

    <Wave slot="picture" taps="built" stems from={0.5} span={0.001}>

      that the drop falls between two dots, at a different place in each cycle.
    </Wave>

    <Spectrum slot="picture" taps="built" linear min={-100} max={6}>
      The spectrum of the build, on an even axis up to half the sample rate. Notice twelve tall
      peaks where a saw on this note has six. Every second one, counting from the tallest, is the
      saw's own. The six between them are not, and nor is the short one at the left edge.
    </Spectrum>

  </Step>
  <Step title="Spread the drop over two samples" controls="note glide">
    The ramp is not the trouble. The drop is. It happens between two samples, in no time, and a change that takes no time holds every frequency there is.

    The cure is to let the drop take a little time. This piece looks at each sample and asks whether a drop lies within one sample of it. If one does, the sample is moved towards the middle of the drop, by more the nearer the drop is. Every other sample is left as it was. The amounts are not guessed: they are what the samples would have been if the saw on paper had been smoothed over two samples' width before it was sampled. Stage 4 of part 5 works them out.

    The line at 1,920 Hz now reads <Claim id="build-step">−76.0 dB</Claim>. Take the piece out again and it is back where it was.

    <Listing
      path="site/labs/samples-and-aliasing.cpp"

  <Fragment slot="readback">
    Read it against the picture. <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} /> is the phase, and each dot is one value of it. <M t={String.raw`\Delta`} /> is the step from one dot to the next: the note's frequency over the sample rate, the same fraction <M t={String.raw`f/f_s`} /> that part 2's list of samples turned on.     The line that sets the step also keeps it under 0.49 of a cycle. So a shape from this class has its fundamental under half the rate whatever note it is asked for.

  </Fragment>

<StageBlock tap="naive" n={3} title="The ramp: a saw, the naive way">
  The kit's saw is one line of code with three terms. The first two are the naive saw of part 4: the phase doubled, less one. The kit never puts those two out by themselves, so the lab reads them off the kit's phase, and this stage is marked as written for the lesson.

  <Controls ids="note" />

  <Spectrum taps="naive:in" linear min={-100} max={6}>
    The spectrum of the naive saw, on an even axis up to half the sample rate. Try Note a little lower,
    and notice the peaks between the saw's own partials break up into many: the folds stop sharing
    six places.
  </Spectrum>

  <M slot="equation" block t={String.raw`\sig{in}{s[n]} = \at{saw-sum}{2\,\varphi[n] - 1}`} />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t="s" /> is the ramp and <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} /> the phase of stage 1. Doubling stretches the phase from a height of one to a height of two, and taking one off centres it on zero.

    As a sum of sines a saw is every multiple of its frequency at once, partial <M t="k" /> at one <M t="k" />th the size of the first. <Lesson id="fourier-transform" cap /> shows why any repeating wave is such a sum.

    <M
      block
      t={String.raw`2\varphi - 1 = -\frac{2}{\pi} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(2\pi k \varphi)}{k}`}
    />

    Measured one partial at a time, the naive saw has exactly those sizes: <Claim id="naive-levels">−3.9 dB at the fundamental and 6 dB less at twice it</Claim>. The sum has no last term, because the drop takes no time at all. On paper that is harmless. In samples, every partial above half the rate folds by the rule of part 2.

    At the note the lab starts on, six partials fit under half the rate. The thirteenth lands in the same place from the other side of the sample rate, and the ones after it climb back up the same six places.

    So in the loop as it sounds, each of those places holds the sum of everything that lands there. <Claim id="naive-lines">There are six of them between the saw's partials, from 1,920 Hz up to 21,120 Hz, and each reads between −19.7 and −21.9 dB</Claim>.

  </Fragment>

<StageBlock tap="correction" n={4} title="The step correction: two samples at each drop">
  The third term of that line is the cure. `blep`, short for band-limited step, is handed the phase and the step. It asks how far the nearest drop is, counted in samples, and answers with a piece of a parabola. Everywhere else it answers zero.

  <Controls ids="note" />

  <SamplesOnWaves
    waves="edgeIdeal:in edgeSmooth:out"
    samples="edgeNaive:in edgeKit:out"
    xTitle="samples from the drop"
  >
    The samples around one drop. The saw as drawn on paper carries the naive samples, and the saw
    with its edge spread carries the kit's. Notice that the two sets of dots part at two samples
    only, the ones either side of the drop.
  </SamplesOnWaves>

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{c[n]} = \begin{cases} \at{blep-after}{\phantom{-}(1 - d)^2} & 0 \le d < 1 \\[6pt] \at{blep-before}{-(1 + d)^2} & -1 < d < 0 \\[6pt] \phantom{-}0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}`}
  />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t="c" /> is what is added to the ramp, and <M t="d" /> is the time since the nearest drop, in samples: the bottom axis of the picture. Just after a drop the code finds it as the phase over the step, <M t={String.raw`\varphi/\Delta`} />. Just before one it is <M t={String.raw`(\varphi - 1)/\Delta`} />, which is below zero.

    - **The first row** is a sample that has just passed a drop. It sits at the bottom of the ramp and is lifted. The lift is 1, to the middle of the drop, when the sample falls on the drop itself, and nothing a whole sample later.
    - **The second row** is a sample with a drop just ahead. It sits at the top of the ramp and is lowered, by the mirror of the same curve.
    - **The last row** is every other sample. No drop is beside it, and the ramp's value stands.

    The code returns these with the sign turned, because the saw's line takes `blep` away where this page adds <M t="c" />.

    The two parabolas come from one idea. Take the saw on paper and replace each instant of it by a weighted average of the time around it, two samples wide. The weight is a triangle: most at the instant, and none a sample either side. A straight ramp averaged evenly about each of its points is the same ramp. A drop becomes the running total of a triangle, which is one parabola curving away and another curving back. What `blep` returns is that curve less the bare drop, and it is zero everywhere but beside the drop.

    In the picture above the drop falls <Src path="site/labs/samples-and-aliasing.cpp" at="constexpr float kPast = 0.65f;">0.65 of a sample before sample 0</Src>. The sample before it is <Claim id="edge-pull">pulled down by 0.42 and the one after it pulled up by 0.12</Claim>, which are <M t={String.raw`0.65^2`} /> and <M t={String.raw`0.35^2`} />.

    <Wave taps="correction:alt" stems fit span={0.001}>
      The correction by itself over one millisecond. Notice a pair of samples at each drop, one down
      and one up, and zero between.
    </Wave>

    It touches <Claim id="two-samples">two samples in each cycle, by 1 at most</Claim>. The cost is two comparisons a sample, and a few multiplications at each drop.

  </Fragment>
  <Listing
    slot="listing"
    path="cpp/kit/osc.h"
    from="static float blep(float t, float dt) {"
    to="return t * t + t + t + 1.0f; } return 0.0f; }"
    name="blep"
    at={{ after: 'return t + t - t * t - 1.0f;', before: 'return t * t + t + t + 1.0f;' }}
  />

</StageBlock>

<StageBlock tap="saw" n={5} title="The kit's saw: the ramp and the correction, added">
  The saw is the two together. It costs one addition more than the naive saw.

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[n]} = \sig{in}{s[n]} + \sig{alt}{c[n]}`} />

  <Check id="sumError" />

  The naive saw with the correction added is the kit's saw <Claim id="sum-exact">to the last bit</Claim>, so the two stages before this one are the whole of it.

  What that small correction does is easiest to see one partial at a time. Averaging over a triangle two samples wide does a simple thing to a sine: it leaves the frequency alone and scales the size.

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`H(f) = \left( \frac{\sin(\pi f / f_s)}{\pi f / f_s} \right)^{2}`}
  />

  <M t="H" /> is the scale for a sine at frequency <M t="f" />, before it folds. It is 1 at the bottom. At half the rate it is <M t={String.raw`(2/\pi)^2`} />, about 0.41. At the sample rate, and at every multiple of it, the sine on top is zero and so is <M t="H" />.

  Put that beside the folding rule. A partial lands low when it was asked for near a multiple of the sample rate, and that is exactly where <M t="H" /> is close to zero. A partial lands near the top when it was asked for near half the rate, or one and a half times it, where <M t="H" /> is not small. So the correction is strongest on the folds that land lowest and weakest on the ones that land at the top. That is the slope across the picture of the kit's saw in part 1.

<StageBlock tap="triangle" n={6} title="The triangle: the same cure, a degree gentler">
  `BlepOsc` makes a triangle from the same phase. Its first line is the naive triangle of part 4. Its second adds a correction at each of the two corners. A corner is where the slope jumps, as a drop is where the height jumps, and `blamp` is to a corner what `blep` is to a drop: the running total of it, a piece of a cubic where that was a piece of a parabola.

  <Controls ids="note" />

  <Spectrum taps="triangleNaive:alt2 triangle:alt3" linear min={-120} max={6}>
    The naive triangle and the kit's triangle. Notice that a triangle has odd partials only, falling
    away fast, and that the peaks between them are lower in the kit's triangle, by most at the left.
  </Spectrum>

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{alt3}{y_{\triangle}[n]} = \at{triangle-fold}{\sig{alt2}{g[n]}} + \at{triangle-fix}{8\,\Delta\,\bigl(b(\varphi) - b(\varphi + \tfrac{1}{2})\bigr)}`}
  />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    <M t="g" /> is the naive triangle, the ramp folded in two. It climbs four units of height in a cycle and falls four, so its slope is <M t={String.raw`4\Delta`} /> a sample on the way up and the same on the way down, and at each corner the slope jumps by <M t={String.raw`8\Delta`} />. That is where the eight comes from, and why the correction grows with the step. <M t="b" /> is `blamp`, the shape of one rounded corner, and it is zero away from a corner. The corner at the bottom of the cycle is added. The corner at the top is half a cycle on and turns the other way, so it is taken away.

    <M
      block
      t={String.raw`b = \begin{cases} \dfrac{(1 - d)^3}{6} & 0 \le d < 1 \\[10pt] \dfrac{(1 + d)^3}{6} & -1 < d < 0 \\[10pt] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}`}

    <M t="d" /> is again the time since the nearest corner, in samples. Both rows are largest at the corner itself and fade to nothing a sample away.

      name="blamp"

<Part id="ways" title="Five ways to live with a sample rate.">

None of these is a preset. Each is a habit of working with shapes that have edges. Press "Try it" and the page moves to settings that show the idea, so you can go back up and see what the spectrum and the samples are doing.

<Ways>
  <Way title="Keep bright notes low" tag="the note under a tenth of the rate" set="promise">
    The header of the kit's oscillators makes a promise: <Src path="cpp/kit/osc.h" at="-60 dB for fundamentals under a tenth of the sample rate">aliasing below −60 dB for fundamentals under a tenth of the sample rate</Src>. Partial by partial, that describes the folds that land low. <Claim id="promise-low">At 440 Hz every fold that lands under 12 kHz is below −63 dB</Claim>. It does not describe the top of the band, where <Claim id="promise-top">the strongest fold of all stands at −46.8 dB, near 23.75 kHz, against −38.7 dB for the naive saw</Claim>. Pads and drones live well inside the promise. After the card, raise Note and watch the spectrum of stage 5 for the peaks between the partials coming up from the right.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Test on a note that moves" tag="Glide, and listen under the note" set="watched">
    A fold on a held note can pass for part of the sound. A fold on a moving note slides against the music. So try an oscillator on a glide, and listen below the lowest note, where a saw has nothing of its own. This card is the moving note of part 3 through the kit's saw. <Claim id="way-watched">Below 800 Hz it holds −71 dB</Claim>, far under what the naive saw left there.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Choose a shape with softer edges" tag="a triangle for a saw" set="soft">
    The cost of a shape is in its corners, so a rounder shape costs less before anything is corrected. On the note the lab starts on, <Claim id="way-soft">the strongest fold of the naive triangle reads −35.2 dB, at 21,120 Hz, where the naive saw has −19.7 dB. In the kit's triangle that line reads −45.7 dB</Claim>. A triangle has fewer and weaker partials than a saw, so it is a duller sound. Where the part allows it, it is the cheapest cure there is.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Use the folds on purpose" tag="the naive saw, gliding" set="grit">
    Folds are tones at exact places, and they move in exact ways. This card is the naive saw on a glide, with nothing done about them. Listen for an edge that swims against the note. After the card, raise Note and listen for it growing. Chosen, that is an effect. Left in by accident, it is the fault this lesson is about.
  </Way>
  <Way title="Make the wave faster than it is played" tag="oversampling">
    For notes bright and high enough that what is left matters, the kit's header <Src path="cpp/kit/osc.h" at="bright leads belong on an oversampled voice">points to an oversampled voice</Src>. The wave is made at a multiple of the sample rate, everything above half the real rate is filtered away, and then every second sample is kept, or every fourth. The partials that fold are then higher ones, which are weaker, and the filter takes out most of what would have landed. <Lesson id="saturation" cap /> builds that filter and measures it.
  </Way>
</Ways>

<Part id="wrong" title="Six ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="The folds near the top stay" set="kit">
    The correction is not a wall at half the rate. <Claim id="kit-line-top">In the kit's saw on the note the lab starts on, the line at 21,120 Hz reads −30.6 dB, only 10.9 dB under</Claim> the naive saw's. Count all the folds and the gain is modest. <Claim id="fold-4k-low">With Note at 4 kHz, the folds that land at or under the note are 41.9 dB lower or more</Claim> in the kit's saw, but <Claim id="fold-4k-all">all the folds added together are just 13.1 dB lower</Claim>, because the ones near the top carry most of what is left. What to do: keep bright notes low, take the top off with a filter after the oscillator, as most patches do anyway, or oversample.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Folds that hide" set="hidden">
    <Claim id="hidden-4k">With Note at exactly 4 kHz a cycle is a whole number of samples, and the naive saw is one pattern of 12 samples repeated</Claim>. Every fold lands on one of the saw's own partials. The spectrum shows no stray peak, and the folds are still there, changing the sizes of the partials they sit on. An oscillator checked on one such note looks clean. Check it on notes that do not divide the sample rate, or on a glide.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A thicket of folds" set="thicket">
    The opposite case. On the note the lab starts on, the folds share six places. Move the note a little, as this card does, and they stop sharing: <Claim id="thicket">the 13 peaks above −60 dB in the naive saw at 3,840 Hz become more than fifty</Claim>. Each is smaller than the six were, and there are far more of them. Look at the spectrum of stage 3 after pressing the card. The cure is the one already built, and it works best on the ones that land low.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The top of the saw is dulled">
    The correction cannot tell a fold from a partial that belongs. It scales everything by the formula of stage 5, so the saw's own partials lose a little too: <Claim id="droop-fundamental">0.18 dB at the fundamental</Claim> and <Claim id="droop-top">7.2 dB at the sixth partial, 23,036 Hz in the measuring frame</Claim>. The loss is small where notes are played and largest at the very top of the band. If the top matters, that is one more reason to oversample.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Exactly half the sample rate">
    The rule of part 1 says below half, and means it. <Claim id="nyquist-silence">A sine at exactly 24 kHz that starts at zero gets two samples a cycle, both where the wave crosses zero, and the loop is silence</Claim>. A little lower and the sine is all there, as part 8 shows. What to do: nothing that matters should sit at the edge of the band.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A shaped wave will not go that high">
    `BlepOsc` limits its step, as stage 1 of part 5 shows. Asked for a note above that limit, a saw or a triangle from the kit plays the limit and says nothing. No music is written up there, but a test that sweeps an oscillator to the top of the band will find a note that stops rising. `SineOsc` has no such limit, and will fold.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Six things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="Digital sound is a staircase.">
    A sample is a number at an instant. It is not a level held until the next one. Under half the rate exactly one wave passes through the samples, and it is smooth: the picture of part 2 draws it through the dots. The stairs are how some pictures draw samples, the last figure of part 5 among them.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A wave needs many samples in each cycle to be stored properly.">
    Any number above two will do. Take a sine so close under half the rate that a cycle gets barely more than two samples.

    <Still
      taps="wave:in samples:out"
      view="curve"
      values={{ freq: 23952 }}
      caption="A sine just under half the sample rate, and its samples, joined here by straight lines. The samples swing back and forth far inside the wave they were taken from, and grow slowly from left to right."
    />

    The samples look nothing like a sine at full level. <Claim id="near-nyquist-dots">At 23,952 Hz they turn sign at every sample, and none of those in the picture reaches a sixth of full level</Claim>. The spectrum of the loop disagrees with the eye. <Claim id="near-nyquist">It finds one line, within a bin of 23,952 Hz and at full level</Claim>. The sine is all there.

  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Aliasing is a kind of noise.">
    It is tones, at the exact places the folding rule gives. On a held note they hold still: stage 3 of part 5 finds six of them between the partials of one saw. They pass for noise only when there are very many, as in the thicket of part 7.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A filter after the oscillator will clean it up.">
    Once a partial has folded it is a frequency under half the rate like any other, and it sits among the partials that belong. In the fault called Folds that hide it sits exactly on top of them. No filter can take out the one and leave the other. A filter can lower the top of the band, folds and partials together, and that is all. The cure has to act before the samples exist, which is what the two corrections of part 4 do.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Frequencies above half the sample rate are simply lost.">
    They are kept, in the wrong place. The sine of part 1 was asked to go twice as high as the samples can follow, and it sounded all the way.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A higher sample rate makes the problem go away.">
