<Part id="why" title="A sound recorded up close has no room around it.">

A drum machine has no room. Neither has a guitar plugged straight into a recorder, or a voice sung close to a microphone. Each note ends where the instrument ends it, and what follows is nothing at all.

Here is the drum loop this page opens on, as it was played. Between the hits it does not only get quiet. Its quietest tenth of a second measures <Claim id="dry-gap">−104 dB</Claim> against full scale, which is silence.

<Still
  taps="in"
  set="dry"
  caption="Four seconds of drums, dry. Each hit is a spike, and the line between the hits is flat: nothing rings on."
/>

Listen to it first. <Hear set="dry" label="Hear the drums as played" />

Nothing heard in a real place sounds like that. In a hall the walls send every hit back, late and from every side, for seconds. The ear reads the place from those returns: how large it is, and how far away the drummer sits.

So the question of this lesson: how is a sound put into a room it was never played in?

### The obvious answer fails

A room sends back echoes. So add echoes: a few copies of the loop, each a little later than the last and of its own size. Here are a few: <Claim id="few-echoes">ten that stand at least a tenth as tall as the tallest, all within a tenth of a second</Claim> of each hit.

<Still
  taps="room"
  set="echoes"
  from={0}
  span={0.12}
  caption="What one click turns into when these ten echoes are added, turned up to be seen: ten spikes, each apart from the next, and then nothing. Every hit of the drums is sent back that way, once for each spike."
/>

Listen for what follows each hit. <Hear set="echoes" label="Hear ten echoes" show />

Each hit now has a short burst of copies close behind it. Whether that sounds like a place is for your ear. The measurement is plainer. The gaps are as empty as they were: the quietest tenth of a second of the result measures <Claim id="echoes-gap">−102 dB</Claim>.

A real room does not return ten echoes. Sound crosses the hall, meets a wall and comes back. Then it meets the next wall, and the one after. Very soon the returns are far too many and too close together to hear one by one. What reaches the ear is a wash that swells, dulls and dies away. No handful of delays makes that.

### The room's answer to one click

The way out is to stop counting echoes and take them all at once. Make one short click in the room and record everything that comes back. That recording is the room's _impulse response_. It holds every echo the room has, each at its own moment and its own strength.

<Still
  taps="room"
  caption="The answer of the engine's room to one click, turned up to be seen. A short silence, a quick swell, and then noise that dies away: echoes too many to tell apart."
/>

The room in this engine was never recorded. The engine writes its impulse response from noise, shaped to behave as a room's does, and part 5 opens that up. For now it is enough that there is one, and that it can be heard by itself: <Listen tap="room">hear the room's answer to one click</Listen>.

A sound is a row of samples, and each sample is a click of its own height. If the room answers one click with its impulse response, it answers a row of clicks with a row of impulse responses. Each one starts at its own click and is as large as that click. Add them all up, and the result is the sound in the room. That sum is called _convolution_.

  taps="out"
  set="hall"
  caption="The same four seconds with the room added. Every hit is where it was. The line between the hits is no longer flat: the room is still ringing when the next hit comes."
/>

Measured on the drums at the settings the page opens on, the quietest tenth of a second is now at <Claim id="gap-filled">−41 dB</Claim>. <Hear set="hall" label="Hear the drums in the room" />

One sample holds very little energy, so one copy of this room is far quieter than a drum loop. In this picture the impulse and its copies are all turned up by one gain, the gain that puts the impulse's largest sample at one half. At the room the page opens on that is <Claim id="lift">30.2 dB</Claim>, and the line below gives it for the room as it is set now.

<Check id="lift" />

As arithmetic, call the impulse response <M t="h" />, and call the first sample <M t="x[k_1]" />: <M t="k_1" /> is where it sits, counted in samples from the start of the loop, and <M t="x[k_1]" /> is its size. The copy it lays down is

<M block t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{y_1[n]} = \sig{in}{x[k_1]} \; h[n - k_1]`} />

Read it against the second lane. <M t="n" /> is a moment in the loop. The copy at that moment is the impulse response as it stands <M t="n - k_1" /> samples after its own start, which is the same as the impulse response moved along to begin at <M t="k_1" />. It is multiplied by the sample's size, so a sample of one half gives a copy of half the height. Before <M t="k_1" /> the copy is nothing, because a room does not answer a click that has not happened yet.

The other two copies are the same with their own samples, and the last lane adds the three:

<M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[n]} = \sig{alt}{y_1[n]} + \sig{alt2}{y_2[n]} + \sig{alt3}{y_3[n]}`} />

That is all a convolution reverb does. A loop of real sound has a sample at every position and not at three, so it lays down a copy for each of them. Part 4 does exactly that.

</Part>

<Part id="build" title="Build the sum: one stamp, three, a run, and then every sample at once.">

Part 2 stamped three samples that were set by hand. This part does the same to the loop's own samples, with the room at its true size, and keeps adding stamps until every sample has one. After each piece the lanes are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece adds. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these lanes and plays "Hear it so far". The room is the engine's. The stamping and the adding are written for this lesson.

A few stamps out of many are far quieter than the finished reverb. So each of the first three steps is turned up until its largest sample is one half, and a line under the step says by how much.

<Build>
  <Step title="One sample, one stamp">
    Take the loudest sample in the second quarter of the loop, and nothing else. On the drums it is the front of the hit that lands a second in. The first quarter is passed over only so that the stamp stands clear of the picture's edge. Lay down one copy of the impulse response, starting at that sample and multiplied by it. That is the whole reverb of a loop that held only that sample.

    `stamp` is the piece every later step is made of. The sample stands at `k`, and `j` counts along the impulse. Its first line works out how much of the copy fits before the loop ends. The second adds that much to `y`, each sample of the impulse times the one sample of the sound. The third adds the rest at the start of `y`: the loop goes round, so the tail of one pass rings on over the next.

    Press "Hear it so far". It is the room, struck once.

    </Lanes>

  <Step title="A run of samples">
    Real sound has a sample at every position. So stamp a run of them, one after another: <Claim id="run-of-stamps">240 samples, which is 5 ms of the loop, and on the drums they come to 13.7 dB under the whole reverb</Claim>.     This is the sum exactly as it is written:

    <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{y[n]} = \sum_{k} \sig{in}{x[\at{run-place}{k}]} \; h[n - k]`} />

    Read it against the lanes. <M t="k" /> runs over the samples that are stamped, the short stretch in the first lane. Each term is one copy from part 2: the impulse response moved to begin at <M t="k" /> and multiplied by the sample there. The big sigma says to add them, which `stamp` does by landing every copy in the same `y`.

    The run stops there because of what the sum costs. The impulse the page opens on is <Claim id="sum-cost">144,960 samples long and the loop is 192,000 samples, so stamping every sample would take 27.8 billion multiplications</Claim> for each channel. The run takes <Claim id="run-cost">34.8 million</Claim>.

    />

    <Lanes slot="picture" taps="builtIn built">
      A short run of the loop's samples, and a copy for every one of them added. Notice that the
      result already has the outline of a reverb's tail.
    </Lanes>

  <Step title="Every sample, by transform">
    Stamping every sample one at a time is out of reach. There is another road to the same sum, and it goes through the Fourier transform of <Lesson id="fourier-transform" />.

    Put a steady sine into a room and what comes back is a sine at the same frequency. It may be larger or smaller, earlier or later, and it is never another frequency. The sum says why: every stamp of a sine is that sine again, later and of another size, and sines of one frequency add up to one sine of that frequency. So to one frequency a room does one simple thing: it multiplies its size by one number and turns its phase by one angle. Those two numbers, for every frequency, are the transform of the impulse response. Transform the sound as well, and the long sum becomes one multiplication for each frequency:

    <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{Y[b]} = \sig{in}{X[b]} \cdot \at{transform-product}{H[b]}`} />

    Here <M t="b" /> is a bin of the transform, <M t="X" /> is the spectrum of the loop, <M t="H" /> the spectrum of the room and <M t="Y" /> the spectrum of the reverb. Each is a size and an angle. Multiplying two of them multiplies the sizes and adds the angles.

    `by_transform` is that sentence in order. It loads the loop into a block with zeros after it, transforms it, multiplies it by the room's spectrum bin by bin, transforms back, and folds whatever ran past the loop's end onto its start. The transform is the engine's own, `kit::Fft`. The block is the whole loop at once: <Claim id="transform-cost">524,288 points, the power of two that holds the loop and its tail, and one transform of that size is 5.0 million butterflies</Claim>, the small step a transform is made of. Three transforms stand in for the billions of multiplications of the step before: the loop forwards, the room forwards, and the product back.

    Two things hold this to be the same sum. The lab's test worked the sum of every stamp out term by term at 64 moments spread across the loop, and there the transform's answer and the sum <Claim id="transform-agrees">differ by less than −130 dB</Claim>, counted against the sum's own level. The other runs on this page at every render, and is the step that is held to the engine. One unit sample goes through the same product of spectra, and what comes out must be the impulse the engine wrote. In every room and length of loop the test tried, the difference was <Claim id="unit-through">more than 130 dB under</Claim> the impulse. The line below is what it is now.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Beside the dry sound" controls="send">
    The last piece puts the sound back in its room. The dry loop is left exactly as it is, and Send sets how much of the reverb is added beside it:

    <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{o[n]} = \sig{in}{x[n]} + \at{send-sum}{g} \, y[n]`} />

    <M t="o" /> is what is heard, <M t="x" /> the dry loop, <M t="y" /> the reverb of the step before, and <M t="g" /> the gain that Send stands for. On the drums, at the Send the page opens on, <Claim id="send-level">the two together are 0.2 dB louder than the dry drums, where the reverb alone was 3.2 dB quieter than them</Claim>. A reverb at a sensible level adds little to a meter. What it adds is in the gaps, where there was nothing.

  </Step>

That is a convolution reverb: a stamp, a way of doing every stamp at once, and a sum with the dry sound. What stood in the way was cost, and the transform answers that for a loop that is known in advance. For sound that is still arriving the engine does not do the sum itself: it writes the room, and hands the sum to the browser.

<StageBlock tap="room" n={1} title="Echoes too many to count are noise">
  Record a real room's answer to a click and it has three parts. First nothing, while the sound crosses the room. Then echoes, so many and so close together that they are noise. Then that noise dying away, the top end first, because air and soft surfaces take more out of high frequencies at every bounce.

  The engine writes exactly that, in <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="export function spaceImpulseChannel(">`spaceImpulseChannel`</Src>. It begins with noise from a small generator of random numbers that is started from a seed. So <Claim id="same-room">the same settings make the same room, sample for sample</Claim>, however often it is written. Left and right start from different seeds. They share a shape and no sample, so the tail is different on each side.

  The filter is two one-pole filters in a row, the filter of <Lesson id="one-pole" />. A filter that closes also lets less noise through, which would make the room shorter as well as darker. So each sample is divided by what the filter lets through at that cutoff. The tone changes, and the level stays where the next stage puts it.

  A tenth of the way into the room the page opens on, <Claim id="dark-end">the level around 5 kHz is about 4 dB under the level around 500 Hz; two thirds of the way in it is 17 dB under</Claim>. One more filter, after this one, thins everything below <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="lowCutHz: 160,">160 Hz</Src>, so that many sounds sent into one room do not pile up as rumble.

  <M
    slot="equation"
    block
    t={String.raw`\at{dark-cutoff}{f_c[i] = f_b \left(\frac{f_d}{f_b}\right)^{i/T}}`}
  />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    Read it against the picture. <M t="i" /> is a sample of the tail and <M t="T" /> is the tail's length in samples, so <M t="i/T" /> runs from 0 at the start to 1 at the end. At the start the bracket is raised to the power 0, which is 1, and the cutoff <M t="f_c" /> is <M t="f_b" />, Top end at the start: <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="brightHz: 7000,">7 kHz</Src> as the engine sets it.   </Fragment>

<StageBlock tap="energy" n={3} title="Swell in, then die away">
  A room's length is given as the time its sound takes to fall <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="/** Seconds for the tail to fall 60 dB. Default 5. */">60 dB</Src>, and Decay is that time. The engine's own room starts at <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="decaySec: 5,">5 s</Src>; this page starts it at <Src path="site/labs/convolution-reverb.cpp" at={'lab::Control decay{"decay", "Decay", "s", 0.3f, 6.0f, 3.0f, lab::kLog};'}>3 s</Src>, so that the tail fits inside the four-second loop.

  A better picture of a decay than the wave is how much energy is still to come at each moment: add up the squares of the samples from that moment to the end. It falls smoothly where the wave is ragged.

  <Controls ids="decay swell" />

  <Curve taps="energy" xTitle="seconds" yTitle="energy still to come, dB" min={-80} max={0}>
    The energy of the impulse that is still to come, against time. Try Decay shorter, and notice the
    line steepen and stay straight. A straight line in decibels is an exponential decay.
  </Curve>

  Nobody waits for the whole fall to read a room's length. The reading here is the one of <Lesson id="plate-reverb" />, which the lessons either side of this one use too: three times the stretch of this curve from 5 dB down to 25 dB down. Read that way, the room the page opens on <Claim id="decay-time">falls 60 dB in 3.0 s</Claim>, which is the Decay it is set to. The reading moves a little with the stretch it is taken from, so it is written to two figures.

  The curve itself is 60 dB down sooner. By the measure in the picture, that room has all but a millionth of its energy behind it <Claim id="energy-60">2.7 s</Claim> in. The curve counts what is left, and near the end almost nothing is: the tail stops at the Decay time, and its last tenth is run out, as is said below.

  The tail does not start at full size. It swells in:

  <M block t={String.raw`\at{shape-swell}{w(t) = 1 - e^{-3t/t_w}}`} />

  <M t="t" /> is the time since the tail began and <M t="t_w" /> is the Swell. At the first sample the exponential is 1 and <M t="w" /> is 0. One Swell time later it is <Claim id="swell-95">95 %</Claim> of the way up, measured on the engine's impulse with a swell and without one.   The <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="const runOut = Math.max(1, Math.round(tail * 0.1))">last tenth</Src> of the tail is also run down to nothing in a <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="const end = i >= tail - runOut ?   </Fragment>

<StageBlock tap="response" n={4} title="How loud a room should be">
  How loud should a room come back? There is no single answer, because it depends on the sound that is sent in. This room is dark. It returns a sound's low mids and little of its top. So the engine picks the band where music has its weight, <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="export const SPACE_LEVEL_LOW_HZ = 150">150 Hz</Src> to <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="export const SPACE_LEVEL_HIGH_HZ = 1500">1,500 Hz</Src>, and scales the whole impulse by one number, so that a steady sound in that band comes back as loud as it went in.

  <Spectrum taps="response responseMean" min={-40} max={12}>
    What the room does to each frequency: bin by bin, and averaged over a third of an octave. Try
    Decay a little longer or shorter, and notice the jagged line redraw completely while the smooth
    one hardly moves.
  </Spectrum>

  Measured on the lab's convolution at the room the page opens on: noise held to that band, more steeply than the rule's own filter holds it, comes back <Claim id="band-as-loud">about 1 dB louder than it went in</Claim>. White noise, most of whose energy is above the band, comes back <Claim id="white-quieter">about 5 dB quieter</Claim>.

  One steady tone is another matter. A room written from noise has a response that is itself noise, up at one frequency and down at the next. Between the two ends of the level band, the single bins that the jagged line is drawn through stand anywhere <Claim id="ragged">from 18 dB under to 7 dB over</Claim> the level a wire would give. <Claim id="tones-vary">Three sines, at 220, 440 and 1,000 Hz, came back between 4 dB quieter and 6 dB louder</Claim> than they went in.   <M slot="equation" block t={String.raw`\at{level-unit}{c = \sqrt{E_1 / E_h}}`} />
  <Fragment slot="readback">
    Read it against the smooth line. <M t="E_h" /> is the energy of the impulse as heard through the level band: the impulse is passed through a filter that keeps that band, squared and added up. <M t="E_1" /> is the same measure of a single sample at 1, which is the impulse response of a plain wire. The listing's `levelDb` is a trim on top of that rule, <Src path="src/core/tracks/space.ts" at="levelDb: 0,">0 dB</Src> as the engine starts it and on this page.
  </Fragment>

<StageBlock tap="spectral" n={5} title="The sum, by the engine's transform">
  The impulse is written. What is left is the sum of part 4, which the build's fourth step did as a product of spectra. This stage does a stretch short enough to work out both ways: <Src path="site/labs/convolution-reverb.cpp" at="constexpr int kExcerpt = 2048;">2,048 samples</Src> from the start of the loop, against as many of the impulse after its Pre-delay.

  <Curve taps="direct spectral" xTitle="milliseconds">
    The sum done term by term, and the product of spectra. Notice that one line lies on the other.
  </Curve>

  <Check id="sums" />

  On the drums, the guitar and noise the two differ by <Claim id="sums-agree">less than −130 dB</Claim>, which is the rounding of the arithmetic.

  The term-by-term sum of this stage is written the other way round from part 4's:

  <M block t={String.raw`y[n] = \sum_{j} \at{sums-echo}{h[j] \; x[n - j]}`} />

  Part 4 went through the samples of the sound, and gave each one a copy of the impulse. This goes through the samples of the impulse, <M t="j" /> counting along it, and gives each one a copy of the sound: the whole loop, <M t="j" /> samples late and <M t="h[j]" /> times as large. Both add up the same products, so both are the same sum. Read this second way, every sample of the impulse is one echo, and a room is as many echoes as its impulse has samples. Part 1's ten echoes were on the right road and stopped far too soon.

  Read the same line for one moment <M t="n" /> of the output, and it says why the sum is drawn in textbooks with the impulse turned round. The newest sample of the sound, <M t="x[n]" />, meets the first sample of the impulse, <M t="h[0]" />. A sample that is <M t="j" /> samples old meets <M t="h[j]" />, because its copy of the impulse has by now run for <M t="j" /> samples. So the sum goes forwards through the impulse while it goes backwards through the sound. Lay the two side by side on one time axis and one of them has to be flipped. Nothing in the room runs backwards.

  <Listing
    path="site/labs/convolution-reverb.cpp"
    from="// y[n] = sum over j of h[j] x[n - j]: every sample of the impulse is one"
    to="direct.data[n] = static_cast<float>(sum); }"
    at={{ echo: 'if (k < samples) sum += static_cast<double>(h[first + j]) * x[k];' }}
    name="sums"
  />

  Over frequency the product of spectra can be seen. In decibels a product is a sum, so the spectrum of the reverb is the spectrum of the loop with the shape of the room's added to it.

Two numbers go in, one of them is turned by an angle, and their sum and their difference come out. A transform is that step, done for every pair, at every doubling of size. The letters in the listing are the kit's own, and are not the letters of this page's equations.

<StageBlock tap="wet" n={6} title="The browser's convolver, and not waiting">
  The product of spectra has a price. It needs the whole of the sound before it can transform it, so it cannot answer until its block is full. A reverb that plays live cannot wait for the end of the song.

  The way out is to cut the impulse into pieces. The first short piece is convolved in small blocks, the sound taken a block at a time as <Lesson id="short-time-fourier" /> takes it a frame at a time, so its answer comes at once. Later pieces of the tail use larger and larger transforms, which cost less for each sample. They can afford to be late, because the part of the tail they work out is late anyway. This is called partitioned convolution.

  The engine does not write that itself. It hands the impulse to the browser's `ConvolverNode`. How a browser does the sum is its own business, and what can be checked from outside is that it does not wait. The engine's room turns the node's own scaling off, because the impulse already carries the level of stage 4.

  That was checked in the browser these pages are tested in. Given an impulse that is a single sample at 1, its convolver returns its input <Claim id="unit-impulse">at the same sample, with a difference below −80 dB</Claim>, and the device reports a latency of <Src path="src/core/devices/native/ConvolverReverb.ts" at="readonly latencySamples = 0">no samples</Src>. After a sound stops, the room goes on for <Claim id="rings-on">the length of the impulse, less one sample</Claim>.

  So there are two convolutions on this page: the lab's, with the kit's transform, and the browser's, which is the one the engine plays through. Each time a control moves, the page puts the loop and the impulse through the browser's own and compares the two.

  <ConvolverCheck source="in" impulse="impulse" result="wet" />

  In the browser these pages are tested in, the two differ by <Claim id="browser-convolver">less than −80 dB</Claim>. The line above is what yours makes of it.

  One thing here is particular to a loop. What plays on this page is the loop as it sounds once it has gone round for longer than the impulse lasts, when the tail of every pass is still ringing over the next. For a sound that repeats every <M t="L" /> samples, that is the same as convolving one pass with an impulse folded onto itself:

  <M block t={String.raw`\tilde{h}[j] = h[j] + h[j + L] + h[j + 2L] + \dots`} />

  Whatever of the impulse lies more than one loop along is added back in at the start, since by then the loop has come round and is saying the same thing again. The lab folds the impulse this way, convolves once, and folds the answer. <Claim id="loop-wraps">On half a second of the drums in a room of 0.3 s, where the sum over a loop that never stops is small enough to do term by term, the two agree to below −130 dB</Claim>.

  <Fault title="How loud is an impulse?">

A convolver that is handed impulses from outside has to decide what "as loud" means. The browser's node has a rule of its own, which the engine's room <Src path="src/core/tracks/SpaceRoom.ts" at="convolver.normalize = false">turns off</Src> in favour of the rule of stage 4, and which the engine's plain device leaves on (stage 7). When an impulse comes from elsewhere, set its level by ear against the dry sound, and expect to do it again for the next one.

<Part id="myths" title="Five things people say about convolution.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A convolution reverb plays back a recording of a room.">
    It keeps the room's answer to one click, and lays a copy of that answer down for every sample of the sound. Nothing is played back as it was recorded. Part 2 shows three copies being laid down and added. And the answer need not be a recording at all: the engine's is written from noise, as stage 1 of part 5 shows.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="Multiplying spectra is a shortcut, so it must be an approximation.">
    It is the same sum, reached another way. Stage 5 of part 5 does one stretch both ways and draws one line on the other, and the fourth step of the build holds the transform's answer to the sum of every stamp. What separates them is the rounding of the arithmetic.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A convolution reverb has to add delay.">
    One large transform does, because it must wait for its whole block. A convolver built in pieces need not. Stage 6 of part 5 measures the browser's: it returns its input at the same sample.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A longer room is a louder room.">
    Not in this engine. The level of its room is set by rule after its length, so white noise comes back <Claim id="length-not-level">4.9 dB quieter at a Decay of 0.5 s and 5.0 dB quieter at 6 s</Claim>. What a longer room does is fill more of the silence between sounds. <Claim id="length-fills-gaps">On the drums, with Send where the page opens it, the output is 0.3 dB over the dry drums at a Decay of 0.5 s and 0.2 dB over them at 6 s, while its quietest tenth of a second rises from −66 dB to −38 dB</Claim>. The Mud card of part 7 shows the same on a mix.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="With the right impulse, a convolver can copy any effect.">
    It can copy whatever treats every moment and every level alike: a room, a filter, an echo. It cannot copy an effect that changes with how loud the sound is, as the compressor of <Lesson id="compression" /> does, or one that adds frequencies, as the distortion of <Lesson id="saturation" /> does. A convolution adds none. The Sine of this page comes back from the room as a sine: <Claim id="no-new-tones">what is not at its frequency is more than 130 dB under what is</Claim>.
  </Myth>
</Myths>
