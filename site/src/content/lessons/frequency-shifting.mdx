<Part id="why" title="A note is a row of partials, and there are two ways to move a row.">

Play one low note on a saw wave. It is heard as one thing at one pitch, and it is not one frequency. Its spectrum is a row of them, called partials. On this note the lowest four stand at <Claim id="saw-in">110, 220, 330 and 440 Hz</Claim>, and the row goes on above them. Each is a whole multiple of the lowest. That is what makes the row a note: a wave built from whole multiples of one frequency repeats itself that many times a second, and the ear hears the repeat as a pitch.

  min={-70}

Listen to it first. <Hear set="note" label="Hear the note" />

Now suppose the whole row is wanted higher. "Higher" can mean two different things, and music nearly always means the first. This lesson is about the second. So here is its question. What happens to a note when every one of its partials is moved up by the same number of hertz?

### The way music moves a note is to multiply

Tighten a string, or play a recording faster, and every frequency in the sound is multiplied by one number.

<M block t={String.raw`f_{\text{out}} = r\,f`} />

Here <M t="f" /> is the frequency of any one partial and <M t="r" /> is the ratio, the same for all of them. The lab plays the loop faster to show it. <Claim id="pitched-lines">Played faster by the ratio that takes the lowest partial from 110 Hz to 160 Hz, the four partials come out at 160, 320, 480 and 640 Hz, each at the level it had</Claim>.

<Still
  taps="rulerIn rulerPitched"
  set="faster"
  view="curve"
  min={-70}
  max={-10}
  caption="The note, and the note played faster. The lines of the faster one still stand at even distances that begin at zero. The distances have grown: the pattern is stretched, as a drawing on rubber is."
/>

The lines are still whole multiples of the lowest, so it is still a note. Every interval inside it is kept, because an interval is a ratio and the ratios are as they were. This is a change of pitch, and <Lesson id="pitch-shifting" /> is about making one without changing the speed. Listen for the same note, higher. <Hear set="faster" label="Hear it played faster" />

It is not what the question asked for. The fourth line has moved four times as far as the first.

### Adding takes a multiplication of another kind

To move a line by a fixed number of hertz, something has to add to its frequency. A filter cannot. A filter changes how much of each frequency there is, and has no way to change where one is.

What can is multiplying the sound, sample by sample, by a second wave. That is ring modulation, and <Lesson id="tremolo-and-ring-modulation" /> is about it. Multiply a partial by a cosine and the partial is replaced by two: one lower by the cosine's frequency and one higher by it. They are called sidebands. <Claim id="ring-saw">Times a cosine at 50 Hz, the saw's lowest partial is replaced by lines at 60 Hz and 160 Hz, each 6.0 dB under it</Claim>.

<Still
  taps="rulerIn rulerRing"
  set="ringed"
  view="curve"
  min={-70}
  max={-10}
  caption="The note, and the note times a cosine. Every line of the note has become two, one on each side of where it stood, and nothing is left where it stood. The upper line of one partial and the lower line of the next stand close together, between the places where the note's own lines were."
/>

That is adding, and it is half of what was asked for. The upper lines are the note moved up. The lower lines are the note moved down. Both are there at once. Listen to the two together. <Hear set="ringed" label="Hear it times a cosine" />

### The fix is to make the product twice, so that half of it cancels

A frequency shifter is a ring modulator that makes its product twice. It uses two copies of the sound and two copies of the cosine, arranged so that the upper sidebands of the two products are alike and the lower ones are equal and opposite. Add the two products and the lower sidebands cancel. What is left is each partial, once, moved up, at the level it had.

<M block t={String.raw`f_{\text{out}} = f + f_c`} />

<M t="f_c" /> is the shift in hertz, the same for every partial. Through the engine's Frequency Shifter, <Claim id="saw-partials">with Shift at 50 Hz, the saw's partials at 110, 220, 330 and 440 Hz come out at 160, 270, 380 and 490 Hz, each at the level it had</Claim>.

The whole pattern has slid along the ruler, and its first distance, from zero, is no longer the same as the rest. The small bump near the left edge is what is left of a line that was cancelled, and part 5 measures how far down it is."
/>

Slid, where the faster copy was stretched. That is the whole difference between the two, and it has a consequence. The lines are as far apart as they were, and they are no longer whole multiples of anything: the second is not twice the first. So the wave has stopped repeating at the note's rate.

Sounds whose partials are not whole multiples of one frequency are familiar ones: a bell, a struck bar, a drum. That is the family to listen for. <Hear set="shifted" label="Hear it shifted" />

### Why anyone wanted it

One early use was a practical one. In a hall, a microphone hears the loudspeakers, and a frequency that comes round that loop a little stronger each time turns into a howl. If everything is moved by a few hertz on each trip, no frequency lands on itself twice, and none can build. That was done for speech, which has no chords to put out of tune.

Musicians found the rest. A large shift turns a note into a clang, which is a sound of its own. A very small one, mixed with the sound as it went in, makes a slow movement that travels through the spectrum in one direction and does not come back. The device in this engine is tuned for that last use, and left to itself it starts there. This lesson starts it as a bare shifter, with nothing but the shifted sound coming out, so that the shift itself shows. Part 9 puts the two beside each other.

<Part id="build" title="A wire, two copies, a carrier and a sum: a shifter in six steps.">

A frequency shifter is a short program. This part begins with something that does nothing and adds five pieces to it, one at a time. After each piece the pictures are drawn again from what has been built so far, and the listing shows the lines that piece added. The listings are the lab's own source: the code that draws these pictures and plays "Hear it so far".

The numbers in this part were measured on the sine, which is a single partial. With one line going in, what each piece does to a line is plain to see. Choose the sine in the row of sounds while you step through, then go through again on the saw.

  <Step title="Make two copies, a quarter turn apart">
    The cancelling of part 1 needs the sound twice: once as it is, and once with every partial a quarter of a cycle further on. No delay can make the second copy, because a quarter of a cycle is a different length of time at every frequency. A filter that turned every frequency by exactly a quarter turn would need the whole future of the sound.

    The kit's answer is to give up on the first copy being the sound itself. It makes two copies, neither of which is the input, and asks only that they stand a quarter turn apart from each other. Each holds every frequency of the input at the level it had.

    <Claim id="build-pair">On the 440 Hz sine, the first copy runs 78.5° behind the sine, and the second runs ahead of the first by 0.34° short of a quarter turn.   <Step title="Add the two products, or subtract them" controls="shift fine lfoRate lfoDepth mode">
    Now the other half is used. The second copy is multiplied by the sine, and that product also holds both sidebands at half height. The two products differ in one thing. Their upper sidebands are the same wave. Their lower sidebands are the same wave, one of them upside down. Stage 3 of part 5 shows why in three lines of trigonometry, and here it is enough to see it happen.

    Add the products and the lower sidebands cancel, while the upper ones double. Subtract and it is the other way round. This is what a shifter adds to a ring modulator: one sideband is kept and the other is cancelled.

    <Claim id="build-sideband">On the 440 Hz sine with Shift at 100 Hz, the line at 340 Hz is now 50.5 dB under the line at 540 Hz, and that line stands 0.0 dB from the sine's own level.     Mode chooses. Up adds and Down subtracts. Stereo adds on the left and subtracts on the right. Ring leaves the second product out, which gives the ring modulator back.

<StageBlock tap="inPhase" n={1} title="Two chains of all-pass filters, a quarter turn apart">
  Each of the two copies is the sound through a chain of four all-pass sections. An all-pass filter lets every frequency through at full level and changes only its phase, which is how far through its cycle a frequency is when it comes out. <Lesson id="phasing" cap /> is built from them. So both copies hold what the sound held, which the build measured, and the two chains differ only in how far they turn each frequency.

  <Curve taps="phaseI:alt phaseQ:alt2" xLog xTitle="hertz" yTitle="phase, degrees" height={300}>
    The phase of each chain against frequency, worked out from the impulse responses of the kit's
    pair. Both lines fall by more than two whole turns between the bottom of the band and the top.
    Notice that the quadrature chain's line stays the same distance above the in-phase chain's all
    the way down.
  </Curve>

  On an axis of even ratios, the phase of one section is a smooth step, half a turn high and a few octaves wide. Four of them placed at even ratios wear into a ramp. The second chain's steps are placed midway between the first chain's, so its ramp is the same ramp moved along by half a step, and half of half a turn is the quarter turn.

  One section is one line of arithmetic:

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\sig{alt}{y[n]} = \at{section-step}{c}\,\bigl(\sig{in}{x[n]} + \sig{alt}{y[n-2]}\bigr) - \sig{in}{x[n-2]}`}
  />

  <M t="x[n]" /> is the sample going into the section and <M t="y[n]" /> the sample coming out, and both reach back two samples. <M t="c" /> is the section's one number. It says where the half turn happens: the nearer <M t="c" /> is to 1, the lower. The numbers that weigh the input, <M t="c" /> and then 1, are the numbers that weigh the output, 1 and then <M t="c" />, in the opposite order. A filter built that way passes every frequency at exactly the level it came in at, and can change only its phase.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/kit/hilbert.h"
    from="struct Section {"
    to="return y; } };"
    name="section"
    at={{ step: 'const float y = flush_denormal(c * (x + y2) - x2);' }}
  />

  The pair is eight of those, four to a chain, each with a constant of its own that is squared to give <M t="c" />, and one sample of delay on the end of the first chain.

  <Listing
    path="cpp/kit/hilbert.h"
    from="void process(float x, float* in_phase, float* quadrature) {"
    to="*quadrature = b; }"
    mark="*in_phase = delayed_;"
    name="pair"
  />

  The sample of delay looks after the top half of the band. A section reaches back two samples, so it cannot tell a frequency from its mirror image about a quarter of the sample rate, and by themselves the two chains would lose their quarter turn there and come out the wrong way round above it. One sample of delay turns each frequency by a share of a turn that grows with the frequency, and that puts it right.

  Here is the distance between the two lines of the picture above, with the axis stretched.

    How far the quadrature output is ahead of the in-phase one, on an axis that shows two degrees
    either side of a quarter turn. Notice that the line ripples evenly about the quarter turn across
    the band, and falls away only at the very bottom.
  </Curve>

  <Check id="quarterError" />

  The quadrature output is a quarter turn ahead, <Claim id="quarter-turn">less than 0.71° out, from 22 Hz to 23,978 Hz</Claim>, which is all that the page's sample rate can hold but a sliver at each end. The ripple is the same height at every crest: the error is spent evenly, and none of the band is better than it needs to be at the cost of another part. Below the band it grows fast: <Claim id="below-band">at 20 Hz the pair is 1.6° from a quarter turn</Claim>.

  Now the pair as sound, read from the running device.

  <Wave taps="in inPhase:alt quadrature:alt2" from={1} span={0.02} fit>
    A short stretch of the input and the pair's two outputs. Try the sine, and notice the outputs
    are one wave at one height, the quadrature output a quarter of a cycle ahead. Then try the saw,
    and notice that neither output looks like a saw.
  </Wave>

  That is the price of the trick. <Claim id="lag-440">At 440 Hz the in-phase output runs a turn and 78.5° behind the input</Claim>, and the lag is different at every frequency, so a wave of many partials comes out with all of them out of step and a new shape. Its levels are untouched: <Claim id="same-level">each of the saw's first twenty harmonics leaves both chains less than 0.001 dB from the level it went in at</Claim>. On the saw, compare by ear <Listen tap="in">the input</Listen> and <Listen tap="quadrature">the quadrature output</Listen>; the button under this stage plays the in-phase one.

  There is a way of seeing the pair that makes the rest short. Take the two outputs as the across and the up of a point on a page. For a sine, the point runs round a circle once a cycle. The pair has turned a wave, which only goes up and down, into a rotation, which has a direction. A frequency is how fast the point goes round. To shift the frequency, turn the point a little faster.

    <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} /> is the carrier's angle in cycles at sample <M t="n" />, kept between 0 and 1: when it passes either end it starts again from the other. <M t="f_c" /> is the shift in force, the line of the first picture, and <M t="f_s" /> is the sample rate.     The device adds the frequency up. It does not work the angle out from the time. So Shift can move without the carrier jumping.     The cosine and the sine of the angle are the point's across and up. The cosine is the same table read a quarter of a cycle further on.

<StageBlock tap="wet" n={3} title="Two products, added or subtracted: one sideband">
  This is the stage the device exists for. Each output of the pair is multiplied by its half of the carrier, and the two products are added or subtracted.

  <Wave taps="productI:alt productQ:alt2 upper:out" from={1} span={0.03} fit>
    A short stretch of the two products and their sum. Try the sine, and notice each product swell
    and shrink while the sum holds one height.
  </Wave>

  Take one partial of the sound. Call its angle as it leaves the in-phase chain <M t="a" />, so that the in-phase output is <M t={String.raw`I = \cos a`} />. The quadrature output is a quarter turn ahead, which makes it <M t={String.raw`Q = -\sin a`} />. Call the carrier's angle <M t="b" />: it is the <M t={String.raw`\varphi`} /> of stage 2, counted as an angle. The first product is

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} \sig{alt}{I\cos b} &= \cos a\,\cos b \\ &= \tfrac12\cos(a-b) + \tfrac12\cos(a+b) \end{aligned}`}
  />

  and the second is

  <M
    block
    t={String.raw`\begin{aligned} \sig{alt2}{Q\sin b} &= -\sin a\,\sin b \\ &= -\tfrac12\cos(a-b) + \tfrac12\cos(a+b) \end{aligned}`}
  />

  Read them against the picture. Each product is a ring modulator's: two waves at half height. The one with <M t="a+b" /> turns faster than the partial did by the carrier's frequency, and is the upper sideband. The one with <M t="a-b" /> turns slower by as much, and is the lower. The upper halves are the same in both lines. The lower halves are equal and opposite. So

  <M block t={String.raw`\sig{out}{I\cos b + Q\sin b} = \cos(a+b)`} />

  <M block t={String.raw`I\cos b - Q\sin b = \cos(a-b)`} />

  The sum is the partial with the carrier's angle added to its own, at full height. The difference is the partial with the carrier's angle taken away. Nothing in those lines asked what frequency the partial had, so they hold for every partial at once, and that is the whole of the shifter.

If the pair is out by an angle <M t={String.raw`\varepsilon`} />, the share of the cancelled sideband that is left, against the one kept, is

  <M block t={String.raw`\tan\frac{\varepsilon}{2}`} />

  So the ripple of stage 1 is the whole story of what leaks. <Claim id="image-worst">At the crests of that ripple the cancelled copy is at least 44.2 dB down</Claim>, which is the least it is anywhere in the band, and <Claim id="image-20">at 20 Hz, below the band, it is only 37 dB down</Claim>.

    Mode is four settings of the direction, one for each side, held in <Src path="cpp/devices/freq-shifter/freq_shifter.h" at="static constexpr float kDirection[4][2] = {" to="{0.0f, 0.0f},    // Ring">a small table</Src>.       caption="The shift in force over the loop. It swings to one side of zero and then to the other, once in the loop."
    />

    With Shift and Fine at nothing, the LFO alone sets the shift, and it goes below zero as far as above. <Claim id="way-sway">With LFO Depth at 0.35 the shift in force sways between −6.1 Hz and 6.1 Hz</Claim>. The notches climb, slow, stop and fall, which is nearer to what a phaser does, with one difference: at the ends of the sway they are moving fastest, where a phaser's would be turning round.

  </Way>
  <Way title="Both sidebands on purpose" tag="Mode on Ring" set="both">
    Ring drops the second product and gives the ring modulator of <Lesson id="tremolo-and-ring-modulation" />, made up in level. On this card the saw is multiplied by a carrier at its own lowest frequency, so every partial's two sidebands land on its two neighbours and the note stays a note. After the card, move Shift a little way off and listen for the two rows of lines drifting apart.
  </Way>
</Ways>

</Part>

<Part id="wrong" title="Seven ways it goes wrong, three of them to hear.">

<Faults>
  <Fault title="A chord comes apart" set="apart">
    <Still
      slot="picture"
      taps="rulerIn rulerOut"
      set="apart"
      view="curve"
      min={-85}
      max={-25}
      caption="Strummed chords on the ruler, going in and coming out. Every line has moved by the same small distance, which is a large interval for the low lines and a small one for the high."
    />

    Intervals are ratios, and a shift keeps differences. <Claim id="fault-apart">Shifted by 30 Hz, the saw's first two partials stand at 140 Hz and 250 Hz: what was an octave has become 10.0 semitones</Claim>. A chord has such intervals between every pair of its notes, and each is bent by a different amount. If the tune must survive, keep the shift to a hertz or two and mix it with the dry sound. If the sound must move by a musical interval, the tool is the one of <Lesson id="pitch-shifting" />.

  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The bottom goes" set="thin">
    Everything moves up, and nothing moves in underneath. <Claim id="fault-thin">Shifted up 100 Hz, the bass's strongest low line moves from 55 Hz to 155 Hz, and the loop holds 26 dB less below 100 Hz than went in, while its level over the whole band is less than 0.1 dB from what it was</Claim>. So a meter will not show the loss. Keep low instruments out of a large shift, or lower Mix so that the dry sound keeps its low end.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Down through zero" set="through">
    Shift a partial down by more than its own frequency and the answer is a frequency below zero. A real signal cannot tell that from the same frequency above zero, so the partial comes back up. <Claim id="fault-through">Shifted down 300 Hz, the saw's partial at 110 Hz comes out at 190 Hz and the one at 220 Hz at 80 Hz, each at its own level</Claim>: the lower partial is now the higher. <Claim id="through-zero">A 440 Hz sine shifted down by 500 Hz comes out at 60 Hz</Claim>. Low partials fold back while high ones are still coming down, and the two pass each other. Keep a downward shift smaller than the lowest partial that matters.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="The other sideband does not quite go">
    The cancelling is exactly as good as the quarter turn. Stage 3 of part 5 has the numbers: how far under the kept sideband the cancelled one is for a test tone, at the crests of the pair's ripple, and below the band, where it is worst. Whether that can be heard under a given sound is for your ear. The place to listen is a single pure tone with a large shift, for a faint second tone on the other side of where the tone stood.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="Past the top">
    The same folding happens at the other end, and nothing in the device guards against it. <Claim id="past-top">A sine at 23.6 kHz shifted up by 1 kHz folds back to 23.4 kHz</Claim>: the place it was sent is past the top of what the page's sample rate can hold. Shift stops at <Src path="cpp/devices/freq-shifter/device.json" at={'"max": 1000,'}>1 kHz</Src>, so one pass can fold only the very top of the band. Feedback carries a partial further up on every pass, and Tone, which darkens each pass, is what keeps that in hand.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="A loop that builds">
    Going once round the feedback loop, a single sideband comes back no larger than it left, so with Feedback under 1 the loop dies away. Ring at a shift of zero is the exception. Both sidebands land on the partial they came from, the make-up gain goes round with them, and each pass can come back larger than the last. The limiter is what stops it. <Claim id="ring-loop">In Ring, with Shift at 0 and Feedback at 0.98, a sine that peaks at a quarter of full scale builds until what comes back is held at 1.0</Claim>, and <Claim id="ring-loop-out">the output stands at 2.4</Claim> full scales. Lower Feedback, or move the shift off zero.
  </Fault>
  <Fault title="What silence does">
    Fed silence, the device puts out <Claim id="silence">exact silence</Claim>. After <Src path="cpp/devices/freq-shifter/freq_shifter.h" at="idle_.reset(sr, kParamMax[kDelay] * 0.001f + 0.2f);">its longest delay and a fifth of a second more</Src> of it, the device rests: the carrier and the LFO stop where they are until sound returns. So a slow drift does not carry on turning through a long gap, and what follows the gap starts from where the carrier stopped.
  </Fault>
</Faults>

</Part>

<Part id="myths" title="Five things that are often said, and what is true.">

<Myths>
  <Myth says="A frequency shifter is a kind of pitch shifter.">
    A change of pitch multiplies every frequency by one number, and a shift adds one number to every frequency. The two agree on one partial at most. The ruler of part 2 shows both at once: the lines of the faster copy fan out from zero, and the shifted lines slide. Only the first keeps a note a note.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="With the shift at zero the device does nothing.">
    The carrier then stands still, with its cosine at 1 and its sine at 0, and what comes out is the in-phase output of the pair. <Claim id="zero-shift">At a shift of 0 Hz the output and the in-phase output differ by at least 140 dB under either</Claim>. <Claim id="zero-level">A 1 kHz sine comes out less than 0.001 dB from the level it went in at</Claim>, and <Claim id="lag-1k">524° behind it</Claim>. A steady tone only starts its cycle somewhere else. A click changes shape: <Claim id="click-smear">a single sample of height 1 comes out as a train of samples, the largest of them 0.79 of the click's height, three samples late</Claim> and upside down, carrying <Claim id="click-energy">the same energy to one part in a million</Claim>. This is also why mixing the dry sound with an unshifted wet one makes the fixed notches of stage 5.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="The unwanted sideband is removed.">
    It is cancelled, by adding it to a copy of itself turned upside down, and the cancelling is only as exact as the pair's quarter turn. Step 4 of the build measured what was left on a sine, and stage 3 of part 5 found where in the band it is least far down.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="A shift of a hertz is too small to hear.">
    By itself it nearly is. Mixed with the dry sound it is a swell and a silence once a second, at every frequency in turn. Stage 5 of part 5 measured both the swell and the depth of the silence, and the picture there shows the bands they make.
  </Myth>
  <Myth says="To move frequencies about, a device must take the sound apart into a spectrum first.">
    This one holds no spectrum at any time. It works a sample at a time: eight all-pass sections, two readings of a table, two multiplications and a sum, as the listings of stages 1 and 3 show. The ruler and the spectra on this page are the lab's, for looking. A device that does work on the spectrum itself is the subject of <Lesson id="spectral-processing" />.
  </Myth>
</Myths>

</Part>
